## 3. `Paper Empires` - 2025 CV project entry

**Source message:** `197730714010c0c7`

**Title:** *Paper Empires: Material Infrastructures and Brian Hodgson’s Epistemic Politics of Colonial Natural History*

**Archive list in the CV:** Kew Garden Archive, Natural History Museum, Royal Asiatic Society, British Library.

**Recovered project text:**

> Brian Hodgson is a significant but often overlooked pioneer in early Buddhist & Himalayan Studies. This project reframes material supports as more than neutral objects by emphasizing specific types of paper, watermarks, and “paper techniques” recently stated in Linnaean studies. It treats these materials as dynamic inscription devices in the sense of Latour. Hodgson carefully distinguishes between British machine-made woven papers (often featuring Whatman or Towgood watermarks), Nepali handmade lokta papers, machine-produced papers from Calcutta, and administrative “blue papers.” Each type embodies a distinct sense of authenticity, bureaucratic authority, or localized knowledge. Thus, they materially shape the circulation and legitimation of natural-historical claims.

**Current editorial status:** retain the material and natural-historical argument; compress the opening characterization in terms of Buddhist/Himalayan Studies.

## 4. BASAS abstract - superseded full framing

**Source:** `BASAS.docx` in Gmail message `19e5407d6912a25c`.

**Title:** *The Self as Archive: Brian Houghton Hodgson and the Epistemic Politics of Buddhist Knowledge*

> Brian Houghton Hodgson (1800–1894), a British colonial administrator in Nepal, is historically recognized as a marginal contributor to early European Buddhist studies. He was primarily regarded as a provider of textual and visual materials to metropolitan Orientalists such as Eugène Burnouf (1801–1852). However, this impression may oversimplify the complexity of his intellectual life.
>
> This paper analyzes Hodgson's scholarly persona, marked by discontinuity, hesitancy, and recursive self-interruption, rather than depicting him as a supplemental biographical figure. This study begins not with his published works but with the archival margins, particularly the numerous annotations he inscribed in his final years on his earlier Buddhist writings. These annotations reveal a significant epistemic transformation: from classifying Buddhism for imperial frameworks to deconstructing the system he once built. In this context, Hodgson can be viewed anachronistically as “self-postcolonial”—not in terms of ideology, but through the fragmented structures evident in his own inscriptions.
>
> This study is divided into two parts. The first examines Hodgson's early classificatory methodology, shaped by institutions like the Linnean Society and Kew Gardens. In this context, diagrams, tables, and lists fragmented Buddhism into modules into imperial networks through various “paper techniques.” The second part revisits these same writings, now annotated with marginalia that challenge the systems they once reinforced. These annotations represent not only supplementary additions but also epistemic devices of deconstruction and strategic self-inscription, mirroring marginalization within scientific institutions.
>
> In conclusion, Hodgson's later works represent Buddhism as both a subject of critique and a methodology that reveals colonial knowledge's limitations.

**Current editorial status:** do not use this as the article's argumentative sequence. Mine it for (a) late annotation, (b) diagrams/tables/lists, (c) Linnean/Kew institutional context, (d) paper techniques, and (e) strategic self-inscription / revision. The “self-postcolonial” and Buddhist-hermeneutic claims are parked.
