# Web research — 2026-09-05 — Nepal paper deep dive

Status: active research log. This file records only findings that materially change the case structure, evidence hierarchy, or next research priority. It is deliberately narrower than a general Hodgson/Nepal archive survey.

## Scope and method reset

The active article is being rebuilt as a Hodgson-centred, case-driven HPS history of attempts to make Nepal paper travel, be remade, and remain useful outside its original production setting.

Current working first-order questions:

- Could Nepal paper be made elsewhere?
- What had to travel for it to be remade elsewhere: finished paper, fibre/pulp, written description, skilled makers, or some combination?
- Who decided whether the resulting paper counted as good paper, and by what operation?

Research guardrails for this wave:

- keep papermakers, intermediaries, assistants, writers, carriers and other labour visible;
- prioritize English-language administrative, scientific, commercial and printed records; do not restrict the search geographically to India;
- do not reopen Royal Asiatic Society physical archival research in this phase;
- avoid archive over-density: open an archival line only when it closes a concrete event chain or actor-level gap;
- use newspapers, technical journals and official print aggressively for event closure and afterlife;
- do not treat Serampore machine incompatibility as the end of the history unless later evidence proves it was.

## Finding 1 — pre-Hodgson public paper specimen, 1818

Evidence status: **STRONG LEAD / specific catalogue-level witness; original newspaper still desirable.**

The Royal Asiatic Society catalogue describes newspaper cuttings reporting the Asiatic Society meeting of 11 February 1818. The meeting included Nathaniel Wallich's botanical communications and a paper specimen described as paper made from the bark of the paper shrub.

Public catalogue lead:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/

Consequence:

The article should not begin by implying that Hodgson discovered Nepal paper in 1831. Nepal paper had already entered the public scientific world of Calcutta before Hodgson's paper-manufacture correspondence.

Open task:

- locate the original 1818 newspaper report or another contemporary printed witness before using the exact meeting detail as a body-level claim.

## Finding 2 — Wallich's 1820 account already included papermaking procedure

Evidence status: **SECURE for publication; process/source attribution still being calibrated.**

Nathaniel Wallich's contribution in *Asiatick Researches*, vol. 13 (1820), includes the paper-producing Daphne and a short account of the manufacture. Later technical literature reproduces a sequence including removal of the outer bark, boiling the inner bark with ash, washing, beating on stone with mallets, mixing pulp and forming sheets on bamboo-based frames/mats.

Primary volume control:
https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/
Search: Nathaniel Wallich, *Asiatick Researches* 13 (1820), pp. 369–415.

A later specialist history by Frederik Trier attributes the practical description used by Wallich not to Wallich's direct observation in Nepal but to Lt. M. R. Murray, who had travelled in Kumaon. This attribution is currently **SECONDARY-CONTROLLED** and must be checked against Wallich's own wording before being promoted.

Consequence:

Hodgson 1831/32 cannot be presented as the first written recipe for Nepal papermaking. The P0 comparison is now:

`Wallich/Murray 1820 account -> Hodgson 1831/32 account`

Questions:

- What procedural detail did Hodgson add or alter?
- Did Hodgson have a different source base, especially direct Nepal workshop/maker knowledge?
- Did Hodgson convert an earlier descriptive recipe into a more operational account tied to specimens, government records, procurement and remanufacture?

## Finding 3 — bank-note/security-paper problem predates the Cowan trial

Evidence status: **STRONG / direct published correspondence; causal link to Cowan still OPEN.**

William Jack's correspondence with Wallich records an earlier proposal to make bank-note forgery more difficult by using paper made from a foreign and difficult-to-procure material with distinctive qualities. Jack proposed Nepal paper and referred to Wallich's account and paper specimens being transmitted through the Raffles network.

Published correspondence witness:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3e/Journal_of_the_Straits_Branch_of_the_Royal_Asiatic_Society_%28IA_journalofstra72741916roya%29.pdf

Consequence:

Cowan's later report that Nepal-made sheets took a bank-note impression with "perfect fidelity" may sit inside an older security-paper problem genealogy rather than being an arbitrary quality test.

Do not yet write a direct causal arrow from this correspondence to Cowan without a transmission link.

Open tasks:

- identify exact date, sender and recipient of the bank-note passage;
- recover/verify the related Raffles/Cartwright/Wallich wording about sending finished paper and material for making it to Britain / the Court of Directors.

## Finding 5 — filtration evidence requires object-state distinction

Evidence status: **STRONG published technical witness; author/date verification still being completed.**

*The Bengal Dispensatory* distinguishes papers used for filtration. For delicate experiments it recommends white unsized Serampore paper, but where the deposit is bulky it states that paper made in Nepal is the best material because of its strength and its ability to permit rapid filtration followed by pressure/expression.

Public scan/search lead:
https://archive.org/
Search title: *The Bengal Dispensatory*.

Consequence:

The article must not generalize from one Campbell/O'Shaughnessy experimental filtering failure to "Nepal paper was a poor filter." It must distinguish at minimum:

- Nepal-made finished paper;
- Campbell/Darjeeling experimental paper;
- bleached/reprocessed experimental states;
- Serampore machine-made paper.

This is a direct case-level warning against treating "Nepal paper" as a single stable technical object.

Open task:

- verify edition, author, date and exact page wording.

## Finding 6 — 1854 Darjeeling report gives useful negative evidence

Evidence status: **CONTROLLED NEGATIVE / absence, not proof of cessation.**

W. B. Jackson's 1854 *Report on Darjeeling* surveys Campbell's development work and names roads, tea/coffee and other improvements, but the searchable full text does not identify the Nepal-paper manufacturing experiment as one of the continuing achievements. Searches for "paper" in the text produce only incidental usages unrelated to papermaking.

Internet Archive:
https://archive.org/details/dli.pahar.0446

Consequence:

The later gazetteer claim that Campbell's Darjeeling factory supplied writing and packing paper "for some years" should remain **P1 RETROSPECTIVE** until one contemporaneous witness confirms continued production or supply.

The Jackson absence does not prove the factory had ended, but it weakens any assumption that papermaking remained a major Darjeeling development project by 1854.

## Active case architecture after current findings

### Case 1 — before and through Hodgson/Cowan: material and description travel

Short antecedent:

`1818 public paper specimen / Wallich botanical network`
-> `1820 Wallich/Murray technical description + bank-note/security-paper discussion`
-> `1831–33 Hodgson specimens/account + Cowan remanufacture`.

Historical question:

What did Hodgson add to an already circulating knowledge of Nepal paper, and why did transported material plus written knowledge still fail to reproduce the Nepal-made sheet in Britain?

### Case 2 — 1841: makers travel

This remains the article's centre.

Required actor-level reconstruction:

- five papermakers: head man, mate/first assistant, three other workers;
- wages/status/background where source-controlled;
- their preferred site and Campbell's decision to relocate/observe them;
- bark, drying, ash, water, vessel metal, beating and sheet-forming judgments;
- two Lepcha trainees;
- which makers, if any, were retained and for how long.

Historical question:

Did moving the makers solve what moving finished paper, pulp/material and written procedure had failed to solve?

### Case 3 — 1841 onward: what happened to the paper they made?

Do not organize this as an abstract sequence of "interfaces." Follow the paper into concrete uses and judgments:

- whitening/bleaching;
- filtering;
- waterproof wrapping;
- Serampore machine manufacture;
- possible continued Darjeeling production/supply;
- later field-science use and procurement.

Historical question:

Who decided whether the paper was successful, for which use, and what became of the manufacture after the 1841–42 experiments?
