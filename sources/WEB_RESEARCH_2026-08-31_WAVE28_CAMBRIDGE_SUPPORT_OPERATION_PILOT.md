# Web research 2026-08-31 — Wave 28
## Cambridge Hodgson inventory: support × operation pilot

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: paper-centred pilot census of the Cambridge online inventory of MSS EUR HODGSON. This wave tests whether paper support correlates with epistemic/documentary operation rather than only subject matter.

## Executive result

A first cross-volume sample shows a strong but non-exclusive workflow pattern.

### Pattern A — Nepali hand-made paper frequently carries local-language source/copy layers

Examples include:

- vol.57 items 1–8: Newar, Khasa, Magar, Gurung, Tamang, Limbu, Kirati and Lepcha customs/manners manuscripts, all on Nepali hand-made paper;
- vol.26/21: Sanskrit-Newari comparative vocabulary on Nepali hand-made paper;
- vol.26/22: Amritananda payment receipt on Nepali hand-made paper;
- vol.26/23: Tri-amnaya description with illustrative drawings on Nepali hand-made paper;
- vol.60/9–16: caste lists, festival officials, funeral rites, royal orders, shop lists and copied regulations, overwhelmingly on Nepali hand-made paper;
- vol.55/20: originals and copies of royal orders/official notes mostly on Nepali hand-made paper, with some machine-made paper.

### Pattern B — machine-made paper frequently carries Hodgson/assistant analytical, English, tabular or forwarding layers

Examples include:

- vol.3/2–4: Hodgson/Campbell sketch maps and route working papers on machine-made paper;
- vol.7/10: revenue tables and calculations with corrections on machine-made paper;
- vol.9/12–14: Tibet Q&A, Meche notes and comparative Himalayan vocabulary on machine-made paper;
- vol.20/1–8: English Buddhist comparison, notes and working notebooks on machine-made paper;
- vol.29/5: Hodgson's list of Buddhist objects shipped to Calcutta in 1828 on machine-made paper;
- vol.58/9: standardized district research questionnaire on machine-made paper;
- vol.103/8: salaries/revenue analytical material on machine-made paper.

### Pattern C — translation, excerpting and composite dossiers often generate mixed support

Examples include:

- vol.29/4: Buddhist names from Lalitavistara on machine-made and Nepali hand-made paper, with English and Farsi transcriptions;
- vol.60/7: law/courts/crime dossier on Nepali hand-made and machine-made paper, multiple hands;
- vol.73/3: Tibetan Buddhism/language on Nepali hand-made and machine-made paper;
- vol.95/6: routes to China and trade commodities on mixed Nepali hand-made and machine-made paper;
- vol.12/1: large legal dossier on different kinds of machine-made paper with one Nepali hand-made sheet.

The pilot hypothesis is therefore:

`support choice correlates with documentary operation and actor/language layer, not simply research subject`.

More specifically:

`local source/copy production -> often Nepali hand-made`

`Hodgson/assistant analytical working, tables, questionnaires, route sketches, forwarding lists -> often machine-made`

`translation/recompilation/long-lived dossier -> often mixed support`.

This is a hypothesis to test quantitatively, not yet a universal rule.

Mechanisms:

- `SUPPORT_OPERATION_CORRELATION`
- `LOCAL_SOURCE_LAYER_ON_COUNTRY_PAPER`
- `ANALYTICAL_WORKING_LAYER_ON_MACHINE_PAPER`
- `MIXED_SUPPORT_AS_VERSIONED_DOSSIER`
- `PAPER_ALLOCATION_AS_RESEARCH_INFRASTRUCTURE`

## Key evidence cases

### MSS EUR HODGSON/3 — routes and sketch maps

Cambridge inventory:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol003.html

- item 2: sketch maps of Nuwakot and Palpa, 1830s — machine-made paper, rough pencil sketch by Hodgson;
- item 3: routes from/to Kathmandu — machine-made paper, Hodgson's rough writing;
- item 4: route northwest from Residency to Timure — machine-made paper, Campbell fair writing with Hodgson pencil corrections.

This is unusually clean evidence for machine-made paper as a working support for route abstraction, sketching and correction.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/7 — tabulation and comparison

https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol007.html

- item 8: list of Nepali/Bhotiya cloth samples — machine-made paper;
- item 10: provincial revenue and salary calculations — machine-made paper, multiple hands, extensive corrections/addenda;
- item 17: Newar matrimonial relations/festivals — Nepali hand-made paper despite English text;
- item 18: brief comparative Newar caste note — machine-made paper.

This volume cautions against reducing paper choice to language alone. Operation and document state matter.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/9 — analytical ethnology/linguistics

https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol009.html

- item 12: Q&A on Tibetans — machine-made;
- item 13: Meche note, including duplicate copy — machine-made;
- item 14: comparative vocabulary in tabular form — machine-made, excellent condition/writing;
- items 15, 18, 20: rough military/revenue working notes — machine-made.

Machine-made paper here repeatedly supports English analytical and comparative working documents.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/20 — English Buddhist working notebooks

https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol020.html

Items 1–8 are overwhelmingly English rough analytical notebooks on machine-made paper: Tibetan/Newar Buddhism comparison, Svayambhupurana sketches, Buddhist philosophy, festival almanac, antiquities, deity lists and chronology.

This is an important counterpoint to Sanskrit/Newari Buddhist source manuscripts on Nepali hand-made paper in volumes 26–29.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/26–29 — source layer vs analytical/list layer

https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol026.html
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol027.html
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol029.html

The Buddhist material is especially revealing because the **subject remains Buddhism while support changes with operation**.

Nepali hand-made examples:

- Sanskrit texts and excerpts;
- Sanskrit-Newari vocabulary;
- Amritananda receipts;
- iconographic descriptions with drawings;
- amnaya manuscripts.

Machine-made examples:

- short classificatory lists of Buddhist texts/deities;
- Hodgson's 1828 forwarding list of Buddhist objects sent to Calcutta.

Mixed example:

- Lalitavistara Buddha-name dossier with English and Farsi transcription layers.

Thus topic cannot explain the distribution by itself.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/57 — ethnographic source corpus

https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol057.html

All eight principal ethnic customs/manners items are catalogued on Nepali hand-made paper. This is the cleanest pilot cluster for `LOCAL_SOURCE_LAYER_ON_COUNTRY_PAPER`.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/58 — questionnaire vs local account

https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol058.html

- item 8: local monastery customs — Nepali hand-made paper;
- item 9: standardized comprehensive research questionnaire prepared by Hodgson and assistants — machine-made paper.

This pair is analytically strong because it juxtaposes a local descriptive source and a research instrument.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/60 — mixed legal dossier and local documentary materials

https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol060.html

- item 7: law/justice dossier — Nepali hand-made and machine-made paper, multiple hands;
- items 9–16: caste lists, funeral rites, royal orders, shops and regulations — Nepali hand-made paper;
- item 6: brief Shakyamuni story — machine-made paper.

The large mixed legal dossier is a likely example of aggregation across documentary stages rather than deliberate use of one paper type.

### MSS EUR HODGSON/73, /95, /103–104 — mixed language and route cases

- vol.73/3 Tibetan Buddhism/language: Nepali hand-made and machine-made paper;
- vol.95/6 China routes and trade commodities: mixed support;
- vol.95/5 and /7 short topographical/place-distance lists: machine-made;
- vol.103/8 salaries/revenue: machine-made;
- vol.103/9 route to Timure: Nepali hand-made;
- vol.104/25 route Kathmandu–Sangabhanjyang: machine-made;
- vol.104/27 route Kathmandu–Makawanpur/Hariharpur: Nepali hand-made.

These cases show that even within `route` as a topic, support varies. The next census must distinguish narrated/local route report, translated route, Hodgson abstract, map/sketch, table/list and fair/rough copy state.

## Revised variables for the full census

Per inventory item record:

- volume/item/folios;
- date;
- subject domain;
- operation class;
- document state: original/source, copy, translation, abstract, questionnaire, table/list, sketch/map, receipt, forwarding manifest, correspondence, notebook;
- principal language;
- secondary language/transcription layer;
- named/attributed actor;
- support: Nepali hand-made / machine-made / mixed / printed / other / unspecified;
- paper quality/condition descriptors;
- writing state: rough/fair/neat/poor;
- pen/pencil/wash or other medium;
- corrections/addenda;
- binding/notebook state;
- custody/container context;
- confidence.

### Critical methodological rule

Do not code `machine-made = European/British` automatically from the inventory wording. The Cambridge catalogue identifies formation class, not maker or production origin.

Likewise, do not code `Nepali hand-made paper = made in Kathmandu`; Hodgson's own paper history warns that `Nepali/Nipalese` can be a market/regional label broader than manufacture place.

## Pilot hypotheses to falsify

### H1

Local-language source/copy layers are more likely to occur on Nepali hand-made paper than Hodgson's English analytical working layers.

### H2

Machine-made paper is disproportionately associated with tables, questionnaires, sketch maps, short lists, English notes and forwarding/administrative abstraction.

### H3

Mixed-support items are disproportionately composite or versioned dossiers assembled across different moments, actors or translation stages.

### H4

Paper support correlates more strongly with **operation/document state** than with subject domain.

### H5

Within a single subject such as Buddhism, ethnology, law or routes, switching from source text to analytical/translation/list layer often coincides with a support switch.

These require a full item-level census before statistical language is used in the article.

## Bottom line

The Cambridge inventory begins to make paper allocation empirically testable. The strongest pilot signal is not that one subject belongs to one paper. It is that documentary layers often differ materially: locally produced source/copy manuscripts frequently sit on Nepali hand-made paper, while Hodgson's analytical notebooks, route sketches, tables, questionnaires and forwarding lists frequently sit on machine-made paper; translation and accumulated dossiers commonly cross both. If a full census confirms this, paper choice becomes part of the architecture by which Himalayan testimony was converted into comparable, portable and publishable knowledge.
