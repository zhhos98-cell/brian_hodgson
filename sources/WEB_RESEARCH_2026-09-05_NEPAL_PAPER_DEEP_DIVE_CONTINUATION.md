## Finding 9 — Hodgson's 1831/32 intervention was not botanical discovery or the first recipe; it made an older process description more operational and transport-oriented

Evidence status: **SECURE for the textual comparison; Hodgson's own local maker/informant source remains OPEN.**

### Murray/Wallich before Hodgson

A near-contemporary 1832 synthesis explicitly states that the papermaking particulars published by Nathaniel Wallich were extracted from the manuscripts of **Lieutenant H. R. Murray**, forming part of Murray's official correspondence with the Military Board at Calcutta. Murray's observations concern Kumaon and already include a compact process chain:

- scrape away the outer bark;
- boil inner bark in clean water with a small quantity of oak ashes;
- wash;
- beat with small mallets on stone;
- mix in clean water to a pulp/slurry;
- spread on bamboo-mat moulds/frames.

The same account already describes grades/sizes/prices and practical uses including cartridges, genealogical records and deeds.

Controlled public witness:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Indian%20Subcontinent/1832%20Historical%20and%20Descriptive%20Account%20of%20British%20India%20Vol%203%20by%20Murray%20s.pdf

### Hodgson's self-positioning

Hodgson's 1832 article explicitly says that **Dr Wallich had already fully described the paper plant**, so Hodgson deliberately turns to the **method of manufacture** rather than claiming botanical novelty.

### What Hodgson adds relative to the Murray/Wallich description

Hodgson turns the concise process into a much more operational specification. He gives:

- an explicit seven-part implement list;
- an ash-liquor recipe/strength test and approximate quantities;
- boiling time;
- dimensions/state of bark strips;
- post-beating churning until stringiness disappears;
- coarse screening to remove knotty/gross material;
- a floating cloth-bottom frame and manual redistribution of pulp;
- intentional thickness/strength control by varying the amount of pulp laid on the frame;
- fire drying;
- peeling/folding;
- optional polishing with shell or hardwood;
- a statement that ordinary manufacture does not require sizing to stop ink running;
- large-sheet capability;
- Kathmandu prices and commodity forms;
- most importantly, **dried pulp made into brick/tile form for transport**, explicitly presented as convenient for transmission to England;
- a postscript reporting that raw pulp had already been sent to England and judged suitable for proof-engraving paper.

### Consequence for Case 1

Hodgson's historical importance should not be stated as:

- discovery of Nepal paper;
- first description of the plant;
- first written recipe;
- first imagination that its material might be transported to Britain.

A stronger and more precise formulation is available:

**Hodgson reformatted already-circulating Himalayan papermaking knowledge into a more executable, measurable and transport-oriented technical account.**

This makes the Cowan remanufacture episode analytically stronger. Cowan's failure can be read as a historical test of whether:

`more explicit procedure + transported material`

was sufficient to reproduce a successful Nepal-made sheet elsewhere.

Do not elevate this into an abstract 'codification' thesis in the introduction. Let the comparison emerge through the two texts and the later maker correction at Darjeeling.

### P0 gap retained

The article still does not know **from whom Hodgson learned the Nepal-specific procedure in 1831**. That actor-level source is now more important, not less, because Wallich/Murray supplies a clear earlier comparison point.

## Finding 10 — Darjeeling-made paper had an imperial exhibition afterlife by 1851

Evidence status: **STRONG catalogue-level evidence; direct PDF page image could not be opened in the current web viewer because the scan is too large. Treat the exact catalogue wording as indexed-text evidence until page-image inspection is possible.**

A searchable copy of the 1851 Great Exhibition catalogue, India section, Class 9 / `Papers, &c.`, lists among other Indian paper exhibits:

- paper made from young bamboo in Assam;
- paper made with the bark of *Daphne cannabina*;
- **`Paper made in Nepal and at Darjeeling`**;
- paper from Cashmere;
- papers exhibited by Dr Royle;
- papers from jute and other fibres.

Search-indexed catalogue witness:
https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/22079393.pdf

The web index identifies the relevant catalogue page as 152. The large PDF itself currently fails direct rendering in the available viewer, so no stronger page-image claim should be made until a local or alternative scan is inspected.

### Consequence

This gives Darjeeling-made paper a clear post-1842 material life as an **exhibition/comparative object** within the East India Company's presentation of Indian products at the Great Exhibition.

It does **not** prove that Campbell's Darjeeling manufactory operated continuously from 1841 to 1851. The exhibited paper might have been surviving experimental production or later production. Keep these claims separate:

- `Darjeeling-made paper represented at Great Exhibition 1851` = STRONG;
- `Darjeeling factory continuously supplied paper for years` = still P1 retrospective / unconfirmed contemporaneously.

This also helps prevent Serampore 1842 machine incompatibility from becoming a teleological endpoint. The material continued to circulate as a product/specimen category even when mechanized general-paper manufacture looked unattractive.
