# Public-web research wave 6 — distributed objects, paper pulp transport, proof engraving, and redundancy as error control

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. No private/archive scans introduced. This wave follows the implications of the post-1844 zoological distribution papers, the 1832 Nepal-paper article, and the Paris manuscript-copying programme.

## 1. Zoological identity was intentionally distributed across institutions

A 1845 Hodgson letter to the Royal College of Surgeons is unusually explicit. He offered osteological remains of Nepalese and Tibetan animals and explained that most were **numbered with reference to the skins and drawings he had presented to the British Museum**. The numbering would allow the species represented by disarticulated skeletal material to be identified by reference to objects held elsewhere.

Hodgson also asked that whoever examined the bones should communicate the results back to him, especially conclusions bearing on natural affinities and habits.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=12410&levels=237&page=3&places=53595&sf_culture=nl&showAdvanced=1&sort=lastUpdated&sortDir=asc&topLod=0

Analytic significance:

> A Hodgson “species object” could be physically distributed as skeleton + skin + drawing across separate institutions, with **numbers** functioning as the infrastructure that made those fragments re-composable.

The proposed donation was therefore also a feedback loop:

`collection in Nepal → distributed objects in London → comparative/anatomical examination → results returned to Hodgson`.

This is stronger than a generic story of circulation.

## 2. Museum space and packing cost affected where scientific objects went

Richard Owen, on behalf of the Royal College of Surgeons, initially welcomed Hodgson's osteological collection and stated that the College would pay the **expense of packaging and transmission**.

Hodgson then reconsidered. After discussing the Hunterian Museum's limited space and its selective display purpose, he decided that his large osteological collection was not well suited to the College and should instead go first to the British Museum.

Sources:

https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=8774&places=61164&showAdvanced=1&sort=startDate&sortDir=asc&topLod=0

This is a concrete case in which **institutional space + transport cost + collection scale** helped determine custody.

## 3. “Original and authentic types” is Hodgson-network natural-history language

H. E. Strickland thanked Hodgson in May 1845 for a donation of 205 bird specimens. He described them as **original and authentic types** of the many novelties Hodgson had already described in print.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?names=50487&places=53595&sort=alphabetic&sortDir=desc&topLod=0&view=table

This matters for the manuscript comparison. `Original` and `authentic` were not concepts confined to textual criticism. In the natural-history network, preserved bodies could serve as authoritative material referents for published names.

A useful comparison is therefore:

- manuscript witness: `original / copy / old / incomplete / correct / incorrect`;
- zoological witness: `original / authentic type / numbered specimen / duplicate / new / old species`.

The comparison should be historical and lexical, not merely metaphorical.

## 4. The British Museum retained material for inquiry rather than display

In March 1845 the British Museum told Hodgson it would retain a complete series of osteological specimens. It did **not** intend to articulate or exhibit them but to arrange and preserve them for scientific enquiries.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?mediatypes=137&names=8774&onlyMedia=1&page=9&sf_culture=es&showAdvanced=1&sort=referenceCode&sortDir=desc&topLod=0&view=table

Preservation here is explicitly epistemic storage: the object remains useful because it can be revisited by later inquiry.

## 5. Hodgson's zoological archive suffered a custody/property failure, not just a shipping failure

By February–March 1845 Hodgson was trying to recover drawings dispersed among individuals and institutions. He reported that he had recovered **65 drawings** and delivered them to the British Museum. Other drawings were expected from the Royal Asiatic Society, Zoological Society, Sir Alexander Johnston, H. H. Wilson, and Mr Howard.

Howard apparently held a complete set of mammal drawings and resisted returning them. The British Museum's solicitors investigated after a legal distress/receivership event and concluded that Hodgson or the Museum might have **lost legal claim to the drawings**.

Sources:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=50487&sf_culture=nl&showAdvanced=1&sort=endDate&sortDir=desc&topLod=0

https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?onlyMedia=1&places=53692&sf_culture=en&showAdvanced=1&sort=lastUpdated&sortDir=asc&topLod=0&view=table

The same correspondence contains Hodgson's pencilled complaint that the Museum's **distribution table was full of omissions and inaccuracies**, and his demand for a corrected copy, which he was willing to pay to have copied.

This gives a rare compound failure:

`object dispersion → disputed custody/property → incomplete recovery → inaccurate distribution metadata → corrective copying`.

## 6. Distribution lists were not retrospective summaries; they were operating instruments

The ZSL archive preserves a July 1845 `Memo of zoological specimens and drawings presented to various institutions`, giving numbers of bones, skins and drawings assigned to the British Museum, India House, Leiden, Paris, Berlin, Frankfurt, Edinburgh, Dublin, Newcastle, Manchester, Canterbury, Earl of Derby, Strickland, College of Surgeons, Haslar, etc.

## 7. The 1832 JASB issue itself joins Tibetan textual analysis and Nepalese papermaking

The very first number of the *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* (January 1832) is strikingly ordered:

1. H. H. Wilson, abstracting Alexander Csoma de Kőrös's analysis of the **Dul-va / first part of the Káh-gyur**;
2. Brian Houghton Hodgson, **On the Native Method of making the Paper, denominated in Hindustan, Nipalese**;
3. natural-history and other scientific articles on snails, minerals, route calculation, rivers, meteorology, etc.

Contents:
https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Journal_of_the_Asiatic_Society_of_Bengal_Vol_1.djvu/15

This adjacency should not be overstated as a causal relation, but it is historiographically useful: Tibetan canon analysis, papermaking, natural history and measurement entered the new journal as parts of the same knowledge economy rather than as already segregated modern disciplines.

## 8. Wilson's Káh-gyur analysis frames textual study as return to original authority

Wilson's 1832 `Analysis of the Kah-gyur`, based on Csoma's work on the Society's copy, says that careful examination of the original Prajñāpāramitā (or a translation of an abridgement) was necessary to determine Buddhist doctrine and to escape the problems of inferior authorities and oral communication.

Source:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Tibet%20and%20China/1832%20Analysis%20of%20Kah-gyur%20by%20Wilson%20from%20JASBv1%20s.pdf

This gives an early `original authority` problem that later Burnouf solves differently through comparative redundancy: not merely one original, but several material witnesses.

## 9. Hodgson's own paper article directly links manufacture, transport, engraving, office records and manuscript dating

Primary source:
Brian H. Hodgson, `On the Native Method of making the Paper, denominated in Hindustan, Nipalese`, JASB 1 (1832), pp. 8–11.

Public scan/transcription:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1832%20Native%20Method%20of%20Making%20Paper%20in%20Hindustan%20Nipalese%20by%20Hodgson%20from%20JASBv1%20s.pdf

Several points are central.

### 9.1 Paper thickness was controllable at manufacture

Hodgson says the amount of pulp laid on the frame could be varied according to the **strength of paper required**. Material properties were therefore deliberately adjustable, not merely inherent in a named paper type.

### 9.2 Pulp itself was made transportable

Raw pulp was dried into **bricks or tiles for convenience of transport**. Hodgson explicitly says this form was admirably adapted for transmission to England.

Thus transportability was designed into the semi-manufactured material before it became a sheet.

### 9.3 Nepalese paper/pulp entered print-image technology

Hodgson says pulp sent to England had been judged by experts to be exceptionally suitable for manufacturing paper used for **proof engravings**.

This potentially connects Nepalese paper not merely to writing but to the production/testing of printed scientific images. It requires further research to identify who tested the pulp and whether the material was actually adopted for engraving papers.

### 9.4 Nepal paper was proposed as archival office infrastructure

Hodgson praises the manufactured paper for office records as nearly as strong and durable as leather while sufficiently smooth for writing. He says it had already been adopted in one or two offices in the plains and argues that it should replace friable Indian record paper.

Paper manufacture was therefore simultaneously an ethnographic/technical subject and an intervention in bureaucratic preservation.

### 9.5 Writing support was already being used as a dating heuristic

Hodgson reports a Nepalese claim that surviving books written on **Palmyra leaves** could, on that material fact alone, be pronounced approximately 500 years old; he then uses this to speculate about the chronology of paper manufacture.

This is highly relevant to authentication/dating. Even in the papermaking article, **support functions as chronological evidence**.

Treat Hodgson's 500-year inference as a historical actor's dating claim, not as a modern codicological fact.

## 10. Paper manufacture was also a trade geography

Hodgson says the so-called Nepalese paper was not manufactured chiefly in Nepal proper but in Cis-Himalayan Bhote regions, with Kathmandu acting as a major market. Paper moved both south into Nepal/Hindustan and northward into Tibetan areas.

He gives market prices for paper and dried pulp and describes paper manufactories in relation to forests, water and fuel supplies.

This makes the paper project simultaneously:

`craft history + resource geography + commodity circulation + documentary infrastructure`.

## 11. The 1842 paper-pulp shipment proves the experiment continued

A later secondary reconstruction cites a Hodgson letter to G. Woodcock, Joint Magistrate at Champaran, dated **25 March 1842**, concerning the supply of **24 packages of Nepalese paper pulp to Calcutta**.

It identifies the primary source as:
`Register of Letters Received from January 1842 to June 1842`, pp. 344–45, Bihar State Archives, Patna.

Secondary source:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1984%20The%20State%20and%20Economic%20Surplus--Production%20Trade%20and%20Resource%20Mobilization%20in%20early%2019th%20Century%20Nepal%20by%20Regmi%20s.pdf

A published excerpt of the letter says Hodgson considered two routes:

- packages to Patna under the Magistrate's care, by land;
- if land carriage Motiharee–Patna was too expensive, direct **water carriage from Govind Gang to Calcutta**, using a `small safe boat` under the charge of a **burkundaz**.

Excerpt source:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/Regmi%20Research%20Series/Regmi_12.pdf

This is direct evidence that the paper project had become a logistics problem involving:

`24 packages → route comparison → freight cost → water/land carriage → security escort → Calcutta destination`.

The original Bihar State Archives letter should eventually be checked, but the public secondary transcription is specific enough to establish a strong archive target.

## 13. Burnouf's Paris programme deliberately purchased redundant witnesses

Recent reconstruction of the Hodgson–Burnouf correspondence shows that Burnouf did not simply accept whatever arrived.

In 1837, even when the Société Asiatique already possessed a well-made 1810 manuscript of the *Kāraṇḍavyūha*, Burnouf ordered additional copies because rapidly executed copies often contained serious errors. He wanted **two or three copies of the same text** in order to collate them.

Source:
https://histoire.unistra.fr/websites/histoire/Departements_instituts/histoire-religions/Publications_PDF/PIHR3_2022_GD_Burnouf_Liseuse.pdf

The same study maps six Hodgson *Kāraṇḍavyūha* witnesses across Calcutta, London, Oxford and Paris, plus five *Guṇakāraṇḍavyūha* witnesses across Bengal/Fort William/India Office/RAS/Paris.

This suggests a strong formulation:

> Hodgson's manuscript network was partly designed as a **distributed redundancy system**. Multiple copies across institutions were not merely duplicate gifts; at least in Burnouf's programme, redundancy was intentionally purchased as protection against scribal error and as material for collation.

## 14. The 64-copy Paris shipment was catalogued immediately

A study of the *Mahāvastu* notes that the shipment of 64 manuscripts specially copied for the Société Asiatique made a major impression and that a catalogue of the shipment was established **on the same day**; it was printed in *Journal Asiatique*, 3rd series, vol. 4 (1837), pp. 296–298.

Source:
https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/17113/1/Tournier%20Mvu%20Vin%20Lok%20ARIRIAB%20XV%202012.pdf

The *Journal Asiatique* 1837 volume is publicly digitised via Gallica/HathiTrust, but the exact pages have not yet been directly extracted in this pass.

Immediate cataloguing is significant: receipt and description were nearly simultaneous operations.

## 15. Emerging mechanism: auditability

Across these materials a new concept may be more precise than `portability` alone: **auditability**.

Objects had to remain recoverable and checkable after they moved.

Mechanisms included:
- numbered correspondence between skeleton/skin/drawing;
- duplicate-copy collation;
- box/crate/basta inventories;
- specimen distribution tables;
- manuscript catalogues made on arrival;
- institutional receipt letters;
- lists of missing drawings and parties holding them;
- corrected copies of inaccurate tables;
- completeness checks (`the whole … procured and properly preserved`).

Provisional claim to test:

> Nineteenth-century long-distance knowledge did not depend only on making objects portable. It depended on making their identities and histories **auditable after movement**.

This may offer a sharper bridge between archive history, manuscript philology and natural history.
