# Public-web research wave 7 — object identity, labels, wrappers, type material, and auditability

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. No new private/archive scans added in this wave.

## 1. Hodgson's zoological numbering system was relational, not one-object/one-number

A systematic study of Hodgson's bird collections reconstructs the original specimen-label system as follows:

- Hodgson's birds originally carried a pair of labels;
- one recorded date of collection, locality, and a local name;
- a second label carried the number of the corresponding drawing, written in red ink;
- Hodgson used the drawing number to relate specimens to his master series of drawings;
- the British Museum (Natural History) later removed Hodgson's original labels from many specimens;
- in some cases the same Hodgson drawing number could occur on specimens now treated as more than one taxon.

This matters because a `Hodgson number` is not automatically a unique specimen identifier. Modern type validation therefore has to combine surviving labels, accession dates, drawings, original descriptions, and collection history.

Source:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228490048_Systematic_notes_on_Asian_birds_52_An_introduction_to_the_bird_collections_of_Brian_Houghton_Hodgson_1801-1894

A related type study explicitly states that the drawing number should lead to the unpublished drawing, but that type identification remains impossible in many cases without evidence that the specimen was collected before the taxon was described. A drawing number could apply to several comparable specimens.

Source:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228904274_Systematic_notes_on_Asian_birds_50_Types_of_the_Aegithalidae_Remizidae_and_Paridae

## 2. Concrete microcase: drawing number 244 and type attribution

A later systematic type review notes that Hodgson drawing no. 244 is linked to multiple surviving specimen labels. The authors argue that specimens annotated `No. 244` cannot simply be treated as types of the name previously attributed to Hodgson/Blyth; their evidential relation instead fits the material used for G. R. Gray's 1846 name.

The important point is methodological: **a surviving number is not self-interpreting evidence**. Type status depends on reconstructing which author saw which specimen at which moment and how the drawing/specimen relation travelled through later collections.

Source:
https://www.aviansystematics.org/uploads/downloads/126/file/ZV340_115-160.pdf

## 3. Contemporary language of authenticity in the zoological network

H. E. Strickland wrote to Hodgson on 23 May 1845 thanking him for 205 bird specimens and describing them as the `original and authentic types` of the numerous novelties Hodgson had described.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/asia-2?listLimit=10&listPage=39&page=10&sort=referenceCode&sortDir=asc

This is analytically useful because `original` and `authentic` are not manuscript-only categories. Naturalists themselves used them for specimen evidence.

## 4. Distributed zoological objects were designed to be reassembled by number

In a 1845 proposal concerning osteological materials, Hodgson wrote that the bones were for the most part numbered by reference to the skins and drawings he had presented to the British Museum. He expected the recipient institution to identify species through that cross-reference and requested that anatomical examination results be communicated back to him.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/nepal-2?listLimit=10&listPage=2&onlyDirect=1&page=10&sort=date&sortDir=desc

The resulting object was therefore deliberately **distributed across institutions and media**:

**skin ↔ drawing ↔ osteological material ↔ number ↔ description ↔ returned anatomical report**.

This is stronger than simple circulation. The scientific object was constituted by a relation among physically separated witnesses.

## 5. Property and custody could break the evidential chain

British Museum correspondence in March 1845 records Hodgson's attempt to recover missing drawings. Hodgson reported having recovered 65 drawings but identified others still held by Mr Howard. Museum solicitors concluded that Hodgson may have lost legal claim to the drawings because of a levy/distress involving Howard's property.

Hodgson annotated the Museum's distribution statement as being full of `omissions and inaccuracies` and asked for a correct copy.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=50487&sf_culture=nl&showAdvanced=1&sort=endDate&sortDir=desc&topLod=0&view=table

This connects property, custody, copying, and scientific auditability. An object's evidential chain could fail because the object was legally inaccessible, physically missing, or incorrectly represented in a distribution table.

## 6. Institutional handling created a natural experiment in provenance survival

The later systematic history of Hodgson's collections records that original Hodgson labels survive especially on specimens that **did not pass through the BMNH during the Grays' tenure**.

- Some Cambridge University Museum of Zoology specimens from the Strickland/Jardine collections retain Hodgson labels, in some cases with numbers in black ink.
- Frederic Moore at the East India Company Museum did not remove Hodgson's original labels; Sharpe later explicitly praised this.
- Leiden later relabelled its entire collection (1897–1904), leaving uncertainty about whether Hodgson specimens arrived there with original labels or with BMNH replacements.

Source:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228490048_Systematic_notes_on_Asian_birds_52_An_introduction_to_the_bird_collections_of_Brian_Houghton_Hodgson_1801-1894

This gives a strong comparative design: **the same donor's specimens passed through different curatorial regimes, and those regimes produced different levels of twenty-first-century auditability**.

## 7. Delayed unpacking was itself a curatorial variable

A history of Hodgson's zoological collections reports that when the Russian traveller Fedchenko visited the India Museum in 1874 he was unable to inspect the Hodgson bird skins because they were still not unpacked. The East India Company collection was later transferred to the British Museum when the India Museum was dissolved.

Source:
https://www.discoveringbuddha.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2005-Origins-of-Himalayan-Studies-Brian-H-Hodgson-by-Waterhouse.pdf

This extends the chain beyond packing for outbound transport. **A packed state could persist for decades inside a receiving institution**, delaying comparison, description, and scholarly access while potentially preserving some original associations that active recataloguing might later destroy.

## 8. Hodgson's manuscript wrappers could determine — and misdetermine — textual identity

### RAS Hodgson MS 66, Prayogamukham

The Royal Asiatic Society catalogue states that the manuscript is incomplete at the end and that its title is known from the wrapper only.

Source:
https://royalasiaticcollections.org/ras-hodgson-ms-66-prayogamukham/

### RAS Hodgson MS 67, Hetubinduṭīkā

This is a particularly strong case. Cowell and Eggeling had catalogued the palm-leaf manuscript as `Anumânakhaṇḍam`, apparently from a provisional title on the wrapper. A 2016 re-examination determined that the codex actually contains two incomplete works: the beginning of Ratnakīrti's *Sthirasiddhidūṣaṇa* and a large portion of Arcaṭa's *Hetubinduṭīkā*.

Source:
https://royalasiaticcollections.org/ras-hodgson-ms-67-hetubindu%E1%B9%ADika/

This is direct evidence that **packaging/paracontent can stabilise a wrong object identity for more than a century**.

## 9. Hodgson slips were themselves bibliographic instruments

Cowell and Eggeling's 1875 catalogue records cases in which a text had no internal title and the designation came from a Hodgson slip. One example is their no. 53, `Gītapustakam`, where the catalogue explicitly says the manuscript is a collection of vernacular hymns without title and that the designation was given on Hodgson's slip. The same catalogue records missing leaves and physical condition such as worm damage.

Accessible transcription/scan surrogate:
https://www.scribd.com/document/844820427/Cowell-E-B-Eggeling-J-Art-I-Catalogue-of-Buddhist-Sanskrit-Manuscripts-in-the-Possession-of-the-Royal-Asiatic-Society

This suggests a hierarchy of identity-bearing inscriptions worth separating:

**internal title/colophon → folio marks → wrapper title → Hodgson slip → institutional catalogue title → modern re-identification**.

Each layer can correct or overwrite an earlier one.

## 10. Modern foliation can also become a misleading archival intervention

A detailed study of RAS Hodgson MS 35 (the `Vanaratna Codex`) finds damaged/lost folios and an apparent jump in the original foliation. A modern hand, possibly connected with earlier cataloguing, later supplied Arabic-numeral folio numbers in pencil. The modern numbering does not necessarily agree with the manuscript's original foliation logic and may embody an earlier reconstruction rather than an original fact.

Source:
https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/21/75/89/06/2021758906/2021758906.pdf

Analytic implication: later `repair` of sequence can itself become a source of epistemic noise.

## 11. Divyāvadāna: copy age, correctness, and legibility do not align simply

Cowell and Neil's later editorial history of the *Divyāvadāna* gives an unusually clear comparison of witnesses:

- some nineteenth-century manuscripts were described as very incorrect;
- another was treated as correct;
- the Hodgson manuscript sent to the Société Asiatique in 1837 was described as a very correct copy;
- importantly, because it had been copied for Hodgson more than fifty years earlier, it sometimes preserved readings that had since become illegible in the original from which the copies descended.

Modern summary reproducing Cowell and Neil's witness descriptions:
https://www.discoveringbuddha.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2008-Divine-Stories-Divyavadana-Part-1-Andy-Rotman-2008.pdf

This provides a major historical point: **a copy can become a better witness than its source once the source deteriorates**. `Original` therefore does not automatically equal `best evidence`.

## 12. A stronger concept than simple portability: auditability

The two evidence domains now expose a common problem.

### Zoological auditability

A taxonomic claim must be traceable across:

**specimen → original label → drawing number → drawing → accession → description → distribution/custody → later type decision**.

### Manuscript auditability

A textual claim must be traceable across:

**folio → foliation → title/colophon → wrapper/slip → copy relation → missing/supplied leaves → catalogue → collation → later re-identification**.

`Portability` explains how objects can move. `Auditability` asks whether, after movement and re-handling, scholars can still reconstruct why a particular body, leaf, copy, title, or number should count as evidence for a particular claim.

## 13. Packaging and paper as preservation interfaces

Hooker's 1849 letter from the field is unusually explicit about paper functions. He asks Hodgson to send two kinds of white paper, one for drawings and the other for specimen tickets. He says they can be placed between quires and tied tightly with Nepal or brown paper used for drying plants so that they arrive safely.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/letter-from-joseph-dalton-hooker-to-brian-houghton-hodgson-22

A separate Hooker letter asks Hodgson to ensure bundles of dried plants are kept away from ground and walls and checked for mould.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?names=8774&page=10&showAdvanced=1&sort=startDate&sortDir=asc&topLod=0&view=table

This gives paper at least five distinct field/logistical functions:

- drawing support;
- specimen-ticket support;
- plant-drying medium;
- protective wrapping/interleaving;
- moisture-management interface.

This is a better model than assigning a fixed epistemic meaning to a paper type.

## 14. BL procurement confirms a separate packing-material economy

The BL Hodgson inventory records an 1843 bill for 1,012 bundles of Nepali paper plus three pieces of wax cloth, thread, and twine, cross-referenced to manuscript production/packing accounts.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol012.html

Direct Hodgson-specific evidence for cotton/straw/tow as void-filling material remains unlocated. Wax cloth, thread/twine, brown/Nepal paper, boxes, packages, crates, carpenters, and porters are confirmed; cushioning/filling should remain a live question rather than a finding.

## 15. Revised working proposition

A stronger formulation now seems testable:

> Hodgson's knowledge infrastructure did not merely make Himalayan objects portable. It attempted to make them **auditable after separation**. Numbers, labels, wrappers, slips, foliation, copies, distribution tables, receipts and catalogues allowed physically distributed witnesses to be reassembled as evidence. The same devices could fail: labels were removed, numbers reused, drawings became legally inaccessible, wrappers supplied wrong titles, foliation was reconstructed incorrectly, and originals deteriorated until copies became superior witnesses.

This formulation preserves real differences between manuscripts and zoological specimens while making their comparison historically specific.
