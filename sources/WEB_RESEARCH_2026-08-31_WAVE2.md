# Public-web research wave 2 — scriptures, paper, copying, authentication, and transport

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. No new private/archive scans imported. This wave deliberately does **not** assume Buddhist material is secondary. The relative weight of zoology, Buddhist manuscripts, drawings, administration, and geography should be decided by evidence density and mechanism, not by a pre-imposed disciplinary hierarchy.

## 1. Scope correction

The earlier working hierarchy treated Buddhist manuscript material as a controlled comparison to natural history. The public evidence now makes that too restrictive.

A better working rule is:

> Treat Hodgson's Buddhist manuscripts, zoological specimens/drawings, geographical papers, and administrative records as distinct classes of knowledge objects moving through partially overlapping infrastructures. Test similarities and differences empirically.

The project should ask how these objects were made **credible, identifiable, countable, portable, and institutionally usable**.

## 2. Hodgson's Buddhist corpus was a major manufactured and distributed archive

W. W. Hunter's 1881 *Catalogue of Sanskrit Manuscripts, collected in Nepal, and presented to various Libraries and Learned Societies by Brian Houghton Hodgson* states that Hodgson's Sanskrit Buddhist works were distributed between 1827 and 1845 to Calcutta, the Royal Asiatic Society, the India Office Library, Paris, and Oxford. It records 64 manuscripts specifically **copied for the Société Asiatique** and a complete Kanjur/Tanjur of 334 volumes procured from Tibet.

Hunter catalogue PDF:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1881%20Catalogue%20of%20Sanskrit%20Manuscripts%20collected%20in%20Nepal%20by%20Brian%20Houghton%20Hodgson%20by%20Hunter%20s.pdf

Important methodological point: the Cowell–Eggeling catalogue excerpt reprinted by Hunter explicitly says that, unless otherwise stated, the manuscript material is **Indian paper**, and then records material, dimensions, number of lines, age, condition, missing/supplied leaves, later hands, and writing quality. This means textual identification was already materially mediated.

Examples include:
- palm leaf vs paper;
- old / oldish / modern handwriting;
- peculiar numeral and letter forms;
- later supplied leaves;
- `very incorrect` manuscripts;
- careless handwriting;
- worm-eaten and defaced leaves;
- rolls, blackened paper, coloured writing, and wrappers.

## 3. Commissioned copying was an explicit scholarly infrastructure

The 1837 Paris catalogue is titled:

**Catalogue des livres bouddhiques, écrits en sanskrit, que M. B. H. Hodgson a fait copier au Népal pour le compte de la Société Asiatique**.

It itemises the commissioned copies by:
- work title;
- number of leaves;
- distichs per unit;
- total distichs.

The original Sanskrit catalogue itself, dated 29 September 1836, was deposited in the Société Asiatique archive.

Hunter reprint, pp. 17–18:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1881%20Catalogue%20of%20Sanskrit%20Manuscripts%20collected%20in%20Nepal%20by%20Brian%20Houghton%20Hodgson%20by%20Hunter%20s.pdf

Burnouf later described Hodgson's offer to procure copies for learned societies and said the 64 Paris manuscripts had been copied on behalf of the Société Asiatique.

Burnouf, *Introduction à l'histoire du buddhisme indien* public scan:
https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/gdb250b3258740.pdf

This is a key research object: copying was not an incidental act but a scalable, measured production process for creating scholarly surrogates.

## 4. Paris correspondence: copies + crates + safe routes + scholarly reward

Royal Asiatic Society archival descriptions preserve a tight sequence:

- 15 May 1836: Burnouf thanks Hodgson for reporting manuscripts procured for the Société Asiatique.
- 5 June 1837: Burnouf thanks Hodgson for the Prajñāpāramitā and 24 manuscript volumes.
- 15 July 1837: Burnouf acknowledges the **safe arrival of three cases of manuscripts** at the Société Asiatique; a Gold Medal is to be struck for Hodgson.
- 15 July 1837: another Burnouf letter acknowledges the copied Sanskrit Buddhist manuscripts, including a `magnificent` copy of Rakṣābhagavatī.
- 29 July 1837: Julius von Mohl again reports the arrival of the **three crates**.
- 27 October 1837: Burnouf asks Hodgson to send further copies for Burnouf personally and provides a **safer address for sending manuscripts**.

RAS Hodgson archive browse:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=673&creators=1146&showAdvanced=1&sort=endDate&sortDir=asc&topLod=0

This links copying, packing, route risk, receipt, intellectual use, patronage/reward, and institutional reputation in one sequence.

## 5. Oxford: access was regulated through custody and safe return

A 2 March 1837 Bodleian letter acknowledges Sanskrit manuscripts sent by Hodgson and records the condition that the Boden Professor H. H. Wilson could use them at his residence **with care and return them safely to the library**.

Source:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/letter-from-bulkeley-bandinel-librarian-of-bodleian-library-to-prof-h-h-wilson-2-march-1837

The object did not become scholarly evidence merely on arrival; institutional custody rules governed its further mobility.

## 6. Identification depended on material comparison and copy comparison

Cowell and Eggeling explicitly state that detailed analysis of Hodgson's Buddhist Sanskrit works would often be impossible without **comparing other copies**.

Cambridge Core:
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/art-icatalogue-of-buddhist-sanskrit-manuscripts-in-the-possession-of-the-royal-asiatic-society-hodgson-collection/BCDBFD2BBAC2E056B6E489258AE74E2C

The RAS digital collection gives concrete examples:

### RAS Hodgson MS 67 — Hetubinduṭīkā
Previously catalogued as *Anumânakhaṇḍam*, apparently because Cowell and Eggeling followed a **provisional title on the wrapper**. Modern re-examination shows the codex actually contains two incomplete works.

https://royalasiaticcollections.org/ras-hodgson-ms-67-hetubindu%E1%B9%ADika/

This is a direct case where wrapper-level identification produced a catalogue identity that later textual comparison overturned.

### RAS Hodgson MS 37 — Saṃpuṭodbhava
The digital record notes damaged leaves and that **a fragment of the original cover was scanned at the end**.

https://royalasiaticcollections.org/ras-hodgson-ms-37-sa%E1%B9%83pu%E1%B9%ADodbhava/

### RAS Hodgson MS 13 — Bodhicaryāvatāra
Palm leaf; described as old; numeral and letter shapes called unusual.

https://royalasiaticcollections.org/ras-hodgson-ms-13-bodhicaryavatara/

### RAS Hodgson MS 52 — Tantraślokasaṃgraha
Paper manuscript described as oldish and much worm-eaten.

https://royalasiaticcollections.org/ras-hodgson-ms-52-tantraslokasa%E1%B9%83graha/

These records make material state part of identification, dating, and textual usability.

## 7. A strong microhistory: Ratnaparīkṣā and copy quality

Louis Finot's 1896 edition of the *Ratnaparīkṣā* compares multiple manuscripts. One Paris manuscript from the Hodgson 1837 Société Asiatique collection is described as Nepalese paper. A Calcutta witness is yellow paper, Newari script, dated 1814, and characterised as highly incorrect; Finot says it appears copied from the same original as another witness and is nearly useless for establishing the text because of its extreme incorrectness. The RAS Hodgson manuscript is also paper/Newari and described as incorrect but with notable variants.

Public scan:
https://bcub.ro/lib2life/Les%20lapidaires%20indiens_Finot%20Louis_Paris_1896.pdf

This is almost a ready-made case study of **material witness → scribal quality → genealogical inference → textual authority**.

## 8. Burnouf's philology depended on multiple Hodgson witnesses

BnF/INHA material records Hodgson's Nepal manuscripts sent to Burnouf: Lalitavistara, Aṣṭasāhasrikāprajñāpāramitā, Mahāvastu, and two Saddharmapuṇḍarīka witnesses. Burnouf collated the Lotus manuscripts with Société Asiatique and British collections.

BnF heritage page:
https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-southasia/eugene-burnouf-article

A modern bibliography of Lotus witnesses records several Hodgson-linked copies with distinct dimensions, line counts, dates, pagination problems, and institutional locations; different witnesses became basic texts for different editors.

Example public record:
https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/de6f0428-40a3-405a-a33e-acd3c4433e0d/content

This suggests a history of Buddhism can be reconstructed as a history of **collation across materially non-identical surrogates**.

## 9. Hodgson's rhetoric of `proof` was explicitly manuscript-based

In *Quotations in Proof of his Sketch of Buddhism*, Hodgson says Orientalists had asked him for proofs, so he published translated passages from ancient Buddhist books still extant in Nepal in `original Sanskrit`. He says Amrita Nanda Bandya had made these extracts while Hodgson was collecting the works.

Cambridge Core:
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/art-xvi-quotations-in-proof-of-his-sketch-of-buddhism/53E9A43A51E36C6B3410F614B123BE4A

The interesting issue is not simply Hodgson's doctrine of `original Sanskrit`; it is the practical chain needed to make an alleged original into usable proof:

**locate → acquire/commission → identify → copy/extract → translate → compare → package → send → receive → catalogue → collate → cite**.

## 10. Buddhist drawings and explanatory manuscripts were also packed knowledge ensembles

RAS archive descriptions show Hodgson did not keep `texts` and `images` conceptually separate.

### 1837–1844: five boxes + sixth package
`List of Buddha Books & drawings, & of Statistics of Nepaul left in Residency in five Boxes, by B.H.Hodgson, Dec 1st 1837.` Each box is individually listed; a sixth package is listed; the boxes were later **repackaged in 1844 to take home**. Hodgson noted that his literary and scientific stores should, in the event of his death, pass to his father for disposal to the advantage of his reputation.

RAS browse source:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=673&levels=241&media=print&names=2296&sf_culture=en&sort=endDate&sortDir=desc&subjects=643&topLod=0&view=table

### 1842–1844: bastas/bundles in Trunk No. 1
A detailed list of manuscripts in `Bastas or Bundles in Trunk No. 1 Cathmandu AD 1842`; subjects include Buddhism, genealogies, army details, catalogues and śāstras. The material was **repacked in 1844 and put on board the Hardwicke with Hodgson**.

RAS browse source:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?levels=241&names=2296&sort=alphabetic&sortDir=asc&subjects=643&topLod=0

### 1855: cloth + paper drawings + trunk papers
A list of pictorial illustrations of Nepalese and Tibetan Buddhism explicitly describes drawings **on cloth and on paper** and adds a list of papers in Trunk No. 2.

RAS archive browse:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=673&page=5&repos=452&sf_culture=nl&sort=lastUpdated&sortDir=asc&subjects=643&topLod=0

### 1857–1859: Institute of France
Hodgson's `Architectural illustrations of Buddhism` says that while `discovering, procuring & testing the general character of the literary monuments of Buddhism` he was also accumulating architectural and sculptural illustrations. The fair copy records delivery to the Institute of France through the British Embassy in Paris.

RAS record:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?levels=241&names=2296&sort=alphabetic&sortDir=asc&subjects=643&topLod=0

The 1859 Académie report says **two cases** sent by Hodgson contained numerous Buddhist paintings and drawings accompanied by explanatory texts.

Persée:
https://www.persee.fr/doc/crai_0065-0536_1859_num_3_1_66153

Thus `drawing + explanation + list + case` can be treated as a composite epistemic object.

## 11. Indigenous manuscript materiality must remain visible

A history of Hodgson's copies cannot reduce Nepalese Buddhist books to raw text awaiting European philology.

Useful comparative material:

### Kathmandu manuscript, 1678
Smithsonian Karandavyūha: 77 numbered folios plus blanks in **blue-black handmade multilayered paper lettered in gilt**, with paintings and polychrome wooden covers.

https://asia-archive.si.edu/object/S1991.161/

### BL Endangered Archives Programme
A survey of Buddhist Sanskrit manuscripts in Lalitpur notes that Nepalese manuscripts generally have metal or wooden covers, significant both as protection and as painted surfaces.

https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002851210

### Cambridge/CSMC, Kathmandu 1719
A bundle of more than 200 yellow-paper leaves between wooden covers; the manuscript was commissioned by a Buddhist household, intended for domestic keeping, and bears traces of ritual veneration on the cover.

https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/aom/mom/42-en.html

### Book as protected portable object
Cleveland Museum notes that pothi boards could be connected by cord and the entire manuscript wrapped in cloth for protection from elements and insects; it explicitly describes the format as portable.

https://www.clevelandart.org/print/art/1938.301.a

These sources matter because Hodgson's acquisition/copying/packing systems intersected with pre-existing South Asian/Himalayan practices of covers, cords, wrappers, ritual handling, and bundle identity.

## 12. Material analysis offers a modern test without anachronism

Helman-Ważny and van Schaik demonstrate that Tibetan manuscript research can combine fibre analysis, codicology, palaeography, and textual evidence to group manuscripts by production region and book culture.

https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-4754.2012.00687.x

This should be used as a modern analytical model for what can be tested on Hodgson objects, not as evidence that Hodgson himself practised modern fibre analysis.

## 13. Immediate hypotheses now worth testing

### H1. Copying as knowledge manufacture
The 64 Société Asiatique copies were a controlled production run. Costs, paper supply, hands, dimensions, layout, error rates, and copying sequence may be reconstructable.

### H2. `Original` was operational, not self-evident
Hodgson and Burnouf used `original Sanskrit` rhetorically, but catalogues and later editors show that originality, age, correctness, completeness, title, and textual identity required material and comparative work.

### H3. Packaging stabilised identity but could also distort it
Bundles, wrappers, title slips, crates, trunks, and box-content lists helped maintain object identity during circulation. Yet wrapper titles could misidentify texts, and later institutional rebinding could create new object boundaries.

### H4. Buddhist and zoological logistics may be genuinely comparable
Both domains exhibit counting, lists, drawings, labels/titles, copies, packaging, transit, receipt, sorting/comparison, and redistribution. The comparison must preserve differences: a manuscript could be a sacred/ritual object and textual witness, while a specimen had different preservation and taxonomic constraints.

### H5. Reputation was built into storage and shipment
Hodgson explicitly linked the future disposal of his `literary and scientific stores` to his reputation. Shipping and archiving therefore participated in authorship and priority, not only in preservation.
