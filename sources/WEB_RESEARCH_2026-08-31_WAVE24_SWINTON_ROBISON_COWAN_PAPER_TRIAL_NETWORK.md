# Web research 2026-08-31 — Wave 24
## Swinton–Robison–Cowan and the British industrial trial of Nepali paper material

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: actor/site identification follow-up to Wave 23. Scope remains paper itself. This note asks who actually handled the failed British remanufacture of `Nipal Paper Stuff`, which industrial setting they belonged to, and how the paper experiment connected Calcutta government to Edinburgh science and Scottish papermaking.

## Executive result

The anonymous-looking British end of the 1833 `Nipal Paper Stuff` report can now be substantially resolved.

The printed JASB proceedings explicitly name:

- **George Swinton** — transmitter of the material/correspondence from India;
- **John Robison** — General Secretary of the Royal Society of Edinburgh;
- **Charles Cowan** — paper manufacturer who `worked-up` the material and supplied trial specimens.

This produces a concrete trans-imperial experimental chain:

`Nepal/Himalayan paper material`
→ `George Swinton / Bengal Government / Calcutta`
→ `Edinburgh scientific network`
→ `John Robison, RSE`
→ `Charles Cowan, Scottish papermaker`
→ `manufacturing trials + specimens + commercial valuation`
→ `results returned to Calcutta`
→ `JASB publication, 1833`.

The result also sharpens the earlier material claim. The failed remanufacture occurred in contact with a Scottish papermaker embedded in one of Britain's early mechanised paper districts, not simply with a metropolitan laboratory.

However, the exact apparatus used for the Nepali pulp trial is **not established**. Cowan's family mill at Valleyfield had a Fourdrinier machine from 1821, but the 1833 report does not say that this particular batch was made on that machine. Do not convert industrial context into an object-level process assertion.

- `INDUSTRIAL_CONTEXT_NOT_TRIAL_METHOD`

# 1. The 1833 report names Charles Cowan

Primary source:

*Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* 2 (1833), Asiatic Society proceedings, p. 93.

Digital scan:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9c/Journal_of_the_Asiatic_Society_of_Bengal_%28IA_journalofasiatic02asia%29.pdf

The report first refers to specimens furnished by `Mr. C. Cowan` to Robison, then explicitly states:

`Mr. Charles Cowan mentions in his notes on working-up the stuff...`

Thus the trial papermaker's first name is not an inference from initials.

Cowan reported that the material had initially been as tough as any material with which he was acquainted, leading the JASB report to infer deterioration after its earlier state.

He also supplied a commercial valuation of the pulp cakes:

- £6–8 per ton in the ordinary estimated state;
- nearly double if suitable for cartridge paper;
- no expectation of a more refined use after the trial results.

These figures make the operation simultaneously:

- material testing;
- product-grade classification;
- commercial valuation.

Mechanism:

`TECHNICAL_PROPERTY_TO_MARKET_GRADE_TRANSLATION`.

# 2. Charles Cowan's industrial setting

Charles Cowan (1801–1889) belonged to the Cowan papermaking family associated with Valleyfield and related Penicuik mills in Midlothian.

Contemporary/later industrial histories and Penicuik papermaking records agree that Valleyfield was mechanised early. A Fourdrinier machine made by Bryan Donkin & Co. was installed in **1821**. The local papermaking history states that one such machine could make in a day what hand production made in a week.

Reference:
https://penicuikpapermaking.org/valleyfieldm.html

Charles Cowan had entered papermaking as a young man and was sent in 1819 to St Mary Cray, Kent, to learn the trade before returning to the family enterprise. Later sources identify him as a leading paper manufacturer; he would subsequently write on papermaking and in 1846 patented a stuff/consistency regulator for controlling paper-machine furnish weight.

These later details demonstrate sustained technical expertise but should not be projected backward to specify the exact 1833 trial procedure.

### Source-control distinction

Secure:

- JASB names `Charles Cowan` as the manufacturer working up the Nepali material;
- Charles Cowan was a professional Scottish papermaker connected to the Penicuik/Valleyfield enterprise;
- Valleyfield had a Fourdrinier machine by 1821.

Not yet secure:

- that the Nepali pulp trial occurred specifically at Valleyfield;
- that the trial sheets were formed on the Fourdrinier rather than by a hand/laboratory method;
- which Cowan mill or vat/machine was used;
- whether trial specimens survive in the Cowan or RSE archives.

Mechanism:

`INDUSTRIAL_CONTEXT_NOT_TRIAL_METHOD`.

# 3. Why Valleyfield context matters even without proving machine use

By 1833 the Cowan enterprise sat within an industrial regime already capable of both older papermaking knowledge and early mechanisation.

Valleyfield's Fourdrinier had been installed a decade before the Nepali trial. Scottish paper-industry history also associates the Cowan mills with bank-note paper and with early adoption of chlorine bleaching and other chemical processing.

This changes how to read the 1833 failure.

It was not simply:

`European ignorance encounters exotic fibre`.

Nor can it yet be written as:

`Fourdrinier machine cannot handle Himalayan pulp`.

The defensible formulation is:

**a technologically sophisticated British papermaker could not reproduce the softness and toughness of the finished Nepal-made paper from transported dried pulp.**

That is already a significant negative result.

It suggests that the missing variables lay in some combination of:

- deterioration during storage/transport;
- pulp preparation state;
- alkaline chemistry;
- fibre beating/dispersion;
- sheet-formation practice;
- drainage regime;
- bleaching intensity;
- drying/finishing.

The trial itself does not isolate which variable caused the failure.

# 4. John Robison: the Edinburgh scientific relay

The `Mr. Robison, Sec. Ed. Roy. Society` of the 1833 report is John Robison, General Secretary of the Royal Society of Edinburgh from 1828 to 1840.

Contemporary confirmation:

British Association report for 1833 lists `John Robison` among the Secretaries for Edinburgh and identifies him with the Royal Society of Edinburgh secretaryship.

Royal Society London archival catalogue also preserves an 18 October 1832 letter from `John Robison, [General] Secretary of the Royal Society Edinburgh`.

Record:
https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/calmview/Record.aspx?id=MC%2F2%2F49&src=CalmView.Catalog

Robison therefore functions here not as a paper manufacturer but as a scientific/material-testing broker connecting Swinton's Indian material transmission to specialist manufacturers.

# 5. George Swinton: Calcutta government as a commercial-science forwarding node

The British Library India Office catalogue identifies George Swinton as **Chief Secretary to the Government of Bengal** in 1831.

A directly comparable Board's Collection file shows his operating procedure with another Indian material:

`IOR/F/4/1330/52529`, redwood dye from Burma.

The file contains:

- Swinton's government note;
- an experiment report from a Calcutta silk printer;
- transmission to the East India Company Court of Directors;
- dyed specimens and raw material;
- an explicit request for further experiments in England to determine value in the London market.

BL record:
https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/041-000745150

The paper-pulp trial therefore belongs to a broader Swinton method:

`Asian raw material/specimen -> Calcutta preliminary evaluation -> Britain/Scotland specialist trial -> commercial/technical judgment -> information returned to India`.

This does not make all such projects identical. It establishes that Swinton repeatedly used long-distance specimen testing as an administrative-commercial knowledge technique.

Mechanisms:

- `SWINTON_TRANSIMPERIAL_MATERIAL_TESTING_PROGRAM`
- `CALCUTTA_AS_SAMPLE_FORWARDING_NODE`
- `IMPERIAL_COMMODITY_EVALUATION_CHAIN`.

# 6. The paper experiment crosses three knowledge institutions

The chain involves at least three institutional regimes:

### Bengal Government / Calcutta

- acquires/forwards material;
- frames commercial usefulness;
- receives and circulates results.

### Royal Society of Edinburgh / Robison

- receives material/correspondence;
- finds specialist testers;
- converts colonial sample into scientific-society problem.

### Scottish paper manufacturer / Cowan

- performs `working-up` operations;
- produces specimens;
- describes physical failure;
- assigns possible paper grades and market value.

The paper itself therefore moves through administrative, scientific and industrial classifications.

At each interface its identity changes:

`local paper material`
→ `transport pulp brick`
→ `experimental paper stuff`
→ `trial sheet`
→ `commercial-grade candidate`
→ `published material report`.

Mechanism:

`MATERIAL_OBJECT_RECLASSIFIED_ACROSS_INSTITUTIONS`.

# 7. The 1833 comparison is stronger because Cowan was a papermaker

The report's contrast between failed Cowan sheets and successful finished Nepal sheets is not merely a comparison between local craft and an amateur reconstruction.

Cowan was a professional manufacturer embedded in a major Scottish papermaking enterprise.

The negative result therefore supports a more exact argument:

**the transfer failure cannot be explained simply by the absence of metropolitan papermaking expertise.**

What was missing was some element of the production history or the material condition preserved by local manufacture but lost between pulp preparation, transport and remanufacture.

This strengthens:

`PROCESS_HISTORY_EMBODIED_IN_SHEET`.

# 8. Relation to the 1842 Serampore result

There are now two different industrial encounters with Himalayan paper material:

## Scotland / Cowan, reported 1833

- material transferred as dried pulp brick;
- professional British manufacturer;
- resulting sheets materially disappointing;
- exact sheet-forming technology used in trial unresolved.

## Bengal / Serampore, 1842

- papermaking knowledge partially reconstructed at Darjeeling with skilled makers;
- fibre/pulp then tested in mechanised industrial production at Serampore;
- mucilaginous behaviour interfered with machine-web flow/thickness.

The two episodes should remain distinct.

Together they establish that the material problem persisted across more than one industrial interface.

Mechanism:

`REPEATED_INDUSTRIAL_INTERFACE_MISMATCH`.

# 9. New archival target: find the trial specimens

The next high-value British archival search should target:

1. Royal Society of Edinburgh correspondence received by John Robison, c.1831–33;
2. papers or business records of Alexander Cowan & Sons / Valleyfield;
3. Charles Cowan's notebooks/diaries and papermaking notes;
4. specimens accompanying Swinton/Robison correspondence;
5. paper samples marked `strongly bleached` or other trial states;
6. references to `Nipal Paper Stuff`, `Nepal paper`, `Swinton`, `Robison`, or `Cowan` in Scottish papermaking archives.

If specimens survive, measure:

- laid/wove or other formation structure;
- thickness/caliper;
- opacity/transparency;
- fibre inclusions/brown spots;
- shrinkage distortion;
- surface smoothness;
- sizing;
- watermark/countermark if any;
- whether the sheets were handmade or machine-formed.

This could turn the 1833 textual report into a direct material comparison with surviving Nepal-made sheets.

## Bottom line

The failed British reconstruction now has identifiable agents and an industrial setting. George Swinton used Calcutta/Bengal government channels to send Himalayan paper material into an Edinburgh experimental network; John Robison brokered specialist evaluation through the Royal Society of Edinburgh; Charles Cowan, a professional papermaker associated with the early-mechanised Penicuik/Valleyfield industry, worked up the pulp and reported failure. The exact apparatus of Cowan's trial remains unproven, but the evidential burden has shifted: metropolitan failure occurred despite access to serious papermaking expertise. The missing historical variable was not simply knowledge of how to make paper in general; it was the particular production history that made Himalayan bark into a successful Nepali sheet.
