# Public-web research wave 5 — mixed transport units, institutional custody, canon chronology, and archival reordering

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. No private/archive scans introduced. This wave develops the distinction between transport units, scholarly comparison units, and later archival units.

## 1. Transport units were not disciplinary units

RAS Hodgson series `BHH/11` is especially important because Hodgson's own lists were sometimes explicitly made for **transportation or storing**, not merely for intellectual classification.

### BHH/11/1 — five boxes plus a sixth package

`List of Buddha Books & drawings, & of Statistics of Nepaul` records material left at the Residency in **five boxes**, each individually listed, plus a **sixth package**. Notes indicate the boxes were **repacked in 1844 to take home**.

The title itself already crosses domains that later archives would separate: Buddhist books, drawings, and statistics occupied one transport/storage system.

The same item includes Hodgson's instruction that, if he died, his literary and scientific stores were to go to his father, who should dispose of them to the advantage of Hodgson's reputation. Packing/storage therefore intersected directly with posthumous reputation management.

Source:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=673&levels=241&media=print&places=618&sf_culture=en&sort=endDate&sortDir=desc&subjects=643&topLod=0&view=table

### BHH/11/3 — `Bastas or Bundles in Trunk No. 1 Cathmandu`

A trunk inventory mixes materials on Buddhism, chronicles / vaṃśāvalīs, Jātamālās, army details, catalogues, and śāstras. These materials were **repacked in 1844** and placed aboard the *Hardwicke* with Hodgson.

### BHH/11/2 — originals and translations travelling together

Another list records vocabulary, geography and ethnography materials and notes that items marked X had been translated; the **original and translation** were together with Hodgson aboard the *Hardwicke* on 9 Feb. 1844.

Analytic implication:

> `box / package / basta / trunk` was an operative nineteenth-century object boundary that often cut across later disciplinary categories.

A key research scale should therefore be: **what was packed together before institutions separated it?**

## 2. Hodgson's return route gives a concrete shipment chronology

Letters to Hodgson's father state that he intended to descend the Ganges, reached Calcutta in Jan. 1844, and took passage on the *Hardwicke*, due to sail at the beginning of February. A printed JASB letter is dated `On board the Hardwicke, Saugor, Feb. 9, 1844`.

This provides a route anchor for reconstructing the physical movement of the repacked trunks and bundles from Nepal → Calcutta/Saugor → England.

Sources:
RAS Hodgson family correspondence browse pages and JASB 1844 digital copy.

## 3. Paris: donation did not end circulation; custody was redistributed to maximise access

Jean Filliozat's 1941 *Catalogue du fonds sanscrit* gives an important institutional history of the Société Asiatique and Bibliothèque royale collections.

It states that in 1835 the Société Asiatique in Paris received from the Asiatic Society in Calcutta a Tibetan **Kanjur collection**. Burnouf and Julius von Mohl argued that material of this importance should be deposited where **all European scholars could use it**, rather than remaining in a private learned-society library. The Société consequently transferred it to the **Bibliothèque royale**.

The transfer was not one-way charity. In exchange, the Bibliothèque royale gave the Société Asiatique a copy of the monumental *Description de l'Égypte* to forward to Calcutta.

This is an unusually clear example of manuscripts functioning within an economy of:

**custody ↔ access ↔ institutional prestige ↔ exchange**.

Source:
Jean Filliozat, *Catalogue du fonds sanscrit*, fasc. I (Paris, 1941), collection history.

## 4. The 1837 Hodgson copies were commissioned for specific recipients and then institutionally redistributed

Filliozat's historical introduction states that from 1834 Hodgson offered to have Nepalese Buddhist texts copied for the Société Asiatique. Burnouf requested copies at his own expense, at Eugène Jacquet's expense, and at the Société's expense; he also indicated that the Bibliothèque royale might absorb the copies if costs became excessive.

The sequence includes:

- 24 manuscripts donated by Hodgson at the beginning of 1837;
- **64 copies** made in Nepal for the Société Asiatique;
- arrival in Paris on **14 July 1837**;
- most of the 64 copies transferred to the Cabinet des manuscrits in **March 1840**;
- the Société retaining a smaller Nepalese manuscript collection.

This means the “Hodgson manuscripts” were divided by recipient and then divided again by institutional custody after arrival.

## 5. Burnouf deliberately commissioned redundancy to correct copying error

Burnouf's correspondence, as quoted and studied by Guillaume Ducœur, makes clear that rapidly produced manuscript copies could be seriously defective. In July 1837 Burnouf asked for **two or three copies of the same text** because difficult passages could not reliably be understood from a single copy.

This is deliberate, funded redundancy: multiple material witnesses were commissioned as an **error-correction technology**.

The resulting network is especially visible for texts such as *Kāraṇḍavyūha*, for which Hodgson-related witnesses were distributed among Calcutta, London, Oxford and Paris.

Analytic implication:

> manuscript duplication was not simply dissemination. It could be an engineered strategy for detecting and repairing scribal error through collation.

## 6. The Tibetan canon chronology is not settled: 1835 and 1838 refer to different events

Current British Library cataloguing for the Hodgson Narthang Tanjur says it was presented to Hodgson and donated onward in **1838**. Hunter's 1881 catalogue likewise gives 1838.

However, an India Office Library historical study citing Company records states that the complete Tibetan sacred codes were actually **presented in 1835**, not 1838. It cites:

- India Political Consultations, Fort William, 24 Aug. 1835, nos. 48–49;
- Hodgson's description of `three hundred and twenty large printed volumes`;
- Court Book, vol. 146, p. 535, 22 Mar. 1836;
- India and Bengal Despatches, vol. 9, 27 Jul. 1836, pp. 405–409.

The same study says Hunter was mistaken because **1838 was the date on which Hodgson's name was inscribed as donor**, citing Court Book vol. 151, p. 55.

This gives a concrete example of catalogue chronology compressing distinct events:

**physical presentation / institutional decision / donor inscription / later catalogue date**.

Until the original Company records are inspected, the article should preserve this distinction rather than choose the later 1838 date by default.

## 7. Multiple Kanjur/Tanjur sets must be reconstructed separately

Public sources conflict over:

- 320, 327, 334 and 221/223-volume counts;
- Kanjur vs Tanjur;
- sets sent to the East India Company / India Office vs Fort William / Asiatic Society;
- whether particular sets were gifts, procured sets, or commissioned copies;
- whether the relevant authority was the Panchen Lama, “Grand Lama,” Lhasa, or another intermediary;
- 1835 vs 1838 acquisition/donation dates.

Do not write a single compressed narrative such as “the Panchen Lama gave Hodgson the Tibetan canon in 1838.” Each physical set needs its own provenance chain.

## 8. Natural-history shipments show the opposite condition: dispatch without receipt

RAS/ZSL records describe a 1837 Hodgson shipment of:

- 26 sheets of mammals and birds;
- **eight boxes** sent via Captain Robinson.

A subsequent RAS minute records that **none of these articles had been received**.

This pairs well with Burnouf's Paris correspondence explicitly certifying the safe arrival of three manuscript cases.

The project can therefore compare:

- manuscript: `dispatch → safe arrival → acknowledgement → collation/use`;
- zoology: `dispatch → non-arrival / custody uncertainty → numbered catalogue as recovery clue`.

## 9. ZSL packing units were themselves inventoried

A Hodgson/ZSL item is explicitly titled:

`List of boxes, crates etc of skins, horns, skulls, osteological specimens of birds and mammals sent to England`.

Another Gray letter says duplicate specimens had been **packed together into boxes** and asks Hodgson how they should be sent.

The exact digitised content of the box/crate list has not yet been retrieved, so box numbers, fillers, weights or detailed contents should **not** be inferred.

## 10. Later archival processing could destroy the very relations the project is trying to reconstruct

Ramesh K. Dhungel's report on the Hodgson cataloguing project in the British Library and RAS says that the provenance/order of the collection was seriously distorted after acquisition.

His key finding is that the India Office's permanent binding / `re-classification` was carried out substantially **according to physical size**, not according to language, provenance or original grouping. Dhungel argues that this operation damaged the manuscript order even more severely than the dispersion and admixture of materials between 1864 and 1919.

This is extremely important methodologically. The modern archive is not a transparent map of Hodgson's working or transport archive.

At least three competing material orders now need to be reconstructed:

1. **transport/storage order** — box, package, basta, trunk, crate;
2. **scholarly comparison order** — text witness / specimen series / catalogue number / copy relation;
3. **institutional archival order** — size-based binding, thematic or linguistic classification, modern shelfmark and conservation container.

Source:
Ramesh K. Dhungel, `Opening the Chest of Nepal's History: The Survey of B. H. Hodgson's Manuscripts in the British Library and the Royal Asiatic Society, London`, SAALG Newsletter.

## 11. One emerging article-scale claim

The strongest formulation after this wave is:

> Hodgson's heterogeneous materials did not travel as disciplines. They travelled as boxes, bundles, copies, skins, leaves, drawings and lists. Disciplinary identities were repeatedly made and remade downstream through receipt, separation, collation, sorting, cataloguing, binding and institutional custody.

This is stronger than saying that natural history and Buddhist Studies simply shared “paper technologies.” The historical question becomes how **material object boundaries preceded, crossed, and were later overwritten by disciplinary boundaries**.
