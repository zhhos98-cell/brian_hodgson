# Web research 2026-09-01 — Wave 46
## Problem genealogy versus textual stemma: 1825 military memorandum to 1833 classificatory paper

Date: 2026-09-01

Status: source-control follow-up to Wave 45. This wave tests a secondary-literature claim that Hodgson's 1833 `Origin and Classification of the Military Tribes of Nepal` began as an 1825 administrative memorandum.

## Executive result

The evidence supports continuity of the **administrative problem**, but does not yet prove direct textual descent from the 1825 memorandum to the 1833 classificatory paper.

The necessary distinction is:

`problem genealogy != textual stemma`.

### Problem genealogy

From 1825 onward Hodgson repeatedly addressed:

- the Nepal army;
- military manpower;
- British assessment of Nepalese soldiers;
- the possibility/desirability of recruiting them into British service.

The 1833 article preserves that recruitment problem while adding a developed ethnological/classificatory apparatus.

### Textual/documentary stemma

The first currently identified surviving paper state that explicitly aggregates the relevant `thar`, Magar-origin and Gurung-clan material is the 1831 rough working in vol.7/20, followed by the October 1832 fair tabular state in vol.6/1.

Therefore:

`1825 administrative problem family`
→ `1831 classificatory source pool`
→ `1832 fair tabular paper`
→ `1833 learned-society print`

is a defensible **problem-to-documentary-recoding sequence**.

But:

`1825 memo -> direct draft -> 1833 article`

is not yet proven.

Mechanisms:

- `PROBLEM_GENEALOGY_NOT_TEXTUAL_STEMMA`
- `ADMINISTRATIVE_PROBLEM_TO_CLASSIFICATORY_RECODING`
- `POLICY_CONTINUITY_ACROSS_DOCUMENTARY_STATES`
- `TEXTUAL_DESCENT_REQUIRES_CONTENT_LEVEL_CONTROL`

## 1. February 1825 — formal administrative memorandum

MSS EUR HODGSON/10 item 1:

`Memoir on the Army of Nepal`, February 1825.

Cambridge describes it as:

- machine-made paper;
- pre-bound notebook;
- English;
- memorandum submitted to Government when Hodgson was Secretary/Assistant Resident;
- 39 headings;
- substantially based on William Bayley's queries;
- includes discussion of the importance of absorbing Nepal's military manpower into British service in India.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol010.html

Documentary operation:

`administrative military assessment / recruitment memorandum`.

It is not catalogued as an ethnic/thar classification.

## 2. 1825 rough administrative state survives separately

MSS EUR HODGSON/9 item 23:

`Rough draft of the report related to the Nepal Army in 1825 by B. Hodgson`.

Cambridge describes it as:

- machine-made paper;
- Hodgson rough writing;
- corrections/addenda including pencil;
- English;
- explicitly part of the Nepal-army manuscript cluster.

This strongly indicates a rough/fair administrative version family around the 1825 military report.

However, the catalogue does not state that this rough report contains the later detailed Khasa/Magar/Gurung classificatory table.

Mechanism:

`ADMINISTRATIVE_REPORT_HAS_OWN_ROUGH_FAIR_VERSION_FAMILY`.

## 3. 1828 — military memoir / Bayley-query reworking

MSS EUR HODGSON/9 item 22:

`Military memoir of Hodgson with answers related to Mr Bayley's queries`, April 1828.

- machine-made paper;
- Hodgson's very rough writing;
- English;
- military memoir and answers to Bayley queries;
- unpublished.

This demonstrates that the 1825 administrative problem remained an active working object after the original memorandum.

Again, it should not be silently converted into a direct draft of the 1833 ethnological article.

Mechanism:

`ADMINISTRATIVE_PROBLEM_PERSISTS_THROUGH_REWORKING`.

## 4. 1831 — first currently identified explicit classificatory source pool

MSS EUR HODGSON/7 item 20:

- machine-made paper;
- Hodgson rough pen/pencil working;
- Khasa thars;
- Brahman family names;
- Magar family names and Bara-Magarant original-home note;
- Gurung clans;
- title `Castes of Parbattiah army tribes`.

This is materially and epistemically different from the 1825 administrative memoir.

The military problem has now been recoded through:

- caste/clan names;
- descent and subdivision;
- geographical origins;
- comparative classification.

Mechanism:

`MILITARY_MANPOWER_PROBLEM_RECODED_AS_ETHNOLOGICAL_CLASSIFICATION`.

## 5. October 1832 — fair classificatory paper

MSS EUR HODGSON/6 item 1:

- same final article title;
- machine-made paper;
- fair tabular writing;
- Brahman/Khasa/Magar/Gurung family-name classification;
- dated October 1832.

Oldfield independently identifies a Hodgson paper dated October 1832 as the source of his later condensed military-tribe table.

This is the strongest surviving publication-facing paper state.

## 6. 1833 print still carries the earlier recruitment problem

The published 1833 article is not merely a detached caste taxonomy.

After its historical/classificatory discussion Hodgson estimates the available military population, considers whether British service could obtain a large body of these men, and evaluates their suitability as soldiers.

Thus the policy/recruitment question visible in the 1825 memorandum remains active in the learned-society article.

The major transformation is that the problem is now supported by an ethnological genealogy and a detailed classificatory table.

This is better represented as:

`administrative manpower question`
→ `ethnological source collection`
→ `tabular classification`
→ `learned-society argument for recruitment`.

Mechanism:

`POLICY_PROBLEM_REPRESENTED_THROUGH_CLASSIFICATORY_INFRASTRUCTURE`.

---

## 7. Paper-centred significance

The sequence is useful because the broad manuscript support class remains machine-made while **genre, audience and epistemic operation** shift:

- pre-bound administrative memorandum;
- rough military draft;
- rough ethnological/classificatory notes;
- fair table;
- learned-society print.

Paper type therefore does not map one-to-one onto institutional genre.

The same broad stock class can support a long documentary transformation in which an administrative problem is made newly legible as scientific/ethnological classification.

Mechanisms:

- `VERSIONING_WITHIN_CONSTANT_SUPPORT_CLASS`
- `GENRE_CHANGE_WITHOUT_SUPPORT_CLASS_CHANGE`
- `ADMINISTRATIVE_TO_SCIENTIFIC_DOCUMENTARY_RECODING`.

---

## 8. Why the distinction matters historiographically

A statement such as `the 1833 essay began as an 1825 administrative memo` may be useful at the level of intellectual/problem genealogy, but it overstates the currently demonstrated textual stemma unless direct comparison is supplied.

The archive allows a more exact claim:

**Hodgson's interest in military recruitment was already formalized in an administrative memorandum in 1825. By 1831–32 that problem had acquired a distinct classificatory paper apparatus organized around thars, ethnic groups, origins and tabulation. The 1833 article preserved the recruitment problem while publishing this newer classificatory infrastructure.**

This formulation keeps continuity and transformation analytically separate.

## Source-control rules

1. Do not use thematic continuity to assert direct copy descent.
2. Keep vol.10/1 and vol.9/23 in an `administrative military memoir` family unless text comparison shows otherwise.
3. Keep vol.7/20 as the first currently identified explicit classificatory source-pool state, not necessarily the first state that ever existed.
4. The modern secondary claim of an 1825 origin is evidence for a historiographical lead, not primary proof of a manuscript stemma.
5. Compare the actual texts before using language such as `edited from the 1825 memorandum`.

## Bottom line

The 1825 military memorandum is securely an ancestor of the **problem** that the 1833 article still addresses, but it is not yet proven to be the direct textual ancestor of the military-tribe classification. The archive instead shows a transition from administrative military assessment to ethnological/tabular recoding. This distinction strengthens the paper argument by showing how a persistent policy problem was materially remade across successive paper objects without requiring a change in broad support class.
