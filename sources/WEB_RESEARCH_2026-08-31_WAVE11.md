# Public-web research wave 11 — inventory reconciliation, catalogue synchronization, numbering failure, and replacement loops

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. This wave follows the receipt/auditability branch opened in Wave 10. No new private/archive scans were added.

## 1. The British Museum arrangement explicitly joined duplicate distribution to specimen lists

J. Forshall, Secretary to the Trustees of the British Museum, wrote to Hodgson on 23 December 1844 after the Trustees considered his proposals. The Museum would:

- receive the whole of Hodgson's remaining specimens and finished drawings;
- select duplicate specimens not required for the Museum;
- distribute duplicates to institutions named by Hodgson;
- **attach in each case a list of the specimens**;
- investigate detained/missing drawings;
- prepare a list or short catalogue of the specimens and drawings presented;
- transmit a copy of the catalogue to Hodgson in Nepal.

ZSL catalogue/transcription, `NZSL/HOD/5/4/6`:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=8774&places=61164&showAdvanced=1&sort=endDate&sortDir=asc&topLod=0

### Analytic consequence

The list was not merely a retrospective institutional finding aid. It was designed to **travel with the duplicate set**. Distribution therefore produced a distributed collection plus a distributed metadata layer.

This is a much stronger form of the auditability claim:

**specimen set + attached list** was the intended transferable unit.

The catalogue/list was a synchronization technology for objects that would no longer share one physical repository.

## 2. Gray's own pledge makes catalogue distribution even more explicit

A John Edward Gray memorandum says that Gray undertook to:

1. publish the zoological novelties;
2. prepare within roughly three months a **corrected Catalogue of the whole**;
3. have that catalogue **distributed with the specimens**;
4. send a copy to Hodgson by overland route via the Secretary of the Asiatic Society of Bengal;
5. work toward a publication combining illustrations from drawings with text from skins and Hodgson's notes.

ZSL `NZSL/HOD/5/4/35`:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&onlyMedia=1&places=53656&sf_culture=nl&showAdvanced=1&sort=relevance&sortDir=asc&topLod=0

A related Gray letter, annotated by Hodgson as `1844 Gray's pledges and report to the Trustees`, states on the outside that the catalogue was to be distributed with each set of duplicates forwarded to Paris, Leiden, India House, Canterbury, Newcastle, Plymouth, Manchester, Liverpool, Edinburgh and Dublin.

ZSL `NZSL/HOD/5/2/13`:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/letter-from-j-e-gray-of-the-british-museum-to-brian-houghton-hodgson-3?sf_culture=en

This turns catalogue production into part of the **distribution contract** rather than merely a later bibliographic product.

## 3. Inventory reconciliation immediately exposed exceptions that the list could not represent automatically

Gray's letter of 6 February 1845 (`NZSL/HOD/5/2/15`) records several concrete reconciliation problems while the collection was being sorted:

- a Tibetan pheasant had not been marked because it was at the **stuffer's** when the list was prepared, so it `escaped` the list;
- no specimens of the Ganges Pelican had been received, only a **head**;
- besides the birds distributed according to the list, numerous specimens were in such bad condition that they were considered useless for distribution;
- the catalogue then being prepared would contain a detailed list of **every specimen received from Hodgson between 1840 and the present**;
- Gray sent Hodgson a list showing how mammals and their bones had been distributed;
- he asked for the bird list back temporarily so the later bird distribution could be added to it;
- fifty bird drawings had been found at the Zoological Society, which was waiting for Hodgson's instructions before delivering them.

ZSL transcription:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=13988&sf_culture=en&showAdvanced=1&sort=startDate&sortDir=asc&topLod=0

### Mechanism

The list is not a static truth about the collection. It is a **reconciliation surface** repeatedly amended when objects are absent, temporarily elsewhere, represented only by parts, too damaged for intended use, or discovered in another institution.

This yields a sequence:

**expected object → physical sort → exception detected → list amended / object status qualified → distribution decision**.

## 4. Hodgson's own material had already been diminished by accidents in transit

In an undated address to the British Museum Trustees (`NZSL/HOD/5/4/33`), Hodgson says that he had brought home further supplies of specimens and drawings as well as memoranda and notes, **`diminished unhappily by accidents on the way`**.

The same document frames the whole as a `large mass of materials` and asks the Museum to help dispose and use it through distribution and publication.

ZSL catalogue/transcription:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=13988&places=53595&sf_culture=en&sort=alphabetic&sortDir=asc&subjects=9613&topLod=0

This is direct evidence that transmission could reduce the evidential apparatus before institutional receipt. `Safe arrival` of a later package therefore cannot retrospectively stand for perfect survival across the whole transport history.

## 5. Numbering itself failed during sorting

An undated Gray letter to Hodgson (`NZSL/HOD/5/2/27`) is exceptionally strong evidence for numerical identity failure.

Gray says:

- the lists sent were **correct in the numbers** but the names were `ludicrously wrong`, which he attributed to the ignorance of the copyist;
- the names could be corrected after Hodgson returned the lists;
- new species could not be described until the **whole collection was sorted**, so old and new specimens could be compared;
- the mammal skins, heads/horns and other materials had already been divided among numbered institutional collections;
- some imperfect skeletons were **not yet unpacked**;
- the bird names were nearly finished;
- Gray feared **many errors in the numbering**, including `two very differing kinds having the same number`.

ZSL transcription:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=13988&places=53595&sf_culture=en&sort=alphabetic&sortDir=asc&subjects=9613&topLod=0

### Analytic consequence

Numbers are an auditability device, but they are not intrinsically stable identifiers. The system fails through at least three routes visible here:

1. **number/name divergence** — the number survives while the copied name is wrong;
2. **number collision** — two distinct kinds share one number;
3. **unavailable object state** — material remains packed or elsewhere and cannot yet be compared against the number/list.

This is a better formulation than saying simply that `labels can be lost`.

## 6. Sorting was epistemically constitutive because novelty depended on comparison of the whole collection

Gray says explicitly that it was impossible to describe the new species until the **whole collection was sorted**, so that `the new and old might be compared`.

This makes institutional sorting part of taxonomic knowledge production. Receipt does not deliver a pre-stabilised set of species. Species status depends on a post-arrival operation of:

**unpacking → sorting → comparison → correction of names/numbers → distinction of new/old → catalogue/description**.

The museum is therefore not merely the endpoint of field collection. It is a transformation site where the epistemic identity of specimens is remade.

## 7. Missing drawings produced an active recovery operation across institutions and private hands

A Hodgson communication associated with the British Museum negotiations records that he had recovered and delivered **sixty-five missing drawings**, while further drawings were expected from:

- Royal Asiatic Society;
- Zoological Society;
- Sir Alexander Johnston;
- H. H. Wilson;
- Frank Howard, who possessed a complete set of mammal drawings and whose retention Hodgson disputed.

ZSL catalogue/transcription around `NZSL/HOD/5/4/18` and adjacent items:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?levels=237&names=50487&onlyMedia=1&showAdvanced=1&sort=identifier&sortDir=asc&topLod=0&view=table

The dispersed drawing corpus therefore required **recovery and institutional recollection** before it could function as a complete evidential set.

This is closely comparable to reconstructing a distributed specimen from labels/numbers: the `collection` was partly an intended relation that had to be materially reassembled.

## 8. The 1847 catalogue did not terminate the correction cycle: it generated a replacement loop

On 29 April 1847, writing from Darjeeling after receiving twelve copies of the General Catalogue of Nepal mammals and birds, Hodgson explicitly described it as founded on his catalogues and **corrected as to synonyms by Gray**.

He then promised to transmit `fresh and superior samples` of specimens and drawings that were still **`defective or missing`**, in order to make the collection `quite complete`.

He also instructed the Museum to distribute remaining catalogue copies after one had gone to each public institution that had received duplicate specimens.

ZSL `NZSL/HOD/5/2/21`:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?levels=237&names=13988&onlyMedia=1&showAdvanced=1&sort=identifier&sortDir=asc&topLod=0&view=table

### Mechanism

The catalogue did not simply record a completed collection. It helped reveal and define incompleteness, which then triggered further collecting/replacement:

**catalogue → identify defective/missing states → procure/send superior replacements → revise collection**.

This is a recursive audit loop.

## 9. Catalogue versions themselves drifted and became objects of disagreement

A later Hodgson letter preserved by ZSL says an earlier catalogue was frequently cited by European writers, but he had run out of copies. He proposed republication because it provided a convenient one-view account of Nepalese species **`notwithstanding its errors`**, and he had marginally marked points where he believed Gray had **unwisely deviated from his own allocation of new types**.

ZSL browse transcription:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=8774&sf_culture=en&showAdvanced=1&sort=startDate&sortDir=asc&topLod=0

This adds a version-control problem. A widely distributed catalogue could coordinate dispersed collections while simultaneously stabilising errors or taxonomic disagreements across the network.

The catalogue is therefore both:

- **error-control infrastructure**, and
- a possible **error-propagation infrastructure**.

## 10. Modern taxonomic historiography confirms that publication date/authorship remained unstable

Dickinson and Walters' 2006 study of the British Museum Hodgson catalogue shows that the work carrying an 1846 imprint was apparently not actually available as a published work until January 1847. They reconstruct authorship as John Edward and George Robert Gray and examine priority conflicts with Hodgson/Blyth publications of 1844–46.

Source:
https://www.aviansystematics.org/uploads/downloads/153/file/ZM80-05_137-154.pdf

This later bibliographic problem mirrors the nineteenth-century material one: catalogue date, availability, authorship, specimen identity and priority were interdependent rather than transparent metadata fields.

Use this secondary study as reconstruction/historiography, not as a substitute for the ZSL correspondence.

## 11. The error-control pipeline can now be stated operationally

Across the 1844–47 British Museum sequence, the project can observe:

1. **dispatch / transfer** of specimens, drawings and notes;
2. **receipt** into institutional custody;
3. **unpacking and sorting**;
4. **inventory reconciliation** against lists;
5. **exception detection** — missing, defective, badly preserved, partial, temporarily elsewhere;
6. **identity correction** — copied names, duplicate numbers, synonymy;
7. **comparison** of old/new forms;
8. **catalogue production**;
9. **duplicate partition and distribution** with attached lists/catalogues;
10. **remote feedback** from Hodgson and other recipients;
11. **replacement / supplementation** of defective or missing material;
12. **republication / marginal correction / renewed disagreement**.

This is much stronger than a linear `field → museum → publication` model.

## 12. Revised proposition: auditability is recursive rather than merely portable

A stronger article proposition after this wave is:

> Hodgson's distributed collections were made auditable through redundant lists, numbers, catalogues, drawings, receipts and duplicate sets, but auditability was achieved only recursively. Sorting exposed wrong names and number collisions; catalogues defined missing or defective states; distributed copies coordinated collections while propagating their own errors; replacement shipments and marginal corrections then attempted to repair the system.

The relevant object is therefore not a stable specimen plus metadata. It is an **iteratively repaired relation among object, number, name, representation, list, catalogue, institution and correspondent**.
