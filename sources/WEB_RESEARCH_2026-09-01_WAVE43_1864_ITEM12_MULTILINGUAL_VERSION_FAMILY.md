# Web research 2026-09-01 — Wave 43
## 1864 Item 12 as a probable multilingual version family

Date: 2026-09-01

Status: high-value custody/versioning follow-up to Wave 42. This wave tests whether the exact old address `Box 2 / Item 12` surviving on MSS EUR HODGSON/6 can be connected to the 1864 India Office manifest and whether the manifest's English/Persian/Khas companion structure survives in the modern dispersed archive.

## Executive result

A major probable mapping has emerged.

The 1864 transfer manifest, under:

`Trunk No.2 -> second large bundle -> item 12`

lists:

- three volumes in English;
- classification of the people (`thar`);
- judicial system;
- customs (`Jagat-bhansar`);
- army;
- with some papers in Persian and Khas on the same subjects.

Modern `MSS EUR HODGSON/6` independently preserves an old Nepali address:

`original Box 2 / Item 12`.

Its Hodgson-era English title is:

`Tribes of people, judicial system, trade, army, topography`.

Its modern contents include:

- classification of military tribes;
- judicial system/law;
- trade/commerce;
- army;
- topography;
- additional paper-making material.

Thus the identification is supported simultaneously by:

1. old container number `2` candidate;
2. exact old item number `12`;
3. nearly identical subject vocabulary;
4. English copy/report documentary state;
5. surviving Hodgson title layer.

This is now best coded as:

`A_NEAR_CERTAIN_CANDIDATE`.

It is not yet labelled proven because the hierarchy requires reconciliation:

`Cambridge: Box 2 / Item 12`

versus

`1864 manifest: Trunk 2 / second large bundle / item 12`.

Event-near RAS list states must be compared directly before those terms are collapsed.

Mechanisms:

- `HISTORICAL_ADDRESS_TO_TRANSFER_MANIFEST_CROSSWALK`
- `NUMBER_PLUS_CONTENT_MATCH_EXCEEDS_THEMATIC_COINCIDENCE`
- `TRANSFER_ITEM_SURVIVES_AS_MODERN_DISTRIBUTED_VERSION_FAMILY`.

## 1. The exact 1864 item 12

Hunter's reproduction of the August 1864 India Office list gives, within Trunk No.2's second large bundle, item 12 as:

- three English volumes;
- classification of people (`thar`);
- judicial system;
- customs (`Jagat-bhansar`);
- army;
- Persian and Khas papers on the same subjects.

Source:
https://dspace.gipe.ac.in/xmlui/bitstream/handle/10973/24484/GIPE-000910-Contents.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=2

This is a particularly useful historical transfer unit because it already declares multilingual equivalence/family membership.

Its ontology is not:

`English papers here / Persian papers elsewhere / Khas papers elsewhere`.

It is:

`shared subject/version family with multilingual states`.

Mechanism:

`MULTILINGUAL_VERSION_FAMILY_EXPLICIT_IN_MANIFEST`.

## 2. vol.6 supplies a matching old address and matching content

Cambridge `MSS EUR HODGSON/6` states that the Nepali title layer mentions:

`original box and Item number (2 and 12 respectively)`.

The volume is composed mainly of English machine-paper copies, most published.

Its title and contents map strongly onto manifest item 12.

### Manifest item 12

`classification of the people (thar)`

### vol.6

`Origin and classification of the Military tribes of Nepal`

and old title `Tribes of people`.

### Manifest item 12

`Judicial System`

### vol.6

law/legal practice plus a long `Judicial system and police of Nepal` copy.

### Manifest item 12

`Customs (Jagat-bhansar)`

### vol.6

large commerce/trade dossier including imports, exports, merchants and trading capital. This is not identical wording and must remain a partial rather than perfect match.

### Manifest item 12

`Army`

### vol.6

large army survey and military classification material.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol006.html

The agreement is therefore too strong to treat as generic subject similarity.

The active hypothesis is:

**MSS EUR HODGSON/6 preserves at least part of, or descends directly from, the English-volume portion of 1864 Trunk 2 / second large bundle / item 12.**

Source-control remains necessary because `Box` and `Trunk/large bundle` terminology is not yet diplomatically reconciled.

## 4. vol.11 may preserve companion English-volume material

Modern `MSS EUR HODGSON/11` consists of six separate pre-bound machine-paper English notebooks covering:

- courts/judicial system;
- revenue and army distribution;
- Tarai revenue;
- institutes/law codes;
- public offices/revenue;
- army.

Several retain Nepali title or bundle descriptions on their covers.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol011.html

This resembles the broad content of the 1864 English-volume family, but no exact `Box 2 / Item 12` label is currently transcribed.

Therefore vol.11 is coded only:

`B_PROBABLE_PARTIAL_DESCENDANT_OR_COMPANION`.

The 1864 statement `three volumes in English` must **not** be equated mechanically with three modern 1921 volume numbers.

The 1921 operation could have split, merged or incorporated earlier pre-bound units according to dimensions.

## 5. The Persian companion ecology survives with striking semantic alignment

Modern vol.101 contains a particularly suggestive cluster.

### vol.101 item 4

- Farsi;
- machine-made paper;
- `Gorkhali courts of Nepal`.

Manifest category:

`Judicial System`.

### vol.101 item 8

- Farsi;
- machine-made paper;
- principal family names (`thar`) of the Khasas.

Manifest category:

`Classification of the People (thar)`.

The Cambridge cross-references connect this family-name material to English and Nepali states including vol.9/10 and vol.56/2; vol.9/10 itself directly cross-references vol.6/1.

### vol.101 item 9

- Farsi;
- machine-made paper;
- custom duties on traded commodities.

Manifest category:

`Customs (Jagat-bhansar)`.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol101.html

Thus three of the four subject labels named in manifest item 12 survive today as Farsi paper states inside modern vol.101.

This is highly significant.

It does **not** prove that these three exact sheets were the Persian papers mentioned in 1864 item 12, because no old item-12 label is yet transcribed on them.

But it greatly strengthens the interpretation that the manifest's item 12 describes a real multilingual documentary ecology now dispersed across modern volumes.

Mechanism:

`SEMANTIC_RECURRENCE_ACROSS_DECLARED_MULTILINGUAL_VERSION_FAMILY`.

## 6. Army companion survives in Persian-script/Urdu material, but language labels must not be normalized

Modern `MSS EUR HODGSON/103` contains Urdu army material, including:

- item 8: salaries of army and civil officials, machine-made paper;
- item 12: salaries paid to soldiers and officials, Nepali handmade paper;
- item 14: salaries and grants to army personnel, Nepali handmade paper.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol103.html

These are obvious candidates for the army side of the multilingual family.

However, the 1864 manifest says `Persian`, while the modern catalogue distinguishes these particular records as `Urdu`.

Do **not** silently normalize the terms.

Possible explanations include:

- different surviving records;
- historical language-label looseness;
- Persian-script classification differences;
- modern cataloguing refinement;
- missing Farsi army companions elsewhere.

This is a falsification target, not a gap to smooth over.

Mechanism:

`HISTORICAL_AND_MODERN_LANGUAGE_LABELS_REQUIRE_OBJECT_CONTROL`.

## 8. The probable historical topology

The evidence now supports the following reconstruction as a **working topology**, not a final stemma:

`1864 Trunk 2 / second large bundle / item 12`

contains a multilingual family around:

- classification/thar;
- judicial system;
- customs;
- army.

Modern dispersed descendants/candidates include:

### English

- vol.6 — strongest exact-address candidate;
- vol.11 — probable companion/descendant;
- vol.9 and vol.7 — likely scattered rough/duplicate states for some topics.

### Farsi

- vol.101/4 — judicial;
- vol.101/8 — thar;
- vol.101/9 — customs.

### Khas/Nepali

- vol.56/2 and /14 — classification;
- vol.60/7 — judicial;
- other local army/customs states remain to map.

### Urdu/Persian-script army candidates

- vol.103/8, /12, /14.

The historical family is therefore potentially distributed across at least:

`6 + 7 + 9 + 11 + 56 + 60 + 101 + 103`.

Do not interpret that list as proof that every named item belonged to item 12. It is a ranked candidate graph.

## 9. This is a strong test of the 1921 rebinding argument

The 2011 Cambridge introduction explicitly states that in 1921:

- papers were bound into 95 volumes;
- physical dimensions partly governed arrangement;
- related originals and translations could be separated simply because their paper sizes differed.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/intro.html

Item 12 offers a concrete historical family against which to test that claim.

If the English, Farsi and Khas states named together in 1864 are now distributed across volumes 6, 56, 60, 101 and beyond, then the historical move becomes observable:

`multilingual transfer family`
→ `dimension/material reclassification`
→ `modern multilingual dispersion`.

Paper size would then be not merely a preservation feature but an active variable in breaking a known archival family.

## 10. Strong article-facing formulation

**One surviving volume may preserve the old address of a multilingual transfer family. MSS EUR HODGSON/6 bears the Nepali archival address Box 2, Item 12 and contains English papers on tribes, judicial administration, trade and the army. The 1864 India Office manifest independently lists, under Trunk No.2's second large bundle, item 12: three English volumes on classification, judicial administration, customs and the army, together with Persian and Khas papers on the same subjects. Modern Farsi manuscripts preserve precisely courts, thar classification and customs in vol.101, while related Khas/Nepali and Urdu states are dispersed elsewhere. The match is not yet diplomatically complete, but it makes visible a historically multilingual paper family whose members were later redistributed by a different archival ontology.**

## 11. Falsification path

The hypothesis can now be killed or confirmed by a short sequence of physical checks.

1. Read `RAS BHH/11/14` item 12.
2. Read annotated printed `RAS BHH/19/5` item 12.
3. Inspect vol.6 old leather/title layer and determine the exact wording/context of `Box 2 / Item 12`.
4. Inspect vol.101/4, /8, /9 for old bundle/item marks.
5. Inspect vol.10 and vol.11 covers for addresses that correspond to manifest items 11/12.
6. Compare handwriting and titles of Khas/Nepali companions.
7. Measure the candidate papers to test whether 1921 size sorting explains current distribution.

If RAS item 12 wording differs substantially or vol.6's Box 2 belongs demonstrably to an earlier unrelated numbering system, downgrade the mapping.

If RAS item 12 matches and companion papers bear consistent old marks, promote it to direct historical crosswalk.

## Source-control rules

1. `Box 2 / Item 12` and `Trunk 2 / second large bundle / item 12` are not yet declared identical hierarchies.
2. Hunter 1896 is a downstream reproduction; RAS BHH/11/14 and BHH/19/5 outrank it when directly inspected.
3. Three historical English volumes are not assumed to equal three modern volume numbers.
4. Farsi and Urdu labels are preserved separately.
5. Khas and modern Nepali labels are related historically but not mechanically interchangeable for every object.
6. Modern cross-reference proves relational relevance, not historical co-bundling.
7. Modern adjacency is not old adjacency.

## Bottom line

Wave 43 produces the strongest manifest-to-modern-archive mapping yet. Modern vol.6 carries an exact old Box 2 / Item 12 address and a content profile closely matching 1864 Trunk 2 / second large bundle / item 12. Modern Farsi papers reproduce three of the same named categories—judicial system, thar classification and customs—while Khas/Nepali and Urdu companion states survive elsewhere. This makes the 1864 item 12 family a high-value test case for the article's central archival claim: paper-based documentary versions could be deliberately co-custodied as a multilingual family and later dispersed when physical format became a new principle of archival order.
