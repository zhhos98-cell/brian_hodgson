## 1. Source-genealogy correction: Hunter 1881 precedes Hunter 1896

Wave 14 used Hunter's 1896 *Life of Brian Houghton Hodgson*, Appendix A, as an accessible witness for the 66-work Fort William list. Mitra's 1882 preface shows that the relevant catalogue genealogy begins earlier.

W. W. Hunter published:

**Catalogue of Sanskrit Manuscripts collected in Nepal and presented to various Libraries and Learned Societies by Brian Houghton Hodgson**, Trübner, 1881.

Bibliographic witnesses:

- Open Library: https://openlibrary.org/books/OL45090569M/Catalogue_of_Sanskrit_manuscripts_collected_in_Nepal_and_presented_to_various_libraries_and_learned_
- BnF/Gallica catalogue records the 1881 Trübner edition.
- Hunter's 1896 biography substantially reuses/revises these distribution lists.

Mitra explicitly refers to Hunter having `published, last year, a catalogue` and says the Calcutta list in it had been compiled by Mitra from Asiatic Society office records.

### Consequence

Store distinct states:

- `HUNTER_1881_CATALOGUE`
- `MITRA_1882_REANALYSIS`
- `HUNTER_1896_APPENDIX_REUSE`

Do not treat the 1896 Appendix as the first stabilization of the list.

## 2. Direct quantitative proof that bundle count and work count diverge

Mitra gives a distribution-level accounting of Hodgson's Sanskrit Buddhist copies. He writes that copies totaling **381 bundles** were distributed to make them accessible to European scholars.

For the Asiatic Society of Bengal he gives an explicit conversion:

**85 bundles comprising 144 separate works.**

He then lists other bundle totals for London, India Office, Bodleian, Société Asiatique / Burnouf distributions.

### Why this matters

This is the direct nineteenth-century quantitative evidence needed for Wave 14's `counting ontology` rule.

A `bundle` is demonstrably not a `work`:

`85 bundles -> 144 separate works`.

The ratio itself should not be generalized to other shipments, but the existence of a many-to-many conversion is proven.

Therefore the Fort William `109 bundles` cannot be converted into a work count by simple identity mapping.

## 3. Mitra says the preserved Calcutta list was corrupt

Mitra's most important methodological statement concerns the list of the Calcutta collection before his reanalysis.

He says the preserved list was corrupt in two opposite directions:

1. it sometimes described **the same work under two or three names as different works**;
2. at other times it gave **one name for four, five, or more distinct works**.

This establishes both error types:

- **false splitting**: one textual work -> multiple catalogue identities;
- **false lumping**: multiple textual works -> one catalogue identity.

The catalogue therefore did not simply contain misspellings. It altered the **cardinality of the collection**.

---

## 4. Mitra gives the material mechanism of false lumping

Mitra explains why one catalogue name could cover multiple works.

Two or more different works were sometimes written **continuously in a single volume without any break in pagination**. During cursory examination, only the first and last page of each codex were read to determine the name of the volume.

The resulting operation was effectively:

**physical codex boundary + first-page title evidence + last-page title evidence -> assumed single work identity.**

Where the physical volume contained several continuously copied works, this procedure collapsed them.

### Historical importance

This is an unusually clean material epistemology case because the error is generated by interaction among:

- physical binding/codex unity;
- continuous pagination;
- inspection depth;
- catalogue expectation that physical volume and textual work correspond;
- time/labour economy of cursory inspection.

The mistake is not reducible to ignorance of Buddhist doctrine. It is produced by a **sampling protocol applied to a composite material object**.

---

## 5. The physical object actively biases the catalogue ontology

A bound volume presents strong visual/tactile unity. Continuous pagination reinforces that unity. If the cataloguer reads only boundary pages, the material object encourages a one-volume/one-work assumption.

The historical inference should be formulated cautiously:

> Physical unity could be mistaken for textual unity when catalogue inspection sampled only codex boundaries.

This is a concrete mechanism by which material form shapes later knowledge without requiring any symbolic claim about the paper or binding itself.

---

## 6. Catalogue error changes collection size

Because false splitting and false lumping move in opposite directions, a historical count of `works` depends on the state of identification.

A collection can change numerical identity without acquiring or losing a single physical leaf:

- identify two names as one work -> work count falls;
- discover several works inside one codex -> work count rises;
- rebind/repartition physical volumes -> volume count changes;
- rename titles without physical intervention -> catalogue identity changes.

This gives a stronger version of the counting-ontology claim:

**counts are not only operation-specific; within one unit class (`TEXTUAL_WORK`) they can change through re-identification.**

## 7. Mitra explicitly criticises the Hunter 1881 Calcutta list

Mitra explains that Hunter's 1881 catalogue brought together information on Hodgson manuscripts in several collections. But he states that the Calcutta list, which Mitra himself had compiled from Asiatic Society office records for Hunter, was `not correct` for the reasons just described.

This creates a rare traceable correction chain:

**institutional office records -> Mitra preliminary compilation -> Hunter 1881 printed catalogue -> Mitra 1882 material reanalysis/correction.**

The printed catalogue therefore freezes an intermediate, known-imperfect descriptive state.

### Archive-method consequence

A printed catalogue should be versioned as an event in object identification, not used as timeless metadata.

---

## 8. Hunter 1896 becomes an evidential afterlife, not an independent count

Hunter's later *Life* Appendix A reproduces distribution lists and says of the 66 Fort William works that some titles are uncertain while others were identified/corrected from other manuscript lists.

That statement now has to be read against Mitra 1882:

- Hunter 1881 captured a list before Mitra's full analysis;
- Mitra 1882 diagnosed the structural errors and produced detailed manuscript descriptions;
- Hunter 1896 incorporated later corrections/comparative identifications into a biographical appendix.

Thus the apparent stability of `66 works` is historically downstream of repeated comparison.

## 9. The 1882 catalogue carries current institutional identifiers

Mitra's contents page states:

> The MSS. as now arranged in the Asiatic Society's Library bear the Nos. shown here.

The entries then carry identifiers such as:

- `B. 26` for Abhidhānottara;
- `A. 13` for Abhidharmakośa-vyākhyā;
- multiple shelf identifiers for some textual headings;
- several works associated with one `B.` number family.

The table contains **85 analytical headings**, while Mitra's preface speaks of **85 bundles comprising 144 separate works** for the Society distribution. The exact relation among `A/B shelf identifier`, bundle, codex, and textual-work count needs reconstruction from the descriptive entries rather than inferred from the contents table.

### Important caution

Do not assume `A` and `B` are automatically collection provenance classes until Mitra's own explanation or institutional records establish their meaning.

## 10. Manuscript descriptions move far beyond title-only cataloguing

Mitra's first detailed entry, `No. B.26`, illustrates the descriptive expansion. The catalogue records material and codicological attributes including:

- `yellow Nepalese paper`;
- dimensions;
- folia count;
- lines per page;
- and further textual/descriptive information.

This contrasts sharply with the failed first/last-page title sampling described in the preface.

The reanalysis increases inspection depth and multiplies observable fields.

### Mechanism

**more intensive material inspection -> finer textual segmentation -> corrected identity -> richer catalogue metadata.**

Catalogue correction is therefore an epistemic labour process, not a purely lexical cleanup.

## 11. Comparison with Cowell & Eggeling 1875 at the Royal Asiatic Society

Cowell and Eggeling's 1875 RAS catalogue describes its aim as providing enough brief description for **identification of the works**, while acknowledging that detailed analysis could require comparison with other copies.

Cambridge Core:
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/art-icatalogue-of-buddhist-sanskrit-manuscripts-in-the-possession-of-the-royal-asiatic-society-hodgson-collection/BCDBFD2BBAC2E056B6E489258AE74E2C

The contrast is useful:

- RAS catalogue explicitly calibrates description to identification and admits comparison requirements;
- Calcutta's earlier list suffered cardinality errors from shallow boundary inspection;
- Mitra 1882 performs deeper analysis and corrects the segmentation.

This suggests `inspection protocol` should be a field in the provenance/catalogue census.

## 13. Stronger article-level mechanism: segmentation failure

The article can now move beyond generic `catalogue error` to a named mechanism:

### Segmentation failure

A receiving institution has to decide where one object/work ends and another begins. Material boundaries can help, but they can also mislead.

In Mitra's Calcutta case:

**one bound codex + continuous pagination + shallow boundary sampling -> multiple textual works falsely segmented as one.**

The reverse error also occurred, with one work acquiring multiple names.

This mechanism should be compared later with zoological cases where one specimen identity can split or multiple specimen/label records can be conflated.

## 14. Relation to Fort William 66 / 109 / 127

Mitra does not directly solve the conversion among Fort William's:

- 109 bundles received in May 1827;
- 127 `ponderous` volumes discussed in November 1827;
- 66 works in Hunter's later Fort William list.

He does something more useful: he demonstrates why a one-to-one mapping is historically implausible.

1. Bundle/work mapping can be many-to-many: 85 bundles = 144 works in a documented distribution.
2. Physical volume/work mapping can be one-to-many: several works may be continuously written in one codex.
3. Catalogue work count itself can change when false splits/lumps are corrected.
4. Later acquisition during 1827 means the May and November corpora need not be identical.

Therefore the Fort William counts should remain separate observations until an explicit reconciliation document is found.

## 15. High-value next move

The most valuable remaining comparison is now **Mss Eur Hodgson/12 item 25, ff.179–182 (1827 detailed list) vs Hunter 1881 vs Mitra 1882 vs Hunter 1896**.

For each recoverable title, construct a version chain:

`1827 title/description -> 1881 Hunter title -> 1882 Mitra corrected identity + shelf no. + material description -> 1896 Hunter title`.

Record:

- title string;
- number of leaves;
- number of ślokas;
- 1827 price;
- physical volume relation;
- later shelf identifier;
- correction event;
- whether identity split/merged;
- evidence used for correction.

This could yield a small but unusually powerful microhistory of **how the same manuscripts changed identity without necessarily changing material custody**.

---

## Bottom line

Mitra 1882 provides the missing causal mechanism behind the repo's counting-ontology argument. Hodgson's manuscripts were not merely misnamed. Earlier cataloguing could change the number of perceived works because physical codices and textual works were segmented incorrectly. Several works could run continuously in one volume; cursory first/last-page inspection then collapsed them into one name. Conversely, one work could acquire multiple names. Mitra's own quantitative statement, `85 bundles comprising 144 separate works`, proves that transport aggregation and textual identity operated at different scales. The catalogue is therefore a machine for segmenting material, and its inspection protocol determines what objects the institution thinks it possesses.
