# Public-web research wave 3 — Buddhist manuscript materiality, Kanjur/Tanjur, verification, and packing

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. No new private/archive scans. This wave follows the decision not to subordinate Buddhist material in advance.

## 1. Rajendralala Mitra's Hodgson catalogue is already a material-verification protocol

Rajendralala Mitra, *The Sanskrit Buddhist Literature of Nepal* (Asiatic Society of Bengal, 1882), is a descriptive catalogue of Hodgson manuscripts in Calcutta. Its object descriptions routinely combine:

- `Substance` / writing support;
- paper colour and construction;
- dimensions;
- folio count;
- lines per page;
- extent in ślokas;
- script (`Character`);
- date;
- `Appearance` (old/fresh/decayed etc.);
- prose/verse;
- a judgement of textual correctness (`Generally correct`, `Incorrect`).

This combination matters: material description, dating, codicological measurement and textual reliability were not separated into distinct analytical domains.

### Strong controlled example: Śatasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā witnesses

Mitra describes several volumes/copies side by side:

- A1: yellow Nepalese paper, pasted double; 467 folios; Newari; old; generally correct.
- A2: yellow Nepalese paper, pasted double; 511 folios; Newari; old; generally correct.
- A3: yellow Nepalese paper, pasted double; 499 folios; Newari; old; incorrect.
- a second copy of volume III: yellow Nepalese paper; 623 folios; older date; generally correct.
- A4: country-made yellow paper; 534 folios; Newari; old; incorrect.

Public OCR / image-derived text:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/book/the-sanskrit-buddhist-literature-of-nepal/ocr/1897080/229

This is nearly a ready-made comparison matrix for testing whether support construction, date, copy lineage, scribal practice and correctness covary — but no causal relation should be assumed simply from the catalogue.

### Palm-leaf vs later paper copy of the same ritual text

Mitra's `Kriyāsaṃgraha-pañjikā` entry compares:
- an old palm-leaf witness, 166 folios, judged incorrect;
- a Nepalese yellow-paper witness, 220 folios, appearance `fresh`, also judged incorrect.

Source:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/book/the-sanskrit-buddhist-literature-of-nepal/ocr/1897080/157

This is useful precisely because recopying into a newer support does **not** automatically improve textual reliability.

## 2. Classification failure: Buddhist material provides a direct history-of-science problem

Mitra explicitly says that applying Hodgson's classificatory scheme to the manuscript corpus is `impracticable`:

- manuscript colophons do not consistently name the classes to which texts supposedly belong;
- definitions supplied to Hodgson by his informants are described as `obviously arbitrary`;
- the definitions do not allow Mitra to classify the codices;
- an old classified catalogue would have helped, but Mitra says such a record was unavailable;
- textual subject matter itself frequently crosses proposed divisions.

Public text, p. xl of Mitra:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/book/the-sanskrit-buddhist-literature-of-nepal/ocr/1897080/44

The BL Hodgson inventory and David Gellner's later reconstruction independently identify Hodgson's four `schools` as a misconceived classification likely shaped by the questions Hodgson posed to Amritananda.

BL inventory:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/intro.html
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol028.html

Gellner, `Hodgson's Blind Alley? On the So-called Schools of Nepalese Buddhism`, JIABS 12/1 (1989):
https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/jiabs/article/view/8746

### Why this belongs in the material project

This is not simply a doctrinal mistake. The classification was generated and later challenged through an infrastructure involving:

**questionnaire → informant response → handwritten extracts/notes → named textual witnesses → colophons → comparison of copies → catalogue → revised classification.**

Thus Buddhist Studies supplies an unusually clear case of classificatory claims failing when confronted with the material archive that was supposed to substantiate them.

## 3. Hodgson's questions survive as paper instruments

BL `Mss Eur Hodgson/18`, ff. 115–132, preserves 152 questions Hodgson developed for studying Buddhism, plus another set of 20 questions given to Amritananda. The inventory describes them on different types of machine-made paper with rough pen/pencil hands.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol018.html

This is a strong bridge between `paper technology` and knowledge production: the questionnaire is not just a source about Buddhism but an **elicitation instrument** that structured the answers later treated as doctrinal data.

## 4. The Hodgson Kanjur/Tanjur is now traceable as a specific printing and transport event

British Library current cataloguing identifies Hodgson's Tibetan canon as the **Narthang** Kanjur/Tanjur.

### Tanjur

BL `Mss Tib X-Y`, `Narthang Tanjur (Hodgson)`:
- Tibetan canonical commentaries;
- print blocks originally produced at Narthang monastery in Central Tibet;
- the set was produced on the order of the 7th Panchen Lama in the 1830s;
- presented to Hodgson in 1838;
- subsequently presented by Hodgson to the India Office Library;
- 221 of 223 volumes survive, containing c. 3,900 texts.

Source:
https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-004282613

Hunter's 1881 catalogue reports the combined Kanjur/Tanjur as 334 volumes and says the set was procured from the `Grand Lama` and deposited in the India Office Library.

Hunter:
https://nepalindata.com/media/resources/items/0/b1881_Catalogue_of_Sanskrit_Manuscripts_collected_in_Nepal_by_Brian_Ho_zRBJsD0.pdf

### Kanjur physical description

Individual BL Kanjur records now give unusually useful codicological data. Example `Mss Tib U 17`:
- form: `xyl_pothī`;
- material/support: paper;
- 453 folios;
- leaf dimensions 170 × 620 mm;
- running section name and pagination in Tibetan;
- collation: **loose leaves**;
- condition: fair;
- binding: **unbound folios boxed at BL in 1995**.

Source:
https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-004367994

This allows three historically distinct container/order systems to be separated:
1. Narthang printing-house folio/volume order;
2. nineteenth-century transport/transfer to Hodgson and India Office;
3. twentieth-century BL conservation boxing.

Do not retroject the 1995 boxes into Hodgson's original transport arrangement.

## 5. Modern fibre analysis gives a testable material baseline for Narthang paper

Agnieszka Helman-Ważny's comparative study of Tibetan Kanjur paper found that examined Narthang Kanjur leaves were made of one-layer Tibetan paper based on **Daphne or Edgeworthia** (Thymelaeaceae) fibres; the paper had uneven thickness with outer-bark particles and fibre bundles. She judges the Narthang paper examined to be lower quality than the Lhasa Kanjur samples in the study.

Public article:
https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/mc/files/articles/mc05-articles-helman-wazny.pdf

This is **not** proof about every leaf of Hodgson's own set. It is a modern comparative baseline that makes direct fibre analysis of Hodgson's Narthang volumes potentially meaningful.

The study also records survival of `Original Paper Strings` in a Cone Kanjur, reminding us that book-binding/ordering accessories themselves can survive as material evidence.

## 6. Canon volumes were genuinely heavy transport units

A Columbia University description of a Narthang canon (not Hodgson's set) describes typical volumes of c.475 sheets printed both sides, held between decorated wooden boards and wrapped in coloured cloth. It reports an estimate of c.10 pounds per bundle and roughly a dozen yaks to transport a Kanjur alone.

Source:
https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_6309312_004/ldpd_6309312_004.pdf

This is comparative context only, but it makes a crucial point for Hodgson research: `334 volumes` is a **major freight operation**, not a bibliographic abstraction. We should look for pack-animal, porter, trunk, route, customs and forwarding evidence around the 1838 Hodgson transfer.

## 7. Indigenous book packaging precedes Hodgson's colonial logistics

South Asian / Himalayan pothi and pecha books were already technologies of portable loose leaves.

A Cleveland Museum description of a Nepal-transmitted Prajñāpāramitā manuscript explains:
- wooden covers protected palm leaves;
- cord through holes/peg kept leaves under even pressure;
- the entire book was wrapped in cloth against elements and insects;
- the format was portable.

Source:
https://www.clevelandart.org/print/art/1938.301.a

Recent Tibetan codicology likewise describes loose-leaf books as normally protected by cloth wrapping and covers; without them folios can become disordered or lost.

Comparative source:
https://mtc-journal.org/index.php/mtc/article/download/48/76/443

Analytic implication: colonial `packing` should not be narrated as if it invented portability. Hodgson's boxes/crates/trunks interacted with pre-existing **cover–cord–wrapper–bundle** technologies. A central question is where one packaging regime was nested inside another.

## 8. Filling/padding: direct Hodgson evidence still absent, but contemporary natural-history practice confirms the mechanism

Direct Hodgson evidence so far gives wax cloth, thread/twine, binding, bundling, packaging, crates/boxes/trunks, carpenters and porters — but not yet an explicit line naming cushioning/filling material.

However, William Swainson's 1822 *The Naturalist's Guide for Collecting and Preserving Subjects of Natural History and Botany* provides very close contemporary context. His directions specify that bird skins should be:
- individually wrapped in paper;
- packed in boxes;
- spaces filled with **cotton or another soft substance**;
- seams closed with tow and pitch or pasted over with strong paper.

For shells he recommends alternating specimens and cotton, filling interstices so objects cannot touch, with delicate specimens additionally wrapped in soft paper.

Darwin Online transcription of Swainson 1822:
https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/Ancillary/BeagleLibrary/1822_Swainson_A855.html

This confirms that `filling voids` was an explicit early nineteenth-century preservation principle in natural-history transport. It does **not** prove Hodgson used cotton/tow in his own boxes; that must still be found in his bills/correspondence.

## 9. Packing preserves morphology and therefore epistemic value

Swainson makes the epistemic stakes explicit. Bad pressure can deform a bird's skull; specimens are valuable only when fragile diagnostic parts survive; each skin is wrapped and immobilised so shaking does not make specimens injure each other.

Thus packing is not merely logistical cost. It protects the morphological characters on which later identification depends.

This gives a strong comparison with manuscripts:

- zoological packing preserves shape / parts / labels;
- manuscript wrappers, covers and bundles preserve folio order / title identity / completeness;
- in both cases transport damage can alter what downstream scholars are able to know.

## 10. `Authentication` should remain broader than forgery

A targeted web search has still not produced a strong Hodgson-specific nineteenth-century case of a deliberately forged Buddhist manuscript.

What **is** strongly documented is:
- incorrect vs generally correct copies;
- later supplied leaves;
- old vs modern hands;
- wrapper titles that can misidentify a codex;
- conflicting textual witnesses;
- colophons that fail to support Hodgson's proposed classifications;
- a classificatory system later judged to have been elicited through leading questions;
- Hodgson's own rhetoric of `proof` and `original Sanskrit`.

Accordingly the strongest current category is **verification / authentication / evidentiary reliability**, with deliberate forgery retained as a targeted question rather than assumed finding.

## 11. A potentially major argument: preservation technologies and classification failure meet

The Buddhist corpus now supplies two different kinds of instability:

### Physical instability
Loose folios can be lost, disordered, worm-eaten, smudged, repacked, supplied by later hands, retitled by wrappers, rebound or boxed by institutions.

### Conceptual instability
The same archive can resist the classificatory schema imposed on it: texts do not map neatly to Hodgson's proposed schools, colophons do not validate the categories, and later scholars must compare witnesses and reorganise the corpus.

A strong formulation to test:

> Nineteenth-century Himalayan knowledge required technologies for stabilising both **objects** and **categories**. Hodgson's archive reveals that these two forms of stabilization were linked but could fail independently: a manuscript could survive transport while its title or category remained wrong, just as a zoological skin could arrive safely while its number or copied name was corrupted.
