# Web research 2026-09-01 — Wave 40
## Old container metadata to modern shelfmark crosswalk

Date: 2026-09-01

Status: paper-centred custody reconstruction following Waves 36–39. This wave moves from the general observation that pre-1921 container metadata survives to the first direct historical-container → modern-volume crosswalk.

## Executive result

The first explicit cross-volume relation is now secure:

`original shipment sack No. 8`

is represented today by at least two physically separated modern archival states:

1. `MSS EUR HODGSON/58 item 18, f.65` — the surviving contents list of sack No.8;
2. `MSS EUR HODGSON/85 item 4, ff.95–108` — a Limbu manuscript whose Nepali title explicitly preserves `original sack number (8)`.

This is direct evidence that an old custody relation survived as paper metadata after the original container had disappeared and after later rebinding had distributed its components across modern volumes.

## 1. Sack No.8: control leaf and surviving member now sit in different modern volumes

Cambridge describes `vol.58 item 18, f.65` as:

- Nepali hand-made paper;
- Nepali;
- `Content of manuscripts from sack No. 8 of the original shipment`;
- a list of manuscripts relating to the customs and manners of different ethnic communities.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol058.html

Cambridge independently describes `vol.85 item 4, ff.95–108` as a Limbu text on traditional beliefs, agricultural cultivation, harvesting and marriage. Its title description in Nepali explicitly preserves:

`original sack number (8)`.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol085.html

The relation therefore does not depend on thematic resemblance alone.

It is keyed by the same historical container number.

Current state:

`old sack 8`
→ `control list now in vol.58`
+
`member manuscript now in vol.85`.

This is the first direct proof that the modern volume boundary can cut through an earlier shipment/container family.

## 2. Why this matters for the 1921 rebinding argument

The 2011 Cambridge catalogue project reports that the Hodgson papers were rebound in 1921 into 95 volumes, with arrangement partly determined by physical dimensions. It explicitly notes that related manuscripts, including originals and translations, could be separated simply because they were written on differently sized paper.

The sack-8 relation provides a concrete object-level consequence of this general rule.

The argument can now move from:

`1921 rebinding probably dispersed old relations`

to:

`at least one historical shipment relation demonstrably crosses modern volume boundaries`.

The paper article can therefore distinguish:

`historical container order`

from

`modern bound-volume order`.

## 3. Box No.3 survives as a paper control surface, but its historical identity remains unresolved

Cambridge `vol.55 item 20` contains:

- ff.126–128: cover pages of different original bundles;
- f.129v: `Content page of box No. 3 of original shipment`.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol055.html

The same modern item is materially heterogeneous: mostly Nepali hand-made paper, some machine-made paper, original sealed royal orders, copies and other papers.

This means a historical container-control leaf has survived inside a modern mixed volume even though the original box relation is no longer physically present.

However, **Box No.3 must not yet be identified with Box 3 in the 1837 five-box list**.

RAS `BHH/11/1` records five boxes left in the Residency in December 1837, later repacked in 1844. The number coincidence is insufficient. The contents of f.129v must be read and compared with the 1837 list before continuity is asserted.

Mechanism:

`CONTAINER_NUMBER_REUSE_REQUIRES_CONTENT_CHECK`.

## 4. vol.94 preserves bundle-level shipment metadata after the manuscripts themselves were separated

Cambridge `vol.94 item 7, ff.40–44` consists of separated leaves on Nepali hand-made paper.

The catalogue says they include:

- a title folio for manuscripts on Newar and other hill castes;
- a list of the contents of Hodgson's original Kathmandu shipment;
- details of **bundle number and categories**;
- Nepali title description.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol094.html

The container is absent and the leaves are separated, but the paper control surface still preserves the old bundle topology.

This is especially important because it supplies the method for recovering a larger graph:

`bundle number on surviving control leaf`
↔ `same number on surviving notebook/cover/title leaf`
→ `modern shelfmarks currently containing the members`.

The current online catalogue does not transcribe the actual bundle numbers. Object/image inspection is therefore the next decisive step.

## 6. Pre-bound notebooks preserve old shipment numbers inside modern machine-paper volumes

Cambridge states that all four notebooks now bound into `vol.4` usually retain:

- a Nepali title;
- the original shipment number in Nepali language/script on their covers.

Likewise `vol.5` consists of six pre-bound notebooks whose covers often retain Nepali titles and original shipment numbers. In `vol.5 item 3`, the two Jatamala notebooks explicitly retain `original sack numbers`, although the online catalogue does not give the values.

Sources:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol004.html
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol005.html

This evidence is especially useful for the paper argument because those volumes are machine-made paper working notebooks. A historically local shipment identifier can therefore survive on a machine-paper derivative object.

The old archive is not recoverable by support type alone.

It is recoverable through identifiers written on supports.

## 7. The RAS list series shows that container bookkeeping was repeatedly rewritten before 1864

The surviving RAS list sequence now provides the historical frame for these old identifiers.

### RAS BHH/11/1 — 1837–1844

`List of Buddha Books & drawings, & of Statistics of Nepaul left in Residency in five Boxes`.

- each of five boxes individually listed;
- sixth package also listed;
- later annotation says the boxes were repackaged in 1844 to take home.

### RAS BHH/11/2 — 1837–1844

`List of MSS relative to Nepal`.

Hodgson later notes that marked items had been translated and that `the whole, original & translation` were with him on board the *Hardwicke* in February 1844.

This is early explicit evidence that container custody and version-family custody intersected.

### RAS BHH/11/3 — 1842–1844

`Details of Contents of Bastas or Bundles in Trunk No.1 Cathmandu AD 1842`.

The list covers Buddhism, Vaisavalis, Jatmalas, army details, catalogues and sastras, with a later note saying the contents were repacked in 1844 and put aboard the *Hardwicke*.

### RAS BHH/11/10 — 1852

`Memo for Dr Withecombe`: literary papers in trunks left at Darjeeling.

### RAS BHH/11/12 — 1855

List of Buddhist pictorial illustrations on cloth and paper, with a further list of papers in Trunk No.2.

### RAS BHH/11/14 — 1858–1864

A private store list begun in 1858 that became the manuscript list for the 1864 India Office donation.

### RAS BHH/19/5 — August 1864

Printed `List of MSS Presented by Mr Hodgson to the India Office Library`, with annotations.

RAS sources:
https://royalasiaticarchives.org/

The sequence establishes:

`1837 boxes`
→ `1842 bastas/bundles in trunk`
→ `1844 repacking and voyage`
→ `1852 Darjeeling trunks`
→ `1855 Trunk No.2 papers`
→ `1858 store list`
→ `1864 handwritten/printed donation states`.

Container metadata was therefore repeatedly revised as the archive moved.

Mechanism:

`CONTAINER_INVENTORY_ITSELF_IS_VERSIONED`.

## 8. New article mechanism: paper both destroys and restores archival relation

The archive now exposes an unusually concrete double role for paper.

First:

paper dimensions became a criterion in twentieth-century rebinding and could separate historically related documents.

Second:

old shipment numbers, box/sack lists and bundle covers written on paper survived that rebinding and can reconstruct those lost relations.

Thus:

`paper dimension -> archival dispersion`

while simultaneously:

`paper inscription -> relational recovery`.

This is stronger than saying materiality shapes archives.

The same material object supplies both:

- a physical property used to rearrange the archive;
- an inscription surface capable of reversing that rearrangement analytically.

Mechanism:

`PHYSICAL_SUPPORT_CAN_BOTH_DISPERSE_AND_RESTORE_RELATION`.

## 9. Source-control rules

1. Do not infer historical co-custody from modern adjacency.
2. Do not infer identical container identity from a repeated number alone; compare contents.
3. Do not expand sack 8 membership by subject similarity. Require an explicit surviving number or a direct match to a legible sack-8 contents entry.
4. Cambridge catalogue descriptions establish that old labels survive, but exact diplomatic transcription requires object/image inspection.
5. RAS 1837/1842/1852/1855/1858/1864 lists are successive inventory states; do not flatten them into one stable numbering system.
6. Modern volume number is a later custody identifier, not a substitute for historical container number.

## Bottom line

The paper archive now contains direct evidence for reconstructing its own lost historical topology. Sack No.8 survives both as a contents leaf in modern vol.58 and as an original sack number on a Limbu manuscript now in vol.85. Other modern volumes preserve box-content pages, bundle covers, shipment numbers and separated title leaves. The 1837–1864 RAS list sequence shows that these identifiers belonged to a repeatedly revised custody system long before the 1921 rebinding. Paper therefore participates twice in archival history: its dimensions helped later institutions disperse old relations, while inscriptions on surviving sheets now make those relations recoverable again.
