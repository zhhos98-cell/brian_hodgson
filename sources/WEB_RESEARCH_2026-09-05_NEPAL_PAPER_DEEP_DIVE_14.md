## Finding 57 — Abdul Ali repeatedly cites one exact 1832 Political Original Consultation: 13 January 1832, No. 19

Evidence status: **STRONG secondary archival extraction; original consultation still to inspect.**

Across several pages and notes, Ali cites variants of:

`Pol. O. C. No. 19, January 13, 1832`

The OCR varies (`FM. O.C.`, `Pd. O.C.`), but repeated clean instances and context establish the intended reference as **Political Original Consultation, 13 January 1832, No.19**.

Ali uses this single consultation for multiple distinct claims/materials:

- Hodgson’s December 1831 assessment of Nepal paper’s toughness, smoothness, abundance and suitability for office records;
- the detailed papermaking process and commercial/material particulars;
- prices of finished paper and dried pulp bricks;
- the 8 December 1831 Hodgson-to-H. T. Prinsep communication transmitting paper specimens for presentation to Lord William Bentinck;
- Hodgson’s recommendation that Nepal paper replace common Hindustan paper for important public records.

Consequence for retrieval:

The P0 central archive target can now be sharpened from a general `Political Consultation, 13 Jan 1832, Nos.18–20` cluster to:

**No.19 = core paper item**

while Nos.18 and 20 remain useful for docketing/context/sequence rather than being equally likely content targets.

This is still an archival locator supplied by Ali. Do not treat Ali’s transcription/extraction as equivalent to visual inspection of the 1832 Original Consultation.

## Finding 58 — the 8 December 1831 letter, the technical recipe, and the office-record proposal belong to the same central consultation dossier

Evidence status: **STRONG source-state join via Ali; original/copy relations still to control.**

Ali states that on **8 December 1831** Hodgson, then at Kathmandu, sent H. T. Prinsep, Secretary to the Governor-General, specimens of Nepal paper for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. His footnote is explicitly `Pol. O. C. No. 19 Jany. 13, 1832`.

In the same consultation reference, Ali reproduces or paraphrases Hodgson’s argument that Nepal paper should replace the perishable paper then used for office records in the plains. Earlier portions of the article cite the same No.19 for the technical manufacturing description.

The documentary-state comparison is therefore now:

1. **Residency outgoing state:** BL `IOR/R/5/43`, letters sent to Government of India, 1 Nov 1831–2 Dec 1832 — exact date range includes 8 Dec 1831;
2. **central Government state:** Political Original Consultation, 13 Jan 1832, No.19;
3. **private/copy state:** BL `Mss Eur Hodgson/6 item6 ff77–81`, covering letter + attached note, in a volume catalogued as copies;
4. **learned-society print state:** Hodgson, JASB 1 (1832), 8–11.

This is a high-value four-state comparison because names, enclosure boundaries, specimen routing, source attribution or observation language may differ across states.

P0 search rule:

Do not ask merely `what did Hodgson write?` Ask which documentary state preserves:

- covering letter versus enclosure;
- specimen reference;
- maker/informant/intermediary attribution;
- wording about personal observation versus reported practice;
- forwarding/docketing language;
- later editorial compression or omission.

## Finding 59 — Case 1 had an India-side administrative consequence: office-record substitution and proposed transfer of the craft to Kumaon

Evidence status: **STRONG via Ali’s archival extraction; exact original wording/control still desired.**

Ali’s extraction of the 8 December 1831 letter shows Hodgson doing more than sending a technical sample abroad.

He argued that Nepal paper should be adopted in the plains for records particularly worth preserving because the common Hindustan paper used for records was highly perishable. He also noted that Nepal paper had already been adopted in some plains offices.

More importantly, Ali states that Hodgson proposed overcoming scarcity in the plains by having the people of **Kumaon**, where the paper plant was abundant, **entrusted with the art of papermaking**.

This creates a first-order consequence previously underweighted in the article architecture:

`Nepal paper as durable finished object`
-> `official-record substitution proposal in the plains`
-> `scarcity problem`
-> `proposal to reproduce the craft in Kumaon where raw material was available`.

This should not be written as proof that the Kumaon programme was implemented. Current status is:

- Hodgson proposed office-record substitution = STRONG;
- Hodgson proposed transmitting the art to Kumaon = STRONG via Ali extraction;
- Government implemented a Kumaon training/manufacturing programme = **NOT ESTABLISHED**.

Historical value:

The 1831 paper intervention already joined **material performance, public-record preservation, raw-material geography and skilled manufacture** before the later British remanufacture and Darjeeling experiments.

## Finding 60 — Campbell’s 1837 proposal supplied a concrete procurement geography for official paper use

Evidence status: **SECURE published-source route via Agricultural-Horticultural Society; not an Original Consultation locator in Ali.**

Ali cites Campbell’s 15 November 1837 letter to T. C. Scott from *Transactions of the Agricultural and Horticultural Society*, vol. V.

Campbell compared surviving Nepal-paper office records with English foolscap and argued for Nepal paper in Government offices. He then addressed procurement constraints created by the Nepal court’s monopoly.

His proposed supply logic was operational:

- buy annually in Nepal during the rains and forward toward the Ganges in the four cold months;
- if court monopoly or agents obstruct bulk purchase, rely on plains/border markets;
- named markets included Patna, Kessariah in Saran, Darbhanga/Tirhoot, Purnea, Govindgunge and Alligunge in Saran, Gorakhpur-area markets, and Oude markets.

The exact historical spellings should remain source-controlled before map normalization.

This matters because the 1837 episode did not merely compare paper qualities. It translated durability into a **regional procurement problem**: season, monopoly, market nodes and onward river access.

Source-state caution:

Ali’s statement that Auckland’s 1837 inquiry inaugurated `European interest` is his framing. The direct 1837 Campbell text is strong; the exact primary Government inquiry that elicited it remains a Stiller/Residency/central-proceedings target.

Do not infer a direct 1837 -> 1841 causal chain from Ali’s narrative sentence alone.

## Finding 62 — the Military Board/Stationery Department tried to renew Darjeeling papermaking after Campbell’s April 1842 halt

Evidence status: **STRONG via Ali’s extraction of Political O.C. No.70; exact initiating Military Board document remains OPEN.**

Ali states that Campbell’s experimental factory worked until **16 April 1842**, when Campbell stopped it because he judged production uneconomic unless the existing `rude` machinery were replaced by better and more expensive factory gear.

Ali then says the **Military Board (Stationery Department)** made an endeavour to have papermaking renewed at Darjeeling. Campbell again objected on economic grounds, estimating that at prevailing Darjeeling labour wages paper from *Daphne cannabina* could not probably be produced below roughly Rs 10–15 per ream for sheets of the size he specified. He allowed that renewal might be desirable for several reasons while doubting current profitability.

Ali ties Campbell’s 21 April 1842 letter to Bushby to `Political O.C. 11 May 1842 No.70`.

This supplies a neglected downstream consequence:

`experimental production`
-> `Campbell halts on cost/machinery grounds`
-> `Military Board / Stationery Department seeks renewal`
-> `Campbell re-evaluates the paper through wages, sheet size, unit cost and potential stationery use`.

This is especially valuable for the article’s first-order question `what happened after testing?` because the trial generated an **institutional attempt to retain/revive a supply technology**, not simply a binary failure verdict.

Open documentary node:

- the Military Board/Stationery Department instruction/request itself;
- any proceeding or correspondence showing its intended stationery destination, required quantities, performance criteria or cost comparison.

Do not infer these details from Ali without the initiating record.

## Finding 63 — Ali’s own 1945 industrial interpretation must not be imported into the article

Evidence status: **SECURE historiographical/source-critical distinction.**

After the 1842 sequence, Ali argues that Government gave the scheme an unfair/lukewarm trial and pivots to a 1940s programme for a nationalist Indian handmade-paper industry. He invokes Jenkinson, Encyclopaedia Britannica, chemical judgements, import statistics and self-sufficiency.

This is useful for understanding why Ali searched the colonial archive, but it is **not** evidence for what Hodgson, Campbell, the makers, Military Board or Government thought in 1831–42.

Project rule:

- use Ali as a locator and controlled extractor of Original Consultations;
- verify his extracted historical claims where possible against the original consultation or contemporary print;
- do not inherit his `lukewarm Government -> hopeless failure -> nationalist revival` explanatory arc.
