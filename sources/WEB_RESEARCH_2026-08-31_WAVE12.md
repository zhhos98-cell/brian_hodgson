# Web research wave 12 — Darjeeling paper transfer, failed experiments, and affordance reversal

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: primary-source recovery and mechanism extraction. This wave replaces reliance on the 1899 *Paper Trade Journal* retrospective for the Darjeeling experiment with the 1842 publication itself.

## 1. Primary source recovered

**Anon./A. Campbell correspondence, “Correspondence relative to the Manufacture of Nipal Paper at Darjeeling, with details as to its cost, relative value, mode of bleaching, and rendering water proof,” *Journal of the Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India* 1 (1842), 210–221.**

The volume also prints a sequel at pp. 273–274: **“Sequel to the Correspondence regarding the Manufacture of Nipal Paper at Darjeeling. Result of Mr. Marshman’s Experiments.”**

Public-domain scan / OCR:

- Internet Archive item: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.109896
- full OCR: https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.109896/2015.109896.Journal-Of-The-Agricultural-And-Horticultural-Society-Of-India--Vol-1_djvu.txt
- Wikimedia scan record: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Journal_Of_The_Agricultural_And_Horticultural_Society_Of_India_Vol._1_(IA_in.ernet.dli.2015.109896).pdf

The printed table of contents gives the paper at p. 210 and attributes it to Dr A. Campbell, Superintendent of Darjeeling. Modern historiography likewise cites *Journal of the Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India* 1 (1842), 210–221.

### Provenance caution

The journal is printing a **correspondence dossier**, not a single retrospective essay. It includes Campbell → Government of India, Government → Campbell, O’Shaughnessy → Government, and later Marshman material. Individual propositions therefore need speaker-level attribution.

---

## 2. Hodgson is not incidental: the transfer begins through him

Campbell opens by recalling that Lord Auckland had asked **Hodgson** to send him people skilled in Nepali paper manufacture. In mid-April Campbell received five paper-makers sent by the Nepal Durbar at Hodgson’s request.

The labour hierarchy and monthly wages are given explicitly:

- 1 maker / head man: Rs 10 per month;
- 1 mate: Rs 8 per month;
- 3 ordinary workers: Rs 8 each, Rs 24 total;
- total monthly wage bill: Rs 42.

Campbell identifies the head man and first assistant as Newars of the Nepal valley and the other three, in the period’s caste/geographical vocabulary, as “low caste Purbutteahs” from the neighbourhood of Ham. Preserve this wording only as source language; the analytical point is that **technical expertise was embodied in a differentiated labour group and moved through diplomatic/administrative channels**.

### Mechanism

This gives a concrete chain:

**Auckland request → Hodgson brokerage → Nepal Durbar order → five skilled makers → Campbell’s experimental station → Lepcha trainees → Government / Calcutta chemical and industrial trials.**

The technology does not diffuse as a disembodied recipe. It moves through people, salaries, authority, travel, tools, raw materials and later specimens of paper.

---

## 3. Campbell explicitly defines the first experiment as information-gathering rather than cost minimisation

The five makers arrived without their implements. Campbell had neither workshop nor materials ready. He states that his immediate object was to obtain full information for Lord Auckland about manufacture in a place where it had not previously been tried, **rather than to discover the lowest production cost**.

He therefore sent the makers with guides to inspect different localities of the paper plant and choose material.

This matters methodologically. The first Darjeeling operation should be treated as an **experimental inquiry / technology-transfer trial**, not simply as an unsuccessful factory.

---

## 4. Siting exposes a three-way conflict: raw material, transport cost, and inspectability

The makers preferred the paper-tree jungle near Pucheem/Pacheem, at roughly the same elevation as Darjeeling, and wanted the manufactory located there to economise carriage.

Campbell overrode this preference because he wanted production **nearer to his own observation**. He selected a small stream crossing the Auckland Road near Mr Smoult’s location, built a small house, dug a tank, and carried water into it in “Lepcha fashion” through bamboo conduits.

The makers’ own siting criteria linked:

- abundant paper plant;
- woods producing suitable ash;
- a clear running water supply;
- carriage distance.

Campbell added another criterion:

- **administrative / experimental observability**.

### High-value claim

The manufactory is an observation instrument as well as a production site. **Inspectability itself has a material cost**, because placing the experiment close to the superintendent can increase the transport burden of bark and fuel.

This gives a useful counterweight to narratives in which colonial “improvement” merely relocates a technique toward economic efficiency. Here Campbell knowingly sacrifices one form of efficiency to increase experimental supervision.

---

## 5. The transferred technique is not identical to Hodgson’s 1831 description

Campbell explicitly cites Hodgson’s published account (JASB, January 1832; also *Transactions Agricultural Society*, vol. 5, p. 228) as the baseline list of implements.

The makers nevertheless introduce practical differences:

- flat stone + double-headed hand mallets for beating bark rather than Hodgson’s stone mortar + wooden pestle;
- rejection of an iron boiler because iron gave the paper a dark tinge;
- tinned copper regarded as best, with brass acceptable;
- continuous flow of water through the tank to carry away alkali from the pulp in the frames.

### Consequence

Hodgson’s recipe is a **portable verbal specification**, but workshop reconstruction immediately reveals tacit variants and material substitutions. The paper census should therefore not treat the 1832 recipe as a fixed technological type against which surviving paper is simply matched.

---

## 6. Bark drying generates a directly observed failure case

Campbell stresses prompt and thorough drying after removal of the outer green bark. Once dried, the bark can apparently be stored, but the makers insist that it should not be rewetted before manufacture.

A shipment of previously dried bark became wet while being carried from Pacheem to Darjeeling. It mildewed and became **altogether useless for paper-making**.

### Failed experiment / affordance value

This is unusually clean because the failure links:

**transport condition → moisture exposure → biological/material alteration → loss of manufacturability.**

Paper manufacture therefore begins before the workshop. Raw-material transport and drying are part of the knowledge infrastructure.

This case should be coded separately from later failures of the finished sheet.

---

## 7. Ash is a quality-bearing process material, not generic fuel residue

Campbell reports that the quality of ash is judged partly by colouring effects: bad ash produces dingy paper. He lists preferred woods and argues that a manufactory should be near suitable trees so that fuel used for drying can simultaneously produce the best ash cheaply.

This closes a material loop:

**wood → drying fuel + ash → alkaline process liquor → paper colour / quality.**

The same resource can therefore perform two sequential functions. Site ecology, fuel ecology and chemical processing cannot be cleanly separated.

The makers even describe the occasional burning of paper-tree sticks as a craft “secret” for improving the ash. Record as reported craft knowledge, not yet as chemically demonstrated fact.

---

## 8. Paper-tree variation is treated experimentally

Campbell records three flower-colour / elevation-defined paper-tree kinds and associates them with different paper qualities. The whitish-pink flowering type is described as the abundant source of most Nepal/Sikkim paper; a scarlet-flowered higher-elevation type is said to yield the smoothest and least dingy sheet.

Crucially, Campbell does not simply trust the description. He plants specimens in his garden and proposes to use flowering to test whether the distinctions are correct.

He also reports a claim that monks at Pemiong in Sikkim had recently made unusually white paper from the inner bark of another tree and proposes inviting an acquaintance from the monastery to learn the process.

### Mechanism

This broadens the paper project from “local material” into **comparative resource identification + controlled cultivation + craft interviewing + process transfer**.

---

## 9. The whitening problem creates a chain of failed and redirected experiments

Campbell identifies colour and smoothness as the two features in which his paper is inferior to English paper. He proposes fine wire sieves for removing stringy material and chlorine for whitening, but initially lacks the required materials.

His local bleaching trials then separate two properties that cannot be maximised together:

### Trial A — immersion + rain/air exposure

- pulp becomes considerably whiter;
- paper loses the “wonderful toughness” of its natural state.

### Trial B — pulp spread on a board and exposed to the elements

- toughness remains unimpaired;
- whitening is slight.

Campbell abandons the immersion method and proposes further testing of the exposure method after the rains.

### Analytical significance

This is a direct **performance trade-off**:

**whiteness ↑ → toughness ↓** in one treatment;

**toughness retained → whiteness barely improved** in the other.

The experiment makes visible that “improvement” is not a scalar. A process optimised toward one desired attribute can destroy another pre-existing affordance.

This is a better failed-experiment case than a generic narrative of “indigenous craft vs European technology” because the conflict occurs inside a shared attempt to re-engineer a specific sheet property.

---

## 10. Government turns the paper into a distributed experimental object

Campbell reports producing 3,300 sheets (two classes/sizes in the OCR), total weight **1 maund 7 seers**. His daily labour calculation gives an approximate cost of **Rs 25-8 per maund**, while warning that the trial was deliberately expensive and not a contract-production test.

He says the informational goals had been achieved to a considerable extent:

- determine whether good paper could be made locally;
- determine whether it could be made in quantity;
- transfer the art to Lepchas.

He successfully persuaded **two Lepchas** to learn the art, thereby creating a local capacity to restart production.

Government then orders the experimental course continued and asks Campbell to keep necessary manufacturers on pay. It also supplies Calcutta market prices as a comparison standard and requests **twenty or thirty sheets by dak in several despatches** for O’Shaughnessy’s bleaching experiments.

### Mechanism

The sheet itself becomes an experimental token moving through a network:

**Darjeeling manufacture → dak despatch → Government office → chemical examiner → bleached specimens → Governor-General inspection → industrial-scale trial at Serampore.**

This is precisely the kind of distributed verification chain the broader Hodgson project is trying to reconstruct.

---

## 11. Two proposed substitutions: filtering paper and waterproof postal wrapping

Government compares imported English filtering paper with Darjeeling manufacture:

- English filtering paper: about 6 reams annual Calcutta consumption over the previous three years;
- price: Rs 9 per ream;
- Darjeeling paper was understood to be producible at about Rs 3 per ream for this purpose.

A second experiment treats Darjeeling paper with India rubber and reports it capable of being rendered waterproof. The process is described as cheap and simple enough potentially to be performed by hill people. The prepared paper is stated to weigh only one-third as much as the same area of wax cloth used by the Government Post Office. Post Office authorities therefore consider replacing wax cloth with the prepared paper to reduce cost/weight and improve protection of packets in wet conditions.

### Affordance point

The paper is not being compared to “paper” in the abstract. Its strength, lightness, permeability and treatability place it into **two different substitution tests**:

1. chemical filtration;
2. waterproof transport wrapping.

The same material qualities can perform differently under each operation.

---

## 12. O’Shaughnessy changes the bleaching chemistry

After Campbell’s unsuccessful local trials, Dr W. B. O’Shaughnessy reports a cheap process using **chlorine dissolved in water rather than chloride of lime**. His explanation is that lime combines with the colouring matter of Darjeeling paper into a substance difficult to bleach, while simple chlorine solution acts more effectively.

He forwards the process to Mr Marshman of the Serampore Paper Mills for practical testing on a larger scale.

The printed correspondence even records chemical inputs and notes availability constraints in the Calcutta bazaar.

### Mechanism

Failure is not terminal. It produces a new experimental handoff:

**Campbell property failure → O’Shaughnessy chemical diagnosis → revised reagent regime → Marshman scale-up test.**

This is a compact example of distributed troubleshooting across administrative, chemical and industrial sites.

## 13. The Serampore sequel produces the strongest affordance reversal

The sequel at pp. 273–274 reports Marshman’s industrial experiment. His verdict is substantially negative.

He finds that:

- bark requires more bleaching effort than ordinary rag materials;
- even after steeping, boiling and sizing, the result is an inferior general paper;
- the sheet may suit a use such as bank-note paper where strength matters more than colour;
- attempts to make thicker paper fail because mucilaginous material prevents the pulp from running freely on the machine web;
- existing machinery therefore makes general adoption doubtful;
- cheaper alternative furnish/mixes available near Serampore produce superior paper.

The editors add that Nepal paper’s supposed superiority as filtering paper is doubtful in use: its strength permits twisting a filter without tearing, but filtration is slow and the resulting liquor may be less clear than with good European filtering paper. They instead regard **packing**, especially waterproofed wet-season dak/bhangy parcels, as a much stronger application because strength and lightness matter while dingy colour does not.

### Core article-level mechanism: affordance reversal at an interface

This case should become a central test case for the project:

**handmade floating-frame system:** long/strong/mucilaginous fibre contributes to toughness;

**machine-web system:** the same material behaviour obstructs flow and thickness control;

**filtration:** toughness is useful, slow/poor clarification is a liability;

**packing:** toughness + low weight become decisive advantages and colour becomes irrelevant.

No single intrinsic label such as “superior paper” can describe the material. The affordance is **operation- and interface-specific**.

This is stronger than a binary of local vs European paper because the historical actors themselves move the same material through multiple testing regimes and discover different rankings.

## 14. Consequences for paper-census coding

Add fields/codes capable of representing:

- `process_stage`: bark / pulp / wet sheet / dried sheet / treated sheet;
- `operation`: writing / filtration / wrapping / bleaching / machine forming / transport;
- `desired_property`: whiteness / smoothness / toughness / permeability / low mass / waterproofness / machinability;
- `treatment`: rain-air exposure / immersion / chlorine water / chloride of lime / India rubber / sizing;
- `failure_mode`: mildew / toughness loss / insufficient whitening / poor machine-web flow / slow filtration / turbidity;
- `interface`: hand frame / machine web / postal packet / chemical filter;
- `comparison_standard`: English filtering paper / Calcutta country-paper grades / wax cloth / rag furnish;
- `actor_role`: maker / trainee / administrator / chemical examiner / industrial papermaker / editor-user;
- `transfer_event`: person / bark / finished sheets / formula / specimen / report;
- `evidence_status`: direct 1842 dossier / later retrospective / modern historiography.

These fields make “affordance” empirical rather than decorative.

## 15. Consequences for the article spine

The paper section can now make a sharper argument than “Hodgson used different papers for different purposes.” A more substantial claim is available:

> Himalayan paper knowledge was experimentally reconstituted by moving skilled makers, bark, sheets, formulas and performance tests across Nepal, Darjeeling, Calcutta and Serampore. The same fibre system changed epistemic and economic value when it crossed technical interfaces: qualities that made a hand-formed sheet exceptionally tough became obstacles on a machine web, and qualities that disappointed in filtration became advantages in waterproof packing.

This connects directly to the repo’s larger interest in **interoperability, transport interfaces, verification after movement, and failure as a diagnostic of infrastructure**.

It also creates a bridge to the paper census: surviving sheets should be observed not merely to identify “Nepali” or “machine-made” support, but to ask which operations selected for thickness, surface, absorbency, foldability, toughness, colour or treatment.

## 17. Source-control notes

1. The 1899 *Paper Trade Journal* retrospective is now a **secondary transmission witness**, useful for reception/history of technical memory but unnecessary for the central factual claims above.
2. The 1842 scan has OCR noise. Before quotation in publication, verify wording against page images for pp. 210–221 and 273–274.
3. The correspondence dossier contains editorial omissions: at p. 221 the editors state that some “unimportant notes” about document transmission and forwarding paper to Marshman were omitted. This omission is itself relevant to the project because the editors explicitly classify **circulation metadata as unimportant**, whereas this repo treats circulation as epistemic infrastructure.
4. The sequel is especially valuable because it reports an industrial test at Serampore and an editorial judgment based on actual filtering use. It therefore prevents a triumphalist reading of the Government’s initial substitution proposal.
5. Plant names should be kept historically as given in each source and cross-walked separately to current taxonomy. Do not silently normalize Campbell’s paper-tree nomenclature.

## Bottom line

Wave 12 produces a strong, source-level HPS case in which **failure reveals affordance**. Hodgson’s 1831 technical description becomes a travelling specification; Hodgson then helps mobilise skilled makers; Campbell reconstructs and observes a workshop at Darjeeling; local material transport fails under moisture; bleaching exposes a whiteness/toughness trade-off; Government circulates sheets for chemical and postal testing; O’Shaughnessy revises the chemistry; Marshman tests the bark against machine manufacture and finds a new failure mode; editors then rank filtration and packing differently. The object’s value changes each time the operation and interface change.
