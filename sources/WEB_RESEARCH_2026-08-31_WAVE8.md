# Public-web research wave 8 — catalogue transformations, species numbers, and support misidentification

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. No private/archive scans added.

## 1. The catalogue itself is an active transformation layer

Recent work on Hodgson's zoological material shows that the British Museum catalogue did not merely record Hodgson's categories. It reorganised them.

A study of Gray & Gray's catalogue notes that:
- Hodgson drawing numbers were attached to skins, anatomical specimens and drawings in uneven ways;
- some Hodgson numbers were accidentally omitted or moved to unexpected places;
- the museum assigned a second series of drawing numbers;
- two or more Hodgson drawing numbers could be grouped under one species name;
- Hodgson annotated his own copy: `A great many species are lumped together in this catalogue`.

Source:
https://www.aviansystematics.org/uploads/downloads/153/file/ZM80-05_137-154.pdf

This is critical: a museum catalogue is not simply evidence about the collection. It is a **new classificatory operation imposed on the collection**.

## 2. Eurasian Collared Dove: a number can propagate an unpublished name

A recent reconstruction of the Eurasian Collared Dove shows a particularly clean Hodgson microcase.

- Hodgson had several Nepal specimens linked to drawing no. 107.
- His 1844 list called the bird `Columba douraca`.
- Hodgson never validly published `douraca` as a scientific name, and the name was not on the drawing itself.
- Nevertheless, the name circulated through ornithology and was accepted by later workers; specimens outside Hodgson's own collection were eventually labelled with it.

Source:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361071222_The_colourful_journey_of_the_Eurasian_Collared_Dove_Streptopelia_decaocto

Bibliographic record / DOI context:
https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122535/records/65df053f0f3e94b9e5d421cd

Analytic implication: **a manuscript/list name can become operationally real through labels and catalogues even when it never acquires formal nomenclatural validity**. This gives a zoological parallel to manuscript wrapper/slip titles that stabilise an identity without being internally authorised by the text.

## 3. Number 830: surviving original label does not automatically prove type status

A modern type review discusses a Hodgson specimen bearing drawing number 830 on an original Hodgson label. The existence of the number helps link the specimen to Hodgson's visual catalogue, but the authors still cannot accept type status automatically because:

- the specimen's later registration number was assigned retrospectively rather than from the contemporary register;
- the specimen's physical condition may explain why it was not registered earlier;
- type status requires evidence that the specimen was actually available to the author at the time of description;
- Hodgson could use the same drawing number on multiple comparable specimens.

Source:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228904274_Systematic_notes_on_Asian_birds_50_Types_of_the_Aegithalidae_Remizidae_and_Paridae

This is a model case for the claim that **surviving identifiers require historical interpretation**.

## 4. Hodgson's numbers began as a classification and then broke down

The systematic history of Hodgson's collections argues that his numbering sequence was not simply chronological. The early series appears to have been structured after he had already accumulated hundreds of birds, with numbers arranged in a systematic family/genus sequence. As new material continued to arrive, the scheme became increasingly irregular and eventually more nearly chronological for newly encountered taxa.

Source:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228490048_Systematic_notes_on_Asian_birds_52_An_introduction_to_the_bird_collections_of_Brian_Houghton_Hodgson_1801-1894

This gives the numbering system a history of its own:

**classification scheme → gap-filling → overflow/new arrivals → irregular sequence → institutional renumbering**.

The number is therefore not merely an identifier. It is a fossil of a changing classificatory architecture.

## 5. Local observation was keyed to the drawing number

ZSL's archival catalogue preserves Hodgson's notebook:

`Memoranda of manners and habits of birds of my Collection (according to the numbers attached to the drawings). Done by my native sportsmen or Shikarees and translated by my writer`.

Source:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=8774&places=53595&sf_culture=nl&showAdvanced=1&sort=startDate&sortDir=desc&subjects=10547&topLod=0&view=table

This means a single drawing number could link:

**shikari observation → writer/translation → behavioural memorandum → drawing → specimen(s) → museum catalogue → published taxon**.

The number is a cross-media translation key.

## 6. ZSL's surviving drawings visibly preserve the relational system

ZSL describes the bird manuscripts as six volumes containing drawings plus scientific/common names, locality, measurements, weights, notes, and reference numbers. ZSL explicitly says the manuscripts contain unpublished information relevant to type specimens.

Source:
https://www.zsl.org/what-we-do/education-learning/zsl-library/introduction-brian-houghton-hodgson

This supports treating the drawings not as illustrations appended to a specimen collection but as a parallel evidence archive.

## 7. Museum handling changed future taxonomic auditability

The same collection history provides a strong institutional comparison:

- BMNH removed many original Hodgson labels;
- East India Company Museum curator Frederic Moore preserved them;
- Cambridge specimens from Strickland/Jardine routes retain some Hodgson labels;
- Leiden relabelled its entire collection in 1897–1904, obscuring whether original labels had survived earlier stages;
- specimens outside the BMNH are now especially useful precisely because some retain original Hodgson labels.

Sources:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228490048_Systematic_notes_on_Asian_birds_52_An_introduction_to_the_bird_collections_of_Brian_Houghton_Hodgson_1801-1894
https://www.discoveringbuddha.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2005-Origins-of-Himalayan-Studies-Brian-H-Hodgson-by-Waterhouse.pdf

This provides an unusually clean history-of-science comparison of **curatorial regime as an epistemic variable**.

## 8. RAS Hodgson MS 35: support misidentification changed the scholarly identity of a manuscript

The manuscript now known as the `Vanaratna Codex` is an exceptionally strong manuscript counterpart.

A modern study reports that Cowell and Eggeling's nineteenth-century catalogue classified Hodgson MS 35 as:
- a paper manuscript;
- written around the end of the eighteenth century.

Re-examination shows instead that:
- the support is palm leaf, not paper;
- the codex is associated with the fifteenth-century Buddhist master Vanaratna (1384–1468);
- it may be an autograph or have been prepared under his close supervision;
- it contains multiple Sanskrit works and Sanskrit translations of material transmitted in Tibetan;
- its historical importance had remained largely hidden partly because of the earlier misclassification.

Sources:
https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110741124-019/html
https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/21/75/89/06/2021758906/2021758906.pdf

This is perhaps the strongest evidence yet that **material description is not peripheral to textual scholarship**. Getting the support wrong can radically distort dating, provenance, authorship and scholarly significance.

## 9. The Vanaratna Codex also exposes reconstruction through foliation

The modern study finds missing/damaged folios and an apparent ten-number jump in the original foliation. A later hand added pencil Arabic numerals, but that supplied numbering encodes one reconstruction and may not reflect the original manuscript sequence.

The case therefore contains successive identity layers:

**original palm-leaf foliation → physical loss/damage → nineteenth-century catalogue (`paper`, late date) → modern pencil renumbering → twenty-first-century codicological reconstruction**.

This is structurally comparable to zoological chains in which:

**original Hodgson label → museum replacement label → accession/register → catalogue → modern type reconstruction**.

## 10. A sharper comparison: operational names without secure internal authority

Two cases now mirror each other unusually well.

### Zoology
`Columba douraca` circulated as a name and entered labels despite lacking valid publication by Hodgson.

### Manuscript culture
A wrapper or Hodgson slip could supply the working title of a codex even when the title was absent internally; MS 67 shows that a wrapper-based provisional title could stabilise a wrong catalogue identity for more than a century.

The comparison is not that labels and wrappers are identical things. It is that **external inscriptions can acquire authority through repeated institutional copying**.

## 11. A possible article-level mechanism: recursive stabilisation

The current evidence suggests a sequence more precise than `circulation`:

1. An object is given an identifier (number, title, slip, wrapper, foliation).
2. That identifier allows packing, listing, routing, comparison and citation.
3. A receiving institution copies the identifier into a register/catalogue/new label.
4. Repetition stabilises the identifier even if the original identification was provisional or wrong.
5. Later contradiction — morphology, codicology, textual comparison, accession chronology, DNA — forces an expensive reconstruction.

This can be called **recursive stabilisation** without assuming that every identifier is false or unstable.

## 12. Research consequences

The next census should record not just objects but **identifier layers**.

For zoological material:
- Hodgson drawing number;
- original specimen label surviving?;
- local name/date/locality label;
- BM/EIC/other accession number;
- later museum labels;
- catalogue name(s);
- published taxon name(s);
- type claim and date of type designation;
- institutional transfers.

For manuscripts:
- internal title;
- colophon title/date;
- foliation system;
- wrapper title;
- Hodgson slip/title;
- catalogue title/date/support judgement;
- later re-identification;
- conservation/rebinding identifiers;
- missing/supplied leaves.

The goal is to reconstruct not a static provenance field but a **history of successive identifications**.
