# Web/archive research 2026-09-02 — Wave 67
## Dual stationery supply and physical quality control: metropolitan consignments, Indian paper markets, musters, reams and supplier traceability

Date: 2026-09-02

Status: contextual paper-supply infrastructure for the Hodgson paper-history mainline. This wave reconstructs East India Company stationery practices that make Hodgson's coexistence of imported/machine-made and locally acquired Nepali paper historically intelligible. It does **not** identify any specific Hodgson sheet with a Company stationery consignment unless object-level evidence does so independently.

## Executive result

Waves 64–66 established three support-acquisition mechanisms inside Hodgson's working environment:

1. billed local paper procurement;
2. collaborator-mediated local acquisition;
3. allocation from retained/mixed working stock.

Wave 67 adds the wider institutional supply ecology in which imported paper circulated.

The East India Company's paper supply was not a simple London-to-India pipeline. It operated as a **dual and failure-prone system** combining:

- metropolitan stationery purchased from more than one supplier and sent in identifiable chests;
- physical paper musters/samples used to adjudicate quality complaints;
- inter-presidency redistribution when one establishment lacked suitable stock;
- local Indian paper markets supplying office-suitable paper;
- later centralized stationery administration in Calcutta with grade-specific indents and quality disputes.

The strongest direct period case is a Court of Directors despatch to Fort St George carried in the 1725–26 packet cycle. Madras had complained of bad paper and sent paper musters to London. The Court observed that the samples had been taken only from the **corded/outside quires** of the ream, which were normally poorer than the rest. It ordered future complaints to report the **marks and numbers of the chest** or another identifying description because the Company was supplied by **two different stationers**.

This produces an unusually complete material quality-control chain:

`stationer`
-> `ream`
-> `within-ream quality gradient`
-> `chest/package`
-> `shipment to India`
-> `local use/complaint`
-> `physical muster returned to London`
-> `sampling-bias diagnosis`
-> `package identifier used to recover supplier provenance`.

For Hodgson's period, Tania Sengupta's archival reconstruction points to related Company stationery problems in 1800–1811: watermarked-paper shortages at Calcutta, inter-presidency paper transfers, and physical paper samples/musters sent in complaints. Those individual IOR records remain retrieval targets rather than direct-read primary witnesses in this wave.

At the Indian-market side, Robert Montgomery Martin's 1838 *Eastern India*, compiled from the East India Company's original survey materials, is cited at vol.1 p.373 for a specialised Patna paper market with dedicated `kaghazi` shops, paper polishers and a principal office-oriented `daftari` paper. The period work is directly identified; the p.373 content is currently controlled through Sengupta's page-specific citation rather than a direct image reading.

The result is a supply model useful for Hodgson without forcing a specific provenance:

`metropolitan stationer / Company stationery system`
+
`inter-presidency redistribution`
+
`Indian bazaar/manufacturing networks`
+
`local collaborator-mediated acquisition`
-> `mixed Residency/research stock`
-> `operation-specific allocation`.

## 1. Direct period case: the Court diagnoses a bad-paper sample as corded-quire bias

Source family:

*Records of Fort St George: Despatches from England, 1724–27*.

The surviving printed record contains a Court paragraph headed around the paper/stationery shipment. The Court says the invoice shows what `paper & Stationary ware` has been sent. It then answers an earlier complaint about paper quality.

The seller had observed that all the paper musters returned from India came only from the **Corded Quires**, which the Court says were `always worse than the rest of the Rheam`.

The Court therefore required any future bad-paper complaint to include:

- the particulars of the defect;
- `Marks & Numbers of the Chest` or another identifying description;
- enough information to determine when the paper had been supplied;
- because the Company was supplied by `two different Stationers`.

Search-accessible period text:
https://dspace.gipe.ac.in/xmlui/bitstream/handle/10973/35353/GIPE-062196.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=3

The same volume identifies itself as `Despatches from England, 1724-27`. A packet list in the volume identifies the relevant Company's General Letter to Fort St George dated **1 December 1725**, sent in triplicate via the *Craggs* and *Sarum*. The nearby paper paragraph also refers to stores carried by the *Craggs*, making the 1725–26 packet cycle the controlled dating context. Full page-by-page collation of the printed volume remains desirable before assigning an item-level date more narrowly than the General Letter context.

Evidence state:

`DIRECT_PERIOD_PRINTED_TEXT + GENERAL_LETTER_PACKET_DATE_CONTROL`.

## 2. Why corded quires matter materially

The Company's explanation depends on an ordinary feature of hand-paper packaging, not rhetorical excuse.

Joseph Moxon's seventeenth-century printing manual describes a ream as twenty quires and identifies the two outside quires as `Cording Quires` or `Cassie Quires`. They protect the package and commonly contain torn, wrinkled, stained or otherwise inferior sheets; printers culled usable sheets from them rather than treating them as representative of the ream's normal quality.

Thus the Fort St George dispute reveals a sampling problem at the scale of the package:

`quality of outside protective quires != quality of inner stock`.

The returned muster was physical evidence but **not statistically/materially representative evidence**.

This is useful for the paper-history article because it demonstrates that even a physical sample can mislead if its position in the paper package is unknown.

Mechanisms:

- `PHYSICAL_SAMPLE_NOT_AUTOMATICALLY_REPRESENTATIVE`
- `PACKAGE_POSITION_IS_A_MATERIAL_METADATA_FIELD`
- `REAM_HAS_INTERNAL_QUALITY_STRUCTURE`.

Source for technical period vocabulary:
Joseph Moxon, *Mechanick Exercises*, section on setting out paper and culling the cording quires.

## 3. The chest mark turns paper quality into a traceability problem

The Court's proposed remedy is especially valuable.

It does not ask merely for a verbal description such as `bad paper`. It asks for marks/numbers of the **chest** or other particulars sufficient to identify the supply event.

Why?

Because two different stationers supplied the Company.

The evidential chain is therefore relational:

`defective sheet`
-> `ream`
-> `chest mark/number`
-> `shipment/supply event`
-> `stationer`.

Without the package identifier, the institutional system cannot reliably attribute quality failure to one supplier.

For Hodgson-paper research this creates a methodological analogue:

watermark/maker evidence should be joined to package, wrapper, bill, bundle or issue-record evidence whenever available rather than treated as a self-sufficient provenance tag.

Mechanism:

`SUPPLIER_PROVENANCE_REQUIRES_CROSS_SCALE_IDENTIFIERS`.

## 5. Inter-presidency redistribution means paper's route need not reveal its manufacture origin

Sengupta's reconstruction includes cases in which one presidency supplied another with paper during shortage.

That materially strengthens an existing Hodgson guardrail:

`place of issue or purchase != place of manufacture`.

A sheet used in Bengal could have arrived from:

- Britain through Company stationery supply;
- another Indian presidency's stock;
- a local or regional Indian paper market;
- older retained institutional stock.

Likewise, a sheet reaching Kathmandu via Calcutta need not have been manufactured in Calcutta.

Mechanisms:

- `PRESIDENCY_TO_PRESIDENCY_STATIONERY_REDISTRIBUTION`
- `SUPPLY_NODE_NOT_MANUFACTURE_ORIGIN`
- `STOCK_ROUTE_CAN_HAVE_MULTIPLE_INTERMEDIATE_CUSTODIANS`.

## 6. 1838 Martin: Patna is a specialised office-paper market, not generic bazaar supply

Period work:

Robert Montgomery Martin, *The History, Antiquities, Topography, and Statistics of Eastern India*, vol.1 (London: W.H. Allen, 1838).

The book identifies itself as a compilation/collation of original survey materials produced under Company governmental authority.

Sengupta cites Martin vol.1, chapter 4, p.373 for the paper-market description:

- many towns sold paper through general or `pasari` shops;
- Patna had dedicated paper (`kaghazi`) shops;
- some shops employed paper polishers who finished rough paper bought wholesale from paper manufacturers;
- a principal Patna grade was called `daftari`, i.e. office-related paper.

Current evidence state:

`PERIOD_SOURCE_IDENTIFIED + PAGE_SPECIFIC_CONTENT_CONTROLLED_THROUGH_SECONDARY_CITATION`.

Even at this controlled level, the material implication is important:

**local paper supply included specialised retail and finishing operations adapted to administrative writing, not merely household or artisanal paper manufacture.**

Mechanisms:

- `RETAILER_CAN_ADD_FINISHING_AFTER_MANUFACTURE`
- `PAPER_SUPPLY_CHAIN_EXTENDS_BEYOND_PAPERMAKER`.

## 7. Local `daftari` paper complicates any imported/local quality hierarchy

The Patna case shows that locally made paper could be deliberately finished and marketed for office use.

Therefore the article should not use:

`imported/machine paper = administrative/fair`

versus

`local handmade paper = rough/source`

as a general hierarchy.

Wave 65 already falsifies that mapping inside Hodgson's commerce archive: Farsi quantitative work survives on both machine-made and Nepali handmade support; vernacular source packets themselves can mix support classes.

Martin's Patna market supplies the broader economic explanation for such overlap: Indian paper networks could produce and finish grades specifically suited to documentary labour.

Mechanism:

`LOCAL_HANDMADE_PAPER_CAN_BE_OFFICE_SPECIALIZED`.

## 8. 1850 Calcutta: drawing paper is an administratively distinct stationery grade

A West Bengal government index to official proceedings for July–December 1850 records several Stationery Office matters, including:

- fresh stationery indents;
- correspondence involving the Superintendent of Stationery;
- a complaint from the Deputy Surveyor-General that some **drawing paper was unfit for use**;
- referral of the Stationery Superintendent's explanation;
- a request to the Court of Directors that drawing-paper supplies be sent **half-yearly rather than annually**;
- paper issues and transport/package questions elsewhere in the same stationery administration.

This is seven years after Hodgson left Nepal and must not be projected backwards as proof of his specific supply channel.

Evidence function:

`LATER_DIRECT_CONTINUITY_OF_INSTITUTIONAL_DRAWING_PAPER_MANAGEMENT`.

It does establish that by mid-century drawing paper was:

- separately named as a stationery grade;
- judged for fitness-for-use;
- managed through a supply cadence;
- handled by a specialized stationery bureaucracy.

This supports the plausibility of treating Hodgson/Raj Man Singh imported drawing stocks as an operation-specific paper category rather than generic `European paper`, while leaving their actual procurement route unresolved.

Mechanisms:

- `DRAWING_PAPER_IS_OPERATION_SPECIFIC_STATIONERY_GRADE`
- `FITNESS_FOR_USE_IS_INSTITUTIONAL_QUALITY_CATEGORY`
- `SUPPLY_CADENCE_AFFECTS_STOCK_AVAILABILITY`.

## 10. Paper quality is relational across scales

Wave 67 makes a methodological point that should remain central.

A sheet's observed quality can depend on:

- manufacturing process;
- grade/sorting;
- whether it came from an inside or cording quire;
- finishing/polishing;
- age/storage;
- transport damage;
- task/interface;
- sample-selection procedure.

Thus:

`quality judgment != intrinsic fibre ranking`.

It is:

`observed fitness = manufacture × sorting/position × finishing × stock history × transport × operation × sampling`.

This links directly back to Cowan, Darjeeling and Serampore:

- process history changes properties;
- interface changes whether a property is useful;
- Wave 67 adds that packaging/sampling can change what evaluators believe the stock's quality to be.

New mechanism:

`QUALITY_EVIDENCE_HAS_A_MATERIAL_SAMPLING_HISTORY`.

7. Do not infer that Hodgson's imported drawing sheets came through Company stationery supply solely because such infrastructure existed.
8. Keep manufacture origin, supply node, issue node and use location as separate fields.
9. A physical muster can itself be biased evidence; record where in a ream/package the sample came from whenever possible.

## Bottom line

Hodgson's mixed paper archive belonged to a wider supply system in which paper moved through several overlapping infrastructures. British stationery arrived in reams and marked chests from multiple stationers, and its quality could be disputed by returning physical samples; Indian markets simultaneously maintained specialist paper shops, finishing labour and office-oriented grades; Company establishments redistributed stock when supply failed; local collaborators and Residency bills supplied Nepali paper through still other channels. The material lesson is not simply that imported and local paper coexisted. **Paper provenance was distributed across maker, stationer, package, presidency stock, market intermediary, collaborator and working allocation, while even paper-quality evidence depended on where a sample had been taken from within the ream.**
