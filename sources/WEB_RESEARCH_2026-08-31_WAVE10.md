# Public-web research wave 10 — load interfaces, case chronology, media status, and receipt verification

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. This wave follows the immediate targets left by Wave 9. No new private/archive scans were added.

## 1. Hodgson's load-unit passage is now located in his own 1857 printed text

Wave 9 had the packing passage only through W. W. Hunter's 1896 quotation. A direct public scan of Hodgson's 1857 *Papers on Colonization, Commerce, Physical Geography, &c. &c., relating to the Himalaya Mountains and Nepal* now exposes the passage in Hodgson's own printed text.

In the commerce discussion, Hodgson describes a route in which a merchant debarks with goods at Kesriah or Govindgunge, uses bullocks to the base of the greater mountains of Nepal, then replaces bullocks with human carriers for the mountain transfer. He therefore recommends that wares be made up at Calcutta into **secure packages adapted for carriage on a man's back**, each of **two Calcutta bazaar maunds**. Packages above that threshold have to be taken apart on the road because two men cannot combine their strength effectively, creating `great hazard and inconvenience`.

He then adds a second design constraint: **two such human-load parcels form exactly one bullock freight**. The merchant should therefore carry apparatus for fixing two parcels across a bullock's saddle.

Direct source:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1857%20Papers%20on%20Colonization%20Commerce%20Physical%20Geography%20%26c.%20%26c.%20to%20Himalaya%20Mountains%20and%20Nepal%20by%20Hodgson%20s.pdf

Hunter's later quotation remains useful as a clean transcription and notes that two Calcutta bazaar maunds were about 160 lb avoirdupois:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1896%20Life%20of%20Brian%20Houghton%20Hodgson%20by%20WW%20Hunter%20s.pdf

### Analytic consequence

This is stronger than generic evidence about `packing` or `transport`. Hodgson is explicitly designing a **standard load unit as an interface between transport regimes**:

**Calcutta packing → bullock carriage → mountain-base transshipment → one-man back load**.

The package dimensions/weight are selected so that the same unit can pass between animal and human carriers without being opened and repacked. A container/load unit therefore reduces handling operations and the error/damage risk generated at transshipment points.

This is a direct historical instance of **material standardisation for interoperability**.

## 2. `Transport unit ≠ disciplinary unit` now has a second, more technical meaning

Wave 9 showed that trunks and boxes could mix Buddhist texts, statistics, drawings, lists and other materials. The 1857 commerce text adds another dimension: a transport unit is defined not by disciplinary content but by **the physical capacities and interfaces of the transport system**.

This suggests separating two container problems:

1. **epistemic grouping** — which things travel together because their relations need to be preserved;
2. **logistical modularity** — which package size/weight can move unchanged across porters, bullocks, roads and mountain paths.

The same physical package can solve both at once, but they are analytically distinct.

## 3. Foucher's 1866 date is demonstrably a container-derived chronology error

A later JRAS `Notes and News` correction is much more explicit than the previous wave's summary. It reports that Alfred Foucher said, in a parenthesis on p. 6 of his catalogue, that **according to a note found in the case**, the collection of paintings had been sent to the Institut in **1866**. Foucher strengthened the late date by noting that explanatory manuscript notes were found in a **separate case** from the paintings.

JRAS correction:
https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DE9E75AAC85D8EA69B2A44A7E84A71AB/S0035869X00146805a.pdf/iii_notes_and_news.pdf

But Barthélemy Saint-Hilaire had already described the **whole collection** in the *Journal des Savants* in February–March 1863, including paintings, drawings, copies by Rajmānsinh, and Sanskrit explanatory legends by Amṛitananda. The collection therefore cannot have first arrived in 1866.

RAS records for Hodgson's own retained copies of Saint-Hilaire:
- `BHH/19/3`, *Journal des Savants: Fevrier 1863*, with Hodgson annotations;
- `BHH/19/4`, *Journal des Savants: Mars 1863*, second and final article.

Catalogue evidence:
https://www.royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?page=7&showAdvanced=1&sort=identifier&sortDir=asc&subjects=643&topLod=0

### Mechanism

This is an unusually clean instance of the exact failure mode the article is trying to explain:

**later case note → treated as shipment-date evidence → case separation makes explanatory material appear to have a different transport history → erroneous chronology enters catalogue**.

The error is not simply `bad cataloguing`. It is a failure to distinguish:

- date of inscription on a container/note;
- date of a particular repacking;
- date of one component's movement;
- date of institutional arrival of the larger assemblage.

Container chronology must therefore be recorded as its own provenance layer rather than equated with object chronology.

## 4. The Institut collection had already become physically partitioned by Foucher's time

The JRAS correction matters because it reveals a later state of the collection: paintings and explanatory manuscript notes were physically separated into different cases. That separation was epistemically consequential because it affected Foucher's reconstruction of arrival date.

This means later storage did more than preserve or damage objects. It could **change the inferential graph** connecting objects, annotations and shipment events.

For the census, add fields for:
- current container;
- earlier container identifier/note;
- evidence for repacking;
- whether associated explanatory material is currently co-located;
- chronology inferred from container evidence;
- independent chronology from correspondence/receipt/publication.

## 5. Foucher explicitly distinguishes paintings from `liasses de dessins`

A modern study by David Waterhouse reproduces Foucher's wording. Foucher says that the paintings must be distinguished from the **bundles (`liasses`) of drawings** Hodgson also sent to the Institut. These drawings were mostly reproductions/copies in pencil or India ink by indigenous draughtsmen after reliefs or paintings in Nepalese temples.

Each drawing had at least two additional identity/evidence layers:

- an attached Sanskrit note from Hodgson's pandits;
- a name written in European form.

Waterhouse PDF (quotation from Foucher, p. 2):
https://www.discoveringbuddha.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2005-Origins-of-Himalayan-Studies-Brian-H-Hodgson-by-Waterhouse.pdf

Thus the drawing was already an **assembled documentary witness** rather than a visually self-sufficient image:

**image copy + Sanskrit explanatory note + European-form identifier**.

This supports the article's `conversion device` claim, but more precisely: the sheet/bundle carries parallel representational systems intended to keep a distant viewer able to identify and interpret the image.

## 6. Foucher also records a change in the accepted evidential medium

In the same passage, Foucher says that after roughly half a century the interest of these drawings had declined and that `photographies` were now required; he additionally suspected Hodgson's draughtsmen of allowing too much `fantaisie`.

This should be treated as historical evidence for a change in **verification regime**, not accepted as a neutral evaluation of indigenous drawing accuracy.

The sequence is analytically important:

**portable drawing as witness → later distrust of draughtsman's mediation → photograph elevated as preferred witness**.

This extends the project beyond physical survival. An object can survive perfectly while the medium in which it encodes evidence loses epistemic standing.

## 7. `Safe arrival` is a repeated custody-verification operation

The phrase is no longer an isolated courtesy formula.

### 1837: Société Asiatique manuscripts

The RAS catalogue records Burnouf's letter of 15 July 1837 acknowledging the **safe arrival of three cases of manuscripts**. Jules Mohl separately reported on 29 July that the three crates had arrived.

RAS catalogue:
https://www.royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/informationobject/browse?page=9&showAdvanced=1&sort=alphabetic&sortDir=asc&subjects=643&topLod=0

This is notable because receipt is redundantly acknowledged by two institutional actors.

### 1858: Institut de France Buddhist image/text gift

Wave 9 established a route redesign after a paper sent by the British Embassy failed to reach the Institut: clearing agent → Mitchell/Mohl → waterproof box → Molini → forwarding → later acknowledgement of two parcels at the Académie.

The change of route was therefore explicitly driven by an earlier **non-arrival**.

### 1870: ornithological box

Hodgson's 11 February 1870 letter to G. F. L. Marshall lists the contents of the `big deal box` and ends with an instruction to `let me hear of safe arrival of the box`.

ZSL record with transcription:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=12410&mediatypes=137&names=4543&places=61275&sf_culture=pt&showAdvanced=1&sort=referenceCode&sortDir=desc&topLod=0&view=table

The memorandum associated with the shipment specifies **1,104 sheets of drawings** plus manuscript lists, a painter's Hindi list, bird-behaviour volumes derived from shikari testimony, memoranda and printed catalogues.

### Provisional interpretation

`Safe arrival` functions as a minimal **custody checksum**. It verifies that an expected transport unit has crossed a risky interval and entered a new custody state. It does **not** verify completeness of every item, correct internal identity, condition, successful unpacking, or later cataloguing.

Hence the workflow should distinguish:

**dispatch → safe-arrival acknowledgement → unpacking → inventory reconciliation → sorting/comparison → catalogue/publication**.

Receipt is a stage, not the endpoint of circulation.

## 8. Preservation chemistry remains unresolved, but a useful contemporaneous formulation surfaced

Targeted public searches for Hodgson-specific arsenic, arsenical soap, spirits, oils or other chemical preservatives did not yet produce a direct Hodgson instruction or receipt.

However, a Hodgson zoological memorandum from around 1845 says that, after the specimens and drawings were presented to the British Museum, the desideratum was to ascertain that `the whole are procured and properly preserved`.

ZSL catalogue/transcription:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=12410&mediatypes=137&names=8774&page=11&sf_culture=en&showAdvanced=1&sort=endDate&sortDir=desc&topLod=0

This is useful because it separates **delivery** from **proper preservation** in Hodgson's own material accounting. It still does not identify the chemical means of preservation.

Do not infer arsenic/spirits from contemporary natural-history norms until Hodgson-network evidence is located.

## 9. New evidence on the scale of zoological redistribution strengthens the auditability problem

A ZSL description of formal specimen lists reports 803 mammals, 4,425 birds, 663 bird skins, and 21 human skulls with origins. Other Hodgson memoranda record redistribution among the British Museum, India House, Leiden, Paris, Berlin, Frankfurt, Edinburgh, Dublin, Newcastle, Manchester, Earl Derby and others.

ZSL:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/formal-copies-of-lists-of-skins-skeletons-and-skulls-sent-to-british-museum

This quantity matters methodologically. At this scale, `identity` cannot be maintained by personal memory or visual familiarity. Lists, numbers, labels, duplicate tables and receipts are constitutive infrastructure for later reconstruction.

The project should therefore treat numerical redundancy not as clerical clutter but as an error-control technology.

## 10. Revised mechanism: interoperability, custody checksum, and container chronology

Wave 10 sharpens three mechanisms that should now be kept distinct:

1. **interoperable load unit** — a package designed to cross transport regimes without being opened/repacked;
2. **custody checksum** — acknowledgement such as `safe arrival` that confirms a transport unit has reached the next custody node;
3. **container chronology** — dates/notes attached to boxes, cases or packets that may record packing/repacking/storage events rather than the chronology of the objects inside.

These mechanisms interact. Standardised loads can reduce repacking; custody acknowledgements can confirm movement without confirming internal integrity; later container notes can preserve one event while obscuring earlier ones.
