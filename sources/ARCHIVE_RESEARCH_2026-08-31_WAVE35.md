# Archive research wave 35 — paper regimes, paper-on-paper knowledge, copy-state conversion, and size-based archival reordering

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: deliberate scope reset after Waves 29–34. Those zoological/custody waves remain evidentially useful, but their purpose is subordinated again to the project's primary history-of-science question: how paper and other documentary surfaces constituted, differentiated, transported and later rearranged Hodgson's knowledge objects.

## Executive result

The Hodgson archive supports a stronger paper-centred argument than a simple contrast between `European machine paper` and `Nepali hand-made paper`.

The key finding is that **paper type, format and physical size repeatedly mark operations and documentary states rather than stable disciplinary identities**.

Four mechanisms now stand out:

1. `PAPER_ON_PAPER_KNOWLEDGE_INTERFACE`: Nepali paper can be the object being described, sampled, costed and transported while the descriptive/accounting instrument itself is machine-made paper.
2. `SUPPORT_CHANGE_AS_COPY_STATE_CONVERSION`: the same intellectual or documentary object can move across palm leaf, Nepali hand-made paper, machine-made paper and mixed-paper copy states.
3. `MIXED_SUPPORT_AS_PROCESS_LAYERING`: machine and hand-made paper can coexist within one research dossier or even one item, often alongside different hands, scripts, translations or copy states.
4. `PAPER_DIMENSION_AS_ARCHIVAL_RECLASSIFIER`: in 1921 India Office Library binding decisions used physical dimensions as an ordering principle, physically separating originals from translations/copies when they were written on differently sized sheets.

The article should therefore treat paper neither as a symbolic marker of cultural origin nor as inert support. Paper becomes an **operational interface whose material properties, formats and dimensions help generate documentary states and later archival relations**.

## 1. Scope reset: zoological and custody evidence must answer a paper-history question

Waves 29–34 reconstructed:

- numbered drawing systems;
- multiple metadata surfaces;
- working vs finished drawing states;
- lists and catalogues as mutable ledgers;
- negative receipts and later recovery;

These mechanisms remain central only insofar as they show what paper surfaces do:

- drawings preserve visual and numeric relations;

- receipts externalize custody transitions;
- working notes preserve revision states;

- copied or rebound documents can break or rebuild those joins.

The project is not becoming a general institutional history of Hodgson's zoology.

### Governing question restored

**How did paper manufacture, paper selection, sheet format, copy state, inscription, folding, binding, packaging, circulation and later archival rebinding shape what could count as stable, revisable and reconstructible scientific knowledge?**

## 2. 1831 — paper about paper is written on another paper regime

### Source

`MSS EUR HODGSON/6, ff.77–81`.

The British Library Hodgson Papers inventory describes this as Hodgson's December 1831 letter to H. T. Prinsep regarding:

- a **sample of Nepali paper**;
- paper-making in Nepal;
- an attached note on manufacture.

The surviving letter itself is catalogued as **machine-made paper, very fine condition**.

### Mechanism

Code:

`PAPER_ON_PAPER_KNOWLEDGE_INTERFACE`

### Operational structure

`Nepali hand-made paper = sampled / described technological object`

while

`machine-made paper = correspondence/reporting surface through which that object is made legible to distant administration/scholarship`.

### Historiographical implication

The relevant contrast is not:

`indigenous paper vs European paper`.

It is:

`paper-as-object` versus `paper-as-reporting interface`.

The same knowledge event requires both.

## 3. 1843 — machine-made paper administratively prices Nepali paper

### Source

`MSS EUR HODGSON/12, f.117`.

The inventory describes a bill made out to Dr Nathaniel Wallich for:

- **1,012 bundles (ḍhyāp) of Nepali paper**;
- ten sheets per bundle;
- two price classes/rates;
- three pieces of wax cloth;
- thread and twine;
- total cost in Nepali and Indian rupees.

The bill itself is on **machine-made paper**, signed by Hodgson at the Nepal Residency.

### Mechanisms

- `PAPER_ON_PAPER_ACCOUNTING_INTERFACE`
- `LOCAL_PAPER_AS_QUANTIFIED_COMMODITY`
- `PACKAGING_MATERIAL_COACCOUNTING`

### Important point

Nepali paper becomes institutionally operable not only through its fibre properties but through another documentary surface that turns it into:

`bundle count + sheets per bundle + price + protective packing materials + monetary conversion`.

The machine-made bill does not replace the Nepali paper. It makes a large quantity of it administratively addressable.

## 4. The same 1843 sheet is reused for unrelated administrative knowledge

The inventory states that the **verso of the paper bill** carries a brief note on Nepal army battalions and troop numbers.

### Mechanism

Code:

`VERSO_REUSE_AS_SURFACE_REALLOCATION`

The sheet therefore moves through at least two functions:

`paper/material procurement account`

and

`military-administrative note`.

### Analytical implication

Paper does not belong intrinsically to one subject domain. A sheet can become a scarce/reusable office surface whose empty area is reassigned.

This is a direct warning against reconstructing historical genres only from later archival subject headings.

## 5. Paper manufacture already extends beyond the sheet into transport ecology

The existing `paper_experiment_census_v0_1.json` reconstructs the 1841–42 Darjeeling/Serampore sequence.

Key operations include:

- transfer of five skilled Nepali paper makers;
- workshop siting near bark, ash-producing woods and flowing water;
- wet-bark carriage causing mildew and complete loss of manufacturability;
- rejection of iron boiler contact because it darkened paper;
- bleaching tests trading whiteness against toughness;
- chemical handoff to O'Shaughnessy;
- filtration tests where strength did not guarantee speed or clarity;
- India-rubber waterproofing for postal wrapping;
- Serampore machine-web failure caused by fibre/mucilage behaviour.

### General mechanism retained

`AFFORDANCE_REVERSAL`

A material property has no stable value outside a specific technical interface.

### Paper-history implication

The paper chain begins before writing:

`plant/fibre -> drying -> transport -> workshop -> water/alkali/metal contact -> sheet-forming -> finishing -> treatment -> writing/wrapping/filtering -> circulation`.

The article should integrate manufacturing and documentary use instead of treating them as separate chapters.

## 6. Machine-made paper is not a simple marker of official/scientific authority

The BL inventory repeatedly finds machine-made paper across:

- political correspondence;
- legal questionnaires;
- Sanskrit Buddhist extracts;
- Urdu/Persian translations;
- zoological lists;
- sketches and mandalas;
- vocabulary notes;
- manuscript-purchase accounts;
- rough working papers.

Examples:

- `vol.6/6`: paper-manufacture correspondence itself;
- `vol.29/5`: list of Buddhist objects shipped to Calcutta;
- `vol.58/9`: standardized district questionnaire;
- `vol.104/1`: Urdu-English terms in pencil on **machine-made coloured paper**;
- `vol.28/11` and `vol.28/14`: Buddhist mandala/sketch work on **machine-made broad-sheet paper**.

### Mechanism

Code:

`MACHINE_PAPER_CROSS_DOMAIN_USE`

### Rule

Do not infer:

`machine-made = official/scientific/final`.

The same support appears in rough, translated, visual, linguistic, bureaucratic and religious work.

- `vol.94/7`: shipment-content list with bundle numbers;
- `vol.104/27–28`: route descriptions;
- `vol.58/3`: animals and animal bones;

Code:

`HANDMADE_PAPER_CROSS_DOMAIN_USE`

### Rule

Do not infer:

`Nepali hand-made = religious/local/original`.

It can be fiscal, scientific, administrative, copied, translated or archival.

## 8. Mixed paper within one item is a historical signal

Multiple BL entries explicitly say:

- `machine-made and Nepali hand-made paper`;
- `mixed paper`;
- different hands on different paper types.

Examples include:

- `vol.29/4`: Buddha-name excerpts with English/Farsi transcriptions;
- `vol.29/10`: Mahayana and ritual materials;
- `vol.60/7`: law/justice material in different hands;
- `vol.94/6`: Tibetan scripture/vocabulary list with rough and excellent hands;
- `vol.101/3`, `vol.101/5`;
- multiple route/history files in `vol.104`.

### Mechanism

Code:

`MIXED_SUPPORT_AS_PROCESS_LAYERING`

### Research hypothesis

Mixed support may encode one or more of:

- different production moments;
- different actors/hands;
- original plus translation;
- local note plus imported-paper fair copy;
- later addition;
- replacement leaf;
- differing sheet availability.

The correlation must be reconstructed item by item.

## 9. Route knowledge gives a controlled test for support/copy-state relations

`MSS EUR HODGSON/104` is especially valuable because similar routes survive in more than one documentary state.

The inventory includes:

- Kathmandu–Likhughat route on **machine-made and Nepali hand-made paper**;
- another Kathmandu–Likhughat state on **machine-made brittle paper**;
- Nuwakot–Battardanda material on mixed machine/Nepali paper;
- a corresponding route version on machine-made paper;
- routes reported by named local hunter Prem Bishwas in these copy clusters.

### Mechanism

Code:

`ROUTE_COPY_STATE_SUPPORT_VARIATION`

### High-value test

Instead of asking whether route science was written on `European` or `Nepali` paper, collate the two states and ask:

- which is closer to oral/field report?
- which is translation?
- which is fair copy?
- which contains Hodgson title/annotation?
- which was intended for preservation, circulation or publication?
- does support change coincide with language or hand change?

This is a direct paper-based history of knowledge conversion.

## 10. Palm leaf -> Nepali paper is an explicit media conversion

### Source

`MSS EUR HODGSON/29, ff.131–154`.

Twenty-four historical property documents originally written on **palm leaf** were copied onto **Nepali hand-made paper**.

The copies preserve:

- content;
- a list;
- rough sketches of seals.

### Mechanism

Code:

`PALM_LEAF_TO_PAPER_SURROGATE`

### Implication

The copy is not simply a duplicate text. It converts an older, place-bound support into a nineteenth-century portable paper witness while selectively reproducing visual authenticity cues such as seals.

The operation is:

`old support + text + seal morphology -> paper surrogate + sketch metadata`.

This belongs at the centre of the article's authentication argument.

## 11. Haritāl-pasted paper is a distinct copying technology

### Sources

`MSS EUR HODGSON/29, ff.162–163` preserves copies of two historical inscriptions on **haritāl-pasted Nepali hand-made paper**.

`MSS EUR HODGSON/30` also includes several Buddhist/caste texts on **Nepali haritāl-pasted hand-made paper in pothi book form**.

### Mechanism

Code:

`TREATED_PAPER_AS_COPYING_SURFACE`

### Importance

Paper type must include treatment, not only fibre origin.

Fields should distinguish:

- base support;
- surface treatment/coating;
- colour;
- format;
- intended graphic/textual operation.

Haritāl treatment may alter visual contrast, writing behaviour or imitation of older manuscript aesthetics. Its function requires material/technical verification rather than symbolic assumption.

## 12. Broad-sheet machine paper functions as a visual-expansion surface

`MSS EUR HODGSON/28, ff.42–43`: Kalachakramandala drawing on **machine-made broad-sheet paper**.

`MSS EUR HODGSON/28, ff.49–50`: Dharmadhatu/Vajradhatu and Dharmachakra mandala sketch work on **machine-made broad-sheet paper**.

Code:

`FORMAT_AFFORDANCE_FOR_VISUAL_COMPLEXITY`

### Hypothesis

Broad-sheet format may matter more than national origin of paper because it supplies expanded planar space for diagrammatic relations.

Test against:

- ordinary letter sheets;
- pothi-format hand-made paper;

The inventory records:

- dimensions c. 24.8 × 20 cm;
- **two types of imported paper, thick and thin**;

- many corrections and addenda;

### Mechanism

Code:

`THICKNESS_VARIATION_WITHIN_WORKING_DOSSIER`

### Research question

Does thick/thin paper correlate with:

- base draft vs inserted addition?
- replacement sheet?
- chronological phase?
- availability?
- emphasis or attachment?

The item gives a controlled object for testing whether thickness has an operational meaning.

## 14. Paper dimensions became an archival classification operator in 1921

### Source

The Hodgson Papers collection introduction states that in **1921** the India Office Library bound the papers into 95 volumes.

Ordering decisions were made partly according to the **physical dimensions of each document**, with similarly sized items bound together.

The catalogue explicitly notes a consequence:

- documents that belonged together intellectually, such as an original manuscript and its translation, could be bound in different places **simply because they were written on different sizes of paper**.

### Mechanisms

- `PAPER_DIMENSION_AS_ARCHIVAL_RECLASSIFIER`
- `SIZE_BASED_RELATIONAL_BREAK`
- `LATER_BINDING_AS_EPISTEMIC_INTERVENTION`

### Major history-of-science point

A paper property that may have been incidental or functional at production stage later becomes an institutional ordering rule.

`production-time sheet size -> 1921 binding criterion -> original/translation separation -> altered archival adjacency`.

Physical dimensions therefore have an afterlife in knowledge organization.

## 15. Archive binding performs an affordance reversal at another temporal scale

At production stage, different paper sizes may serve:

- letter writing;
- route tables;
- drawings;
- translations;
- pothi imitation;
- appendices.

In 1921 those same differences become reasons to physically separate related documents.

### Mechanism

Code:

`ARCHIVAL_AFFORDANCE_REVERSAL`

A material difference that supported distinct original functions later obstructs relational preservation.

This extends the paper experiment concept beyond manufacturing:

`same material property -> different value under different institutional interface`.

## 16. Condition is another historical paper state, not merely conservation noise

BL descriptions distinguish:

- very fine/fine condition;
- brittle;
- becoming brittle;
- heavily brittled/rotten;
- restored;
- poor quality vs good quality.

Example:

`vol.104/32`, a route copy, is explicitly **machine-made brittle paper**.

### Mechanism

Code:

`PAPER_AGING_AS_ACCESS_STATE`

### Research implication

Physical ageing can affect:

- handling frequency;
- rebinding/restoration;
- availability for comparison;
- later digitization;
- survival of marginal additions.

Do not treat conservation condition as external to epistemic afterlife.

## 17. Whatman watermark identification: use as provenance evidence, not prestige shorthand

The user's Whatman reference corpus supplies important control rules.

Balston notes that after the 1806 split:

- Hollingworth at Turkey Mill habitually used `J WHATMAN TURKEY MILL` on hand-made paper through 1859;
- Balston at Springfield used `J WHATMAN` and related marks.

Balston also warns that Whatman countermarks were widely forged in the nineteenth century.

### Mechanisms

- `WATERMARK_AS_PROVENANCE_HYPOTHESIS`
- `BRAND_MARK_NONUNIQUE_AUTHENTICITY`

### Rule for Hodgson archive

A visible `J WHATMAN`, `J WHATMAN TURKEY MILL`, `W`, posthorn or related mark must be recorded as:

`observed mark -> candidate maker/mill/date range -> compare chain lines / countermark / sheet format -> confidence`.

Never write:

`Whatman watermark = unquestionably Whatman paper`.

### Current archive status

The BL online inventory does not provide a systematic watermark census. Watermark claims must therefore come from page-level inspection of originals/scans, not from catalogue silence.

## 19. Corrected autograph is a paper-state problem, not merely a textual variant

The 1860 `Notice on Buddhist Symbols` scan is explicitly labelled `corrected autograph` in the surviving printed/material witness.

### Material research questions

- are corrections manuscript on printed paper?
- are they proof corrections, authorial copy corrections, or archive annotations?
- what paper stock carries the printed article?
- do pasted/inserted corrections alter the sheet surface?
- how do corrections relate to the separate symbol sheets Hodgson says he transmitted?

### Mechanism

Code:

`CORRECTION_LAYER_ON_PRINTED_PAPER_STATE`

The analytical object is not `text before/after correction` alone, but:

`printed support -> authorial inscription -> new hybrid witness state`.

## 20. Translation-ready Lepcha copies are a designed paper layout

The SOAS/India Office descriptive witness says Hodgson's Lepcha copies were prepared with:

- Lepcha text occupying part of the page;
- blank space reserved on the **right** for future translation;
- Nepalese translation already inserted in places.

### Mechanism

Code:

`PAPER_LAYOUT_AS_FUTURE_OPERATION_RESERVE`

### Paper-history formulation

Blank paper is not absence. It is allocated capacity.

The page is designed as:

`current text zone + future translation zone`.

This should be compared directly with:

- margins reserved for correction;
- broad-sheet diagram space;
- verso reuse;
- inserted slips/addenda.

## Bottom line

The strongest Hodgson paper history is not a binary story about European versus Nepali paper. The archive repeatedly shows one paper regime describing, buying, copying or reorganizing another; the same intellectual object moving across support types; different papers coexisting inside one research process; blank sheet space reserving future work; broad sheets enabling complex visual comparison; and later archivists using sheet dimensions to physically separate documents that had originally belonged together. Paper's historical force lies in these changes of operation and interface. It is simultaneously manufactured material, experimental object, writing surface, copy technology, accounting unit, package component, spatial design and archival sorting criterion.
