# BL Hodgson inventory — paper, copying, binding, packaging, and circulation scrape

Working source: the online **Hodgson Papers: Inventory of Hodgson's private papers at the British Library** (historically produced as the descriptive catalogue of the BL Hodgson Collection; current pages hosted at `hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk`). The current BL Archives and Manuscripts catalogue independently identifies the fonds as `Mss Eur Hodgson`.

This file is a first high-signal scrape, not yet an exhaustive extraction of all 972 catalogue items.

## 1. Why this catalogue is unusually useful for a material history

The inventory routinely records **paper type, paper quality/condition, format, hand, ink/pencil, copy-state, binding, and physical arrangement** alongside intellectual content. At collection level it also states that the India Office Library bound the papers in 1921 partly by **physical dimensions**: items of similar size were bound together, which in some cases separated originals from translations/copies because they were written on different sizes of paper. This makes the modern archive itself an additional layer of material re-ordering.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/intro.html`

Key catalogue vocabulary already observed:
- `Machine-made paper`
- `Nepali hand-made paper`
- `Machine-made and Nepali hand-made paper`
- `imported paper, thick and thin`
- `machine-made coloured paper`
- `machine-made, broad-sheet paper`
- paper described as `poor quality`, `good quality`, `very thin`, `becoming brittle`, `heavily brittled and rotten`, `properly restored`
- `loose leaves`, `pre-bound notebooks`, sheets/folios with attached smaller pieces, mixed hands, corrections/addenda, pencil additions

These are not incidental conservation notes: they let us test whether paper regimes correlate with genre, copy-state, intended destination, linguistic mediation, bureaucratic/scientific authority, and transport.

## 2. Direct paper-manufacture evidence

### Mss Eur Hodgson/6, item 6, ff. 77–81 (Dec. 1831)
**Letter from Hodgson to H. T. Prinsep regarding a sample of Nepali paper and paper making in Nepal.** The catalogue describes the surviving letter as machine-made paper in very fine condition and notes an attached account of paper-making.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol006.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/6, item 9, ff. 181–184 (July 1841)
A. Campbell to J. R. Colvin. At Lord Auckland's request, Hodgson **sent a group of Nepalis skilled in traditional local paper making**; Campbell reports on the arrival of the five-person party in Darjeeling, enquiries and experiments, and their monthly salaries. This is a technology-transfer episode, not merely a description of local craft.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol006.html`

## 3. Procurement of paper and binding/packing materials

### Mss Eur Hodgson/12, item 3, f. 117 (1843)
**Bill made out to Dr Wallich for Nepali paper and book-binding material.** Signed by Hodgson at Nepal Residency.

Catalogue summary:
- total: Nepali Rupees 79.30 (Indian Rupees 61.12)
- **1,012 bundles (ḍhyāp) of Nepali paper**
- 10 sheets per bundle
- different paper prices recorded
- **3 pieces of wax cloth**
- **thread and twine**
- cross-reference to manuscript-production bills and notes on paper manufacture

This is a concrete material bill of quantities and should be treated as a central source.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol012.html`

## 4. Manuscript production as labour + logistics

### Mss Eur Hodgson/12, item 8, ff. 128–130 (April, June, Nov. 1827)
**Bills for manuscripts sent to Dr Wallich in Calcutta.** The catalogue explicitly itemises the activity as:
- copying
- binding
- bundling
- packaging
- sending by shipment to Calcutta
- purchasing local paper and related materials
- wages of **carpenters**
- wages of regular **labourers**
- wages of **porters**
- price of local materials

This is probably the strongest single source for turning the project from “paper as support” into **knowledge objects made transportable through skilled labour and packaging**.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol012.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/29, item 18, ff. 160c (1827)
Receipt from Pandit Amritananda to Hodgson for the **cost of copying Buddhist and Hindu scriptures**.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol029.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/12, item 19, ff. 159–166 (1824)
W. Carey correspondence records prices paid for copying manuscripts: **Rs. 2 per 100 stanzas**; also discusses Hodgson's lists of sacred writings and works/curiosities presented onward.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol012.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/12, items 20–21, ff. 167–173 (1825)
Government sanction for purchasing Oriental manuscripts. One stage of the scheme proposed sending **weapons and equipment worth 1,000 rupees to barter for Nepali religious manuscripts**, later replaced by money for purchases. This ties manuscript acquisition to colonial fiscal/procurement machinery.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol012.html`

## 6. Long-distance copying and forwarding as a chain

### Mss Eur Hodgson/94, item 6, ff. 33–39
List of Tibetan scriptures/deities/vocabulary with a colophon-like description. The catalogue says it records Hodgson's suggestion to the Dalai Lama to have the **Kagyur and Tangyur (100 volumes) copied and sent to the palace of Nepal through Tibetan businessmen for delivery to Hodgson**. It describes the process of **finding, locating, copying and forwarding** copies from Lhasa to Kathmandu.

### Mss Eur Hodgson/94, item 7, ff. 40–44
Separated leaves containing the contents of manuscripts **shipped from Kathmandu**. The list preserves **bundle numbers and categories** for an original shipment.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol094.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/98, item 4, ff. 57–64 (6 July 1830)
Description of the **content and copying of the Tangyur for Hodgson**, including the visit of Tenjin Norbu for delivery and details of the **costs and process of copying** under Tibetan patronage.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol098.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/102
Volume-level description includes lists of manuscripts purchased and sent to Calcutta, and lists of Newari/Tibetan books purchased and sent to Fort William College. Item 20, f. 114, records a list of books sent to Calcutta on 31 Jan. 1829, including **number of pages, verses, and expenditure**.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol102.html`

## 7. Copy-state, substitution, and material transformations

These items are immediately relevant to authentication / textual authority because the archive repeatedly distinguishes originals from copies and records changes of support.

### Mss Eur Hodgson/29, item 14, ff. 131–154
**Copies of 24 land documents originally written on palm leaf**, recopied onto **Nepali hand-made paper**. This is an explicit media conversion from older documentary support to nineteenth-century paper.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol029.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/29, item 20, ff. 162–163
Copies of two historical inscriptions on **haritāl-pasted Nepali hand-made paper**. Useful for asking how visual/material features of inscriptions were translated into portable documentary surrogates.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol029.html`

### Mss Eur Hodgson/55, item 20, ff. 114–172 (1802–1854)
Originals and copies of royal orders and official notes/letters, mostly on Nepali hand-made paper but with some machine-made paper; includes original red- and black-sealed orders. This gives a controlled corpus for comparing **originality, seal, support, copy, and evidentiary status**.

Source: `https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol055.html`

## 8. Paper regimes crossing genre boundaries

A few diagnostic examples:

- `vol.1/4`, ff. 216–223: Hodgson to Governor-General, 20 Dec. 1834; **two types of imported paper, thick and thin**, locally produced ink, extensive corrections/addenda, Hodgson's own hand; dimensions 24.8 × 20 cm. (`vol001.html`)
- `vol.18/12`, ff. 221–269b: Hodgson's 1828 essay on Buddhist languages/literature/religion on **two types of machine-made paper**, with corrections/addenda, extra pencil pages and signature. (`vol018.html`)
- `vol.28/11`, ff. 42–43: Kalachakramandala drawing on **machine-made broad-sheet paper**. (`vol028.html`)
- `vol.29/4`: textual excerpts on a mixture of **machine-made and Nepali hand-made paper**. (`vol029.html`)
- `vol.58/3`, ff. 4–14: list of **animals and animal bones** on Nepali hand-made paper, some pencil writing. (`vol058.html`)
- `vol.60/7`, ff. 39–132: law/justice material on **Nepali hand-made and machine-made paper**, different hands. (`vol060.html`)
- `vol.94/1`: rough Mahayana notes on both Nepali hand-made and machine-made paper. (`vol094.html`)
- `vol.104/1`: selected Urdu terms on **machine-made coloured paper**, pencil. (`vol104.html`)

This already warns against a simple equation such as “European/machine paper = science/officiality, Nepali paper = indigenous/religious.” The two supports coexist, are mixed within single dossiers, and cross administrative, ethnographic, linguistic, religious, geographic and natural-historical genres. Any functional claim has to be demonstrated case by case.

## 9. Immediate research architecture suggested by the scrape

The strongest object is no longer “paper” in isolation but a chain:

**procure fibres/paper → select support → copy/write/draw → collate and count leaves/verses → authenticate/label → bind → bundle/package → protect with materials (wax cloth, twine, etc.) → hire labour/porters → move through routes and intermediaries → receive/receipt → institutional reclassification and rebinding**.

The archive can support at least four linked questions:

1. **Material differentiation**: what distinctions did actors make between imported/machine-made and Nepali hand-made supports, and when did those distinctions matter?
2. **Reliability and authentication**: how were originals, copies, seals, colophons, palaeographic/material features, and recopying onto new supports used to stabilise or contest textual authority?
3. **Making books transportable**: what labour and materials turned loose leaves/manuscripts into boxes, bundles and packages that could survive Nepal–Calcutta–London circulation?
4. **Knowledge logistics**: how did merchants, pandits, carpenters, porters, Residency staff, colleges, libraries and scientific institutions form a distributed infrastructure for manuscript/scientific collection?

## 11. What is not yet established

- The inventory gives **direct evidence for bundling, packaging, wax cloth, thread/twine, boxes, packages, porters and shipment**.
- It does **not yet give a clear hit for cushioning/filling/padding materials** (straw, cotton, waste paper, etc.). That question should be pursued in correspondence, shipping bills, East India Company/Residency packing instructions, museum/specimen transport records, and contemporary manuscript/book trade practice rather than assumed.
- No systematic watermark census has yet been extracted from this site in this pass.
- This file is a targeted first scrape; a complete catalogue-wide paper census still needs to be run.
