# Web/archive research 2026-09-02 — Wave 59
## From battalion return to Board copy: the 1830 classification chain survives through an institutional copy architecture

Date: 2026-09-02

Status: archive/custody follow-up to Wave 58. This wave identifies the record-series status of `IOR/L/MIL/3/32` and connects the 1830 military-classification case to the article's larger paper/version/custody argument.

## Executive result

Wave 58 reconstructed the following epistemic/documentary chain through A.P. Coleman's archival citations:

`Bentinck classification request`
-> `Kennedy/Young local battalion returns`
-> `Bengal Military synthesis/letter to London`.

British Library series-level catalogue evidence now identifies the surviving London-side witness more precisely.

Coleman cites:

`I.O.R., M.D.R., L/MIL/3/32, letter no.52 of 6 Nov.1830`.

The British Library catalogue places `IOR/L/MIL/3/32` inside:

`IOR/L/MIL/3/1–78 — Military Letters from Bengal and India (Board's Copies), 1803–1858`.

Therefore the currently identified surviving witness of the 6 November 1830 Bengal Military letter is not simply an abstract `original letter`. It belongs to the **Board of Control copy series**.

The larger `IOR/L/MIL/3` administrative history further states that military correspondence was historically kept in parallel sets at East India House and the Board of Control; after the Company's and Board's demise, duplicated records were extensively weeded. For military letters from India before 1858, the Board copies were retained in preference to the Company's set.

Thus the survival path is itself selective:

`administrative transmission created duplicate institutional record branches`
-> `later archival weeding removed parts of that duplication`
-> `the Board-copy branch became the preferred surviving series`.

Mechanisms:

- `GOVERNANCE_COPY_IS_AN_OPERATIONAL_DOCUMENT_STATE`
- `PARALLEL_INSTITUTIONAL_RECORD_BRANCHES`
- `ARCHIVAL_WEEDING_SELECTS_SURVIVING_VERSION_BRANCH`
- `SURVIVING_ARCHIVE_COPY_IS_NOT_EQUIVALENT_TO UNIQUE_ORIGINAL`
- `RECORD_SERIES_ARCHITECTURE_IS_VERSION_HISTORY`.

## 3. Copying is part of governance, not post-facto archival noise

The British Library's series history says that separate military-letter series were maintained in London at both:

- East India House / Company headquarters;
- Board of Control.

This means duplication was generated by the governing architecture itself.

The copies were not merely later preservation surrogates. They were operational records required by divided institutional oversight.

The paper argument should therefore distinguish:

`document copy made to govern`
from
`copy made to preserve`.

The 1830 Bengal Military letter belongs to the first category before it later becomes an archival survivor.

## 4. Later archival survival remakes the original institutional ecology

British Library states that after the demise of the East India Company and Board of Control, duplicate records at the two former headquarters were extensively weeded.

For incoming military letters from India up to 1858, the Board copies were retained in preference to the Company set.

The extant archive therefore does not transparently reproduce the documentary ecology that existed in 1830.

Historical state:

`parallel Company + Board record sets`.

Later archival state:

`selectively retained Board-copy branch + surviving related proceedings/consultations + losses/weeded duplicates`.

The material archive has performed a version-selection operation.

Mechanisms:

- `CUSTODY_IS_VERSION_SELECTION`
- `WEEDING_COLLAPSES_PARALLEL_RECORD_BRANCHES`
- `ARCHIVAL_SURVIVAL_CAN_HIDE FORMER DUPLICATION`
- `SINGLE_SURVIVING_COPY_CAN_DESCEND_FROM MULTIPLE-COPY ADMINISTRATIVE ECOLOGY`.

## 5. The larger L/MIL series makes duplication even more explicit

The British Library notes that later India Office military correspondence can contain substantial duplication among:

- Board copies;
- Company fair copies;
- India Office fair copies;
- original drafts;
- multiple-copy incoming `originals`.

It also notes that the Military Department later removed enclosures from incoming correspondence and rebound them separately, creating further overlap in the enclosure series because incoming records themselves could exist in multiple copies.

This later procedure should not be projected automatically onto the specific 1830 letter. The dedicated Bengal/India enclosure subseries begins in 1835.

But at series level it demonstrates the general institutional mechanism:

**a correspondence file can be materially decomposed and redistributed after its original administrative use, while duplicate documentary branches complicate the relation between letter, enclosure and surviving volume.**

Mechanisms:

- `LETTER_ENCLOSURE_RELATION_CAN_BE_REMATERIALIZED_BY_ARCHIVE`
- `REBINDING_CHANGES_DOCUMENTARY_NEIGHBORHOOD`
- `MULTIPLE_ORIGINALS_COMPLICATE_ORIGINAL_COPY_BINARY`.

The 1830 military-classification episode is now a paper-copy history as well as a history of categories.
