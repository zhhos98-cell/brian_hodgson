## Finding 8 — 1851 Great Exhibition preserves Darjeeling paper as an imperial exhibition category

Evidence status: **SECURE CATALOGUE-LEVEL WITNESS; continuity inference NOT ALLOWED.**

The official Great Exhibition catalogue for India, Class 9 / paper-related exhibits, lists:

- paper from young bamboo in Assam;
- paper made with the bark of *Daphne cannabina*;
- **paper made in Nepal and at Darjeeling**;
- paper from Kashmir;
- several other experimental fibre papers.

Public catalogue witness:
https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/22079393.pdf

Consequence:

By 1851 `paper made ... at Darjeeling` remained legible enough within the imperial material-exhibition system to be catalogued as a distinct product/material category. This is a real afterlife for the 1841–42 experiment.

However, this source does **not** prove continuous manufacture from 1841 to 1851. The displayed material could have been older experimental sheets. Therefore the later Darjeeling Gazetteer claim that Campbell supplied writing and packing paper `for some years` remains unconfirmed at contemporaneous production level.

Source-control caution:

The nearby catalogue heading `Papers exhibited by Dr. Royle` must not be assigned automatically to the Darjeeling item without page-layout verification. Do not name Royle as exhibitor of this particular paper on the basis of OCR sequence alone.

Article use:

- useful as an epilogue or afterlife marker;
- do not use it to infer factory continuity, output, or commercial viability.

## Finding 10 — Hooker's 1849 correspondence shows a differentiated field-paper ecology sustained by carrying labour

Evidence status: **SECURE / direct digitized correspondence.**

The ZSL Hodgson correspondence makes paper supply a concrete field-science operation rather than an abstract use case.

Key points already closed across the 1849 Hooker letters:

- Hooker distinguishes white paper for drawing from white paper for specimen tickets and from `Nepal or brown` paper used for drying plants and protecting the white papers in transit.
- A 5 July 1849 letter explicitly lists `Writing Paper` separately from the paper `for drying plants Nepal or brown`.
- Other letters request another `load` of Nepal paper and even `6 loads`, immediately tying paper demand to the availability of coolies.
- On 12 July 1849 Hooker reports that the long-awaited coolies finally arrived with the paper he had been anxiously waiting for.
- On 18 October 1849 he says there was a problem with Hodgson's people sent to Nepal for paper: his own men had gone and returned in seventeen days, whereas another party had produced `neither men, paper or money`.

Direct catalogue/transcription witnesses include:
https://zsl-archive.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index.php/informationobject/browse?media=print&names=8774&page=13&sf_culture=en&showAdvanced=1&sort=referenceCode&sortDir=asc&topLod=0&view=table

and the dated Hooker items already logged in the first deep-dive file.

Consequence:

The scientifically important afterlife of Nepal paper does not depend on proving that Campbell's Darjeeling factory survived. By 1849 Nepal-made paper was still worth organizing people, money, loads and routes around because it performed specific field operations.

Do not flatten this into `Nepal paper = botanical paper`. Hooker's expedition used a **paper ecology** with distinct supports for drawing, labels/tickets, pressing/drying and protection/transport.

Actor-level implication:

Paper demand directly creates labour demand. The relevant history is:

`need for paper -> Hodgson/Campbell coordination -> people/coolies -> procurement or stores -> bundle/load -> field camp -> drying/protective use`.

A modern reconstruction says seven porters were engaged simply to carry paper for pressing/drying plants. Treat that number as **SECONDARY** until tied to Hooker's own published narrative or account; the direct correspondence already proves the labour dependence without needing the number.

## Finding 11 — Serampore's `machine web` was a concrete continuous papermaking system, not an abstract sign of mechanisation

Evidence status: **STRONG near-primary technical reconstruction; exact machine-name calibration separate from process description.**

William Carey's answer to an 1832 Agricultural and Horticultural Society inquiry, reproduced in later biographical publication, distinguishes three paper-production regimes at Serampore:

1. early local hand manufacture using native papermakers and mat frames;
2. an English hand method using fine-wire frames and sheet-by-sheet formation;
3. the then-current machine method, in which pulp **ran continuously onto a wire web**, passed over **several cylinders**, and reached a final **steam-heated cylinder**, yielding paper in roughly two minutes from the liquid state.

This description gives the operational environment needed to read Marshman's 1842 sequel.

Consequence:

When Marshman reports that the mucilaginous Nepal/Darjeeling pulp would not run freely on the `machine web`, the failure is specifically a mismatch with a continuous-feed production regime requiring regular pulp flow, web formation, cylinder passage and rapid drying.

This sharpens the comparison with Hodgson's 1831/32 floating-frame account, where a maker could vary thickness by manually changing the amount and distribution of pulp on an individual frame.

Historical formulation:

`thicker/stronger sheet` in the floating-frame system was partly a skilled distribution problem;

`thicker/stronger sheet` in the Serampore continuous system became a furnish-flow and web-control problem.

Do not write `machine = scale-up`. The machine changed the conditions under which the material had to behave.

Open calibration:

- modern specialist scholarship identifies Serampore as having adopted a Fourdrinier machine by the early 1830s;
- for article prose, the historically secure operational description above is sufficient even if exact manufacturer/model remains unresolved.

## Finding 12 — Hodgson's 1831/32 intervention is not first discovery or first recipe; it is a major operational re-encoding

Evidence status: **STRONG COMPARATIVE FINDING; actor-source of Hodgson's knowledge remains OPEN.**

Murray/Wallich before Hodgson already described the paper plant and a concise manufacturing chain: strip/scrape bark, boil with ash, wash, beat on stone with mallets, disperse in water, and form sheets on a frame/mat. Raffles/Wallich correspondence also already imagined sending both finished paper and the material for making it to Britain.

Hodgson explicitly acknowledged Wallich's botanical priority. His 1831/32 account instead expands manufacture into a much more operational protocol, including:

- a detailed implement list;
- preparation and strength of ash liquor;
- boiling and beating sequence;
- churning/dispersal until stringiness is lost;
- screening out knots/lumps;
- a floating cloth-bottom frame;
- manual control of pulp distribution and sheet thickness;
- fire drying;
- optional polishing/burnishing;
- ordinary absence of sizing in the process he describes;
- large-sheet capability;
- commodity/market geography;
- office-record use;
- and especially drying processed pulp into brick/tile form for transport and remanufacture elsewhere.

Consequence:

The safest current formulation is not `Hodgson discovered Nepal papermaking` or `Hodgson first made the technique portable`.

Instead:

**Hodgson substantially re-encoded an already circulating Himalayan papermaking knowledge into a more explicit operational specification tied to specimens, administrative evaluation and the actual transport of processed pulp for remanufacture.**

Cowan's later British failure then becomes a historically powerful test of whether that operational encoding plus transported material was enough.

P0 actor gap remains:

- who supplied or demonstrated the Nepal-specific manufacturing knowledge behind Hodgson's 1831 account?
- did Hodgson observe makers directly, work through a Residency intermediary, or rely on another written/oral source?

This gap is now more important precisely because the Wallich/Murray antecedent is clearer.
