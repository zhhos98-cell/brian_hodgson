# Web research 2026-09-02 — Wave 58
## Before the 1831 thar paper: military administration already classified hill soldiers by origin, caste/class, and service

Date: 2026-09-02

Status: revision of the early military-tribes genealogy after Waves 46–57. This wave does not establish a direct documentary transfer into Hodgson's 1831–33 paper. It reconstructs a neighboring military-administrative classification infrastructure and tightens the distinction between administrative classification and Hodgson's later publication-facing ethnological recoding.

## Executive result

The earlier model was too binary if phrased as:

`administrative military problem -> ethnological classification`.

The new evidence shows classification already operating inside military administration before Hodgson's surviving 1831 `Castes of Parbattiah army tribes` working.

Three stages can now be distinguished.

### 1825 — Hodgson's Nepal-army memorandum already sorts military manpower

A.P. Coleman cites Hodgson's 10 February 1825 memorandum through two archival channels: Bengal Secret Political Consultations and the Hodgson manuscript collection. The passages Coleman reproduces sort Nepal's forces by:

- peace establishment;
- war establishment;
- reserve status;
- military effectiveness/training;
- territorial origin/local recruitment;
- availability for British recruitment.

One passage distinguishes troops raised locally in conquered provinces, for example Kumaon, from other Nepalese/Gorkhali forces.

Thus the 1825 administrative object is not pre-classificatory in any simple sense.

Its categories are primarily operational and military:

`force status + origin + training/effectiveness + recruitability`.

### 1830 — Bentinck orders an explicit personnel-classification abstract

In a minute of 27 July 1830 concerning the Nasiri and Sirmoor battalions, Lord William Bentinck directed commanding officers to report the men's:

- `Casts`;
- `Districts`;
- `Provinces`;
- `length of service`.

The requested documentary form is crucial. Bentinck specified that these particulars could be shown in an **abstract** rather than through the detail of a full `Description Roll`.

Coleman identifies the source as:

`I.O.R., Bengal Military Consultations, P/33/55, no.11, 25 July–13 August 1830`.

The resulting returns were supplied by Captain Kennedy of the Nasiri Battalion and Major Young of the Sirmoor Battalion.

### 1830 returns — the abstract stabilizes a categorical comparison

Coleman's reconstruction of the returns gives country/district-of-origin totals and a class/caste table using categories including:

- Brahmans;
- Rajputs;
- Khas Gorkhas / Chhetris;
- Magars, Gurungs and Garhwalis;
- Sarkis and Damais;
- inferior-class Hindus;
- Muslims.

Sources identified by Coleman include:

- Kennedy and Young returns: `I.O.R., B.M.C., P/33/57, nos.34–37 / nos.36–37, 1 October 1830`;
- Bengal Military letter to the Court of Directors: `I.O.R., Military Department Records, L/MIL/3/32, letter no.52, 6 November 1830`.

The later Bengal Military letter is especially important because it acknowledges that the apparent caste difference between the two corps arose from a **`capricious system of classification`**. The same bureaucracy that tabulated the categories therefore also recognized that the classifications were not naturally or uniformly given.

Core result:

`classification required administrative stabilization because classificatory practice varied by recorder`.

## 1. The 1825 Hodgson memorandum is already a sorting device

Coleman's archival notes point to:

- `I.O.R., B.S.P.C., P/Ben/Sec/328, 4 March 1825, no.16, para.6`;
- ibid., para.33;
- `I.O.R., Hodgson papers, MSS.9, ff.177–178`.

The quoted/reported passages show a memorandum dated 10 February 1825 and forwarded by Gardner to Calcutta.

The memo distinguishes approximately:

- 10,000 peace-establishment troops;
- 20,000 war-establishment troops;
- another 10,000 in rotational reserve;
- troops by degree of training/effectiveness;
- locally raised provincial troops versus other Nepalese forces.

The important point for the paper is not the numbers by themselves. It is the documentary operation:

**military manpower is made administratively knowable by decomposing it into statuses, capacities, origins and recruitment possibilities.**

Mechanism:

`MILITARY_MANPOWER_ALREADY_EXISTS_AS_CATEGORIZED_ADMINISTRATIVE_DATA`.

This also exposes a copy/record-channel problem. The same 1825 inquiry is now traceable through both a Government consultation record and Hodgson's own manuscript archive.

Potential material sequence:

`Residency/Hodgson working manuscript`
-> `official submission / forwarding`
-> `Bengal Secret Political Consultation record`

but the exact copying/dispatch relation must still be reconstructed before drawing a direct stemmatic arrow.

Mechanisms:

- `ADMINISTRATIVE_SUBMISSION_CREATES_PARALLEL_RECORD_CHANNELS`
- `AUTHOR_ARCHIVE_AND_GOVERNMENT_RECORD_ARE_DISTINCT_WITNESS_FAMILIES`.

## 2. Bentinck's 1830 request is a documentary-format instruction

The most useful feature of the 1830 minute is not simply that caste appears as a variable.

Bentinck asks for several variables to be converted from personnel-level information into an **abstract**. He explicitly contrasts that compressed form with a `Description Roll`.

This creates a documentary transformation:

`individual personnel record / description-roll granularity`
-> `abstracted categorical counts`
-> `comparison between corps`.

The change in format changes the scale at which military knowledge can operate.

A description roll attaches characteristics to individual soldiers. An abstract detaches selected attributes from individuals, aggregates them, and makes distributions visible at battalion scale.

Mechanisms:

- `DESCRIPTION_ROLL_TO_ABSTRACT_COMPRESSION`
- `AGGREGATION_FORMAT_PRODUCES_CLASSIFICATORY_VISIBILITY`
- `DOCUMENT_FORMAT_CHANGES_SCALE_OF_KNOWING`
- `SELECTED_FIELDS_BECOME_COMPARABLE_VARIABLES`.

This is directly relevant to a paper-object history because the epistemic operation is specified as a change of documentary form, not merely a change of ideas.

## 3. The 1830 tables and the instability of classification

Kennedy and Young did not classify their men in exactly the same way.

The Bengal Military letter to the Court of Directors subsequently characterized the apparent divergence as produced by a `capricious system of classification` rather than a substantive difference between the corps.

This produces an unusually valuable controlled case:

1. Government requests standardized variables.
2. Local commanding officers operationalize those variables differently.
3. Their returns are converted into comparable tables.
4. A higher administrative level detects the instability of the classifications.
5. The aggregated result is nevertheless transmitted upward as military knowledge.

Thus the table does not merely reveal a pre-existing social taxonomy.

It **stabilizes heterogeneous classificatory practices long enough to make corps comparable**.

Mechanisms:

- `CLASSIFICATION_IS_RECORDER_DEPENDENT`
- `ABSTRACT_TABLE_STABILIZES_HETEROGENEOUS_CATEGORIES`
- `ADMINISTRATIVE_COMPARABILITY_CAN_COEXIST_WITH_CATEGORY_INSTABILITY`
- `PAPER_FORM_TEMPORARILY_FIXES_CONTESTED_CLASSIFICATION`.

This is stronger than treating caste labels as transparent ethnographic data.

## 4. Temporal adjacency to Hodgson's 1831 rough paper

Cambridge identifies:

`MSS EUR HODGSON/7 item 20 — Castes of Parbattiah army tribes — 1831`.

Its directly catalogued content includes:

- Khasa thars;
- Brahman family names;
- Magar family names and a note on Bara-Magarant/original home;
- Gurung clans.

The lexical/category overlap with the 1830 military returns is striking at the level of broad groups:

`Brahman / Khas(a) / Magar / Gurung`.

But no direct transmission has yet been demonstrated.

There is currently no evidence that:

- Hodgson saw Kennedy's or Young's 1830 returns;
- Bentinck's request caused Hodgson's 1831 classification;
- vol.7/20 copied the battalion abstracts;
- the category sequence derives from Bengal military forms.

Therefore the correct relation is:

`INSTITUTIONAL_NEIGHBORING_CLASSIFICATION_INFRASTRUCTURE`,

not:

`DIRECT_SOURCE_OF_HODGSON_1831`.

## 5. What changes in the early genealogy

Wave 46's strongest distinction remains valuable:

`problem genealogy != textual stemma`.

Wave 57 added:

`historical document attestation != surviving witness identity != textual descent`.

Wave 58 adds another correction:

`administrative knowledge != absence of classification`.

The revised early sequence is now:

`1825 Hodgson military-administrative sorting`
`(force status + territorial origin + effectiveness + recruitability)`

+

`early classificatory military-tribes memoir retrospectively attested`
`(physical identity unresolved)`

+

`1830 Bengal military abstract regime`
`(caste/class + district/province + service length; description-roll data compressed to abstracts)`

->

`1831 Hodgson surviving rough ethnological/classificatory working`
`(thar + family/clan + group + original-home/origin narrative)`

->

`October 1832 fair publication-facing table/paper`

->

`1833 learned-society print + recruitment argument`.

This is best described as a change in **classification regime and documentary scale**, not a passage from an unclassified administrative problem to classification as such.

Working formulation:

**military administration already rendered hill soldiers through categories; Hodgson's surviving 1831–33 paper sequence intensified and reconfigured classification by moving from personnel/manpower variables toward thar, clan, origin and genealogical relations, then attached that infrastructure to a learned-society recruitment argument.**

Mechanisms:

- `ADMINISTRATIVE_CLASSIFICATION_TO_ETHNOLOGICAL_RECODING`
- `CLASSIFICATION_REGIME_CHANGES_WITH_DOCUMENTARY_OPERATION`
- `STATE_MILITARY_AND_ETHNOLOGICAL_CATEGORIES_OVERLAP_WITHOUT_IDENTITY`
- `CLASSIFICATORY_INFRASTRUCTURE_PRECEDES_LEARNED_SOCIETY_PUBLICATION`.

## 6. Why this helps the paper argument

This case now supplies a concrete bridge between material/document history and the history of classification.

The operative sequence is not only conceptual:

`observation -> classification -> theory`.

It is also documentary:

`individual/service information`
-> `Description Roll or equivalent personnel record`
-> `selected fields`
-> `abstract counts`
-> `corps comparison`
-> `higher-level administrative interpretation`

alongside Hodgson's own:

`army memorandum / rough military working`
-> `rough thar/clan classificatory paper`
-> `fair tabular paper`
-> `journal print`.

Different paper forms select different units of analysis.

The 1830 abstract treats soldiers as aggregable instances of administrative variables. Hodgson's 1831–32 classificatory papers treat names, thars, clans, origins and groups as relations that need to be ordered and explained.

This gives the article a precise material-epistemic claim:

**classification changes when the documentary unit and aggregation procedure change.**

## Source-control rules

1. Coleman is a secondary reconstruction but gives precise India Office references. Treat the archival references as retrieval targets, not as if the primary files had been directly inspected in this wave.
2. Do not infer direct influence from Bentinck/Kennedy/Young to Hodgson without correspondence, shared wording, copied tables, or other transmission evidence.
3. Keep the 1830 battalion abstracts as `institutionally neighboring classification infrastructure`.
4. Do not describe 1825 Hodgson work as unclassified administrative data; it already sorts manpower by operational status and origin.
5. Do not equate 1825 operational categories with the 1831 thar/clan/genealogical apparatus.
6. Keep `Description Roll`, `abstract`, and later Hodgson tabulation as distinct documentary operations.
7. Preserve recorder-dependent category instability rather than normalizing Kennedy's and Young's labels into one supposedly natural caste scheme.
8. The statement `capricious system of classification` belongs to the Bengal Military transmission to the Court of Directors as cited by Coleman, not to Hodgson.

## Bottom line

The prehistory of Hodgson's military-tribes paper is no longer best represented as a policy problem that only became classificatory in 1831. The 1825 army memorandum already categorized manpower by status and territorial origin, while Bentinck's 1830 military administration explicitly demanded caste, district/province and service data in abstract form and generated comparative tables whose classificatory instability was acknowledged by officials themselves. Hodgson's 1831–33 sequence therefore enters an already classificatory military information environment but performs a different documentary and epistemic operation: it deepens broad personnel categories into thars, clans, family names, origins and genealogical relations and carries that recoded infrastructure into learned print. The key transition is from one classification regime and paper form to another, not from administration to classification in the abstract.
