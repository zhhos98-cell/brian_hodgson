# Web/archive research 2026-09-02 — Wave 57
## An earlier classificatory military-tribes memoir is retrospectively attested before the surviving 1831 working state

Date: 2026-09-02

Status: source-control revision to Wave 46's problem-genealogy/textual-stemma model.

## Executive result

Wave 46 correctly separated:

`problem genealogy != textual stemma`.

That distinction remains necessary. But a newly recovered service-history witness changes one important boundary.

The 1868–69 privately printed *Notes of the Services of B. H. Hodgson, collected by a friend* contains a `MEMORANDUM OF MR. HODGSON'S OFFICIAL SERVICES`. In the surviving OCR/transcription held in the research library, the memorandum says that Hodgson, after beginning in 1825 to seek work beyond the routine duties of the Residency, drew up **two memoirs**:

1. one on the military system of Nepal, including force, pay, discipline, arms and ammunition;
2. one on the **military tribes**, including their `race`, `tribal classification and names`, and their `physical and moral qualities as soldiers`.

The same retrospective account connects these memoirs directly to Hodgson's proposal to obtain Gorkha soldiers through the Durbar.

Rajendralala Mitra's 1882 *The Sanskrit Buddhist Literature of Nepal* gives a closely parallel account: while Assistant to the Resident, Hodgson's military studies resulted in two memoirs covering the existing Nepal force, the military tribes and races from which it was drawn, their tribal names and classification, their physical and moral qualities, pay, discipline, arms and ammunition, ending in the Gorkha-recruitment proposal.

Hunter in 1896 reproduces Mitra's passage and explicitly says he uses it because Mitra had been in personal communication with Hodgson and because it helps fix the period of these early inquiries.

This does **not** prove that the surviving February 1825 `Memoir on the Army of Nepal` (MSS EUR HODGSON/10 item 1) is itself the lost/attested military-tribes memoir, nor that either surviving 1825/1828 military manuscript is the direct copy-text of the 1832–33 paper.

It does establish a narrower but important result:

**a classificatory military-tribes memoir is retrospectively attested in Hodgson's Assistant-Resident work before the first currently identified surviving explicit classificatory working of 1831.**

Revised distinction:

`earliest historically attested classificatory military-tribes memoir != earliest surviving directly inspectable classificatory manuscript state`.

Current states:

- `EARLY_CLASSIFICATORY_MILITARY_TRIBES_MEMOIR = RETROSPECTIVELY_ATTESTED`;
- `PHYSICAL_WITNESS_IDENTITY = UNRESOLVED`;
- `DIRECT_TEXTUAL_DESCENT_TO_1832/1833 = UNRESOLVED`;
- `EARLIEST_SURVIVING_EXPLICIT_CLASSIFICATORY_STATE = vol.7/20 (1831)`.

Mechanisms:

- `RETROSPECTIVE_ATTESTATION_ADDS_A_MISSING_DOCUMENT_NODE`
- `ATTESTED_DOCUMENT_DOES_NOT_EQUAL_IDENTIFIED_SURVIVING_WITNESS`
- `EARLIEST_SURVIVING_STATE_DOES_NOT_EQUAL_EARLIEST_HISTORICAL_STATE`
- `PROBLEM_GENEALOGY_NOT_TEXTUAL_STEMMA`.

## 1. The 1868–69 service pamphlet

Bibliographic witness:

*Notes of the services of B. H. Hodgson, esq., late British minister at the court of Nepal, collected by a friend*.

Google Books records full-view 1868 and 1869 states. Its indexed vocabulary includes `COPY OF MEMORANDUM`, `OFFICIAL SERVICES`, `Military Tribes`, `System of Nepal`, `Gorkha`, and related headings.

Harihar Joshi's bibliography describes the work as a privately printed, `NOT PUBLISHED` pamphlet distributed among Hodgson's circle and notes multiple impressions/editions in a short period. Joshi also reports that Hodgson himself referred to it as a pamphlet.

Research-library OCR of a scanned witness preserves the relevant section under:

`MEMORANDUM OF MR. HODGSON'S OFFICIAL SERVICES`.

The sequence matters. The memorandum first says Hodgson began in 1825 to pursue work beyond Residency routine. Under its first numbered activity it then describes two military memoirs, separating military-system content from military-tribes content. The latter already contains three elements central to the later 1833 paper:

- `race`;
- `tribal classification and names`;
- `physical and moral qualities as soldiers`.

It then derives the recruitment argument from this work.

A subsequent numbered section turns to Hodgson's work as chargé d'affaires in 1830–32 and to commerce. This ordering places the military inquiry earlier in the retrospective service narrative, but it should not be converted into an exact composition date for the second memoir.

Safe dating field:

`ASSISTANT_RESIDENT / EARLY_RESIDENCY WORK; RETROSPECTIVE NARRATIVE BEGINS 1825; EXACT MEMOIR DATE UNRESOLVED`.

Do **not** silently assign `1825-02` to the military-tribes memoir.

## 2. Mitra 1882 is a second provenance layer, not automatically an independent textual witness

Rajendralala Mitra, *The Sanskrit Buddhist Literature of Nepal* (1882), prefatory biographical notice, states that Hodgson's early military studies resulted in two memoirs and explicitly lists:

- existing force of the Nepal Durbar;
- military tribes and races from which it was drawn;
- tribal names and classification;
- physical and moral qualities as soldiers;
- pay and discipline;
- manufacture of arms and ammunition;
- recommendation to recruit a large body of Gurkha soldiery.

Source:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/ocr/essays/sanskrit-buddhist-literature/cu31924007399342.pdf

The wording is close to the service-pamphlet tradition. Therefore Mitra should **not** be counted mechanically as a fully independent textual attestation.

Its value is provenance and temporal framing: Mitra was a contemporary scholar in Hodgson's network and wrote while Hodgson was still alive.

Evidence class:

`NEAR_CONTEMPORARY_RETROSPECTIVE_ATTESTATION_WITH_POSSIBLE_TEXTUAL_DEPENDENCE`.

## 3. Hunter 1896 strengthens provenance but still does not identify the manuscript

Hunter's *Life of Brian Houghton Hodgson* reproduces Mitra's description and explains why he uses it: Mitra had been in personal communication with Hodgson, and the passage helps fix the period of the military, commercial and legal inquiries.

Hunter further says that the results were later published in different forms, including learned-society papers, Government *Selections*, *The Phoenix*, and Hodgson's collected essays.

Source:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1896%20Life%20of%20Brian%20Houghton%20Hodgson%20by%20WW%20Hunter%20s.pdf

This supports continuity of a research programme and publication history.

It still does not supply:

- a shelfmark for the second early military-tribes memoir;
- direct wording shared with the 1832/1833 paper;
- proof that vol.10/1 is the second memoir;
- proof that vol.9/22 or vol.9/23 is the second memoir;
- a direct copy edge to vol.6/1 or vol.9/10.

Thus Hunter supplies `PROVENANCE_SUPPORT`, not `TEXTUAL_DESCENT_PROOF`.

## 5. Wave 46 must be revised at one sentence, not discarded

Wave 46 said the first currently identified surviving paper state explicitly aggregating thar/Magar/Gurung classificatory material is vol.7/20 (1831).

That remains correct as a statement about **surviving directly identified manuscript evidence**.

What must change is any broader inference that 1831 marks the historical beginning of classificatory work.

The service-pamphlet/Mitra tradition shows that a military-tribes memoir already existed in the earlier Assistant-Resident research programme and was remembered as containing `tribal names and classification`.

Revised sequence:

`1825 administrative army rough/fair family (direct surviving witnesses)`
+
`early military-tribes classificatory memoir (retrospectively attested; physical identity unresolved)`
-> `1828 military reworking (direct surviving witness)`
-> `1831 explicit surviving thar/clan classificatory working`
-> `October 1832 fair paper`
-> `1833 learned-society print`.

This is no longer a simple transition from a purely administrative problem in 1825 to classification first appearing in 1831.

A better model is:

**administrative military assessment and classificatory military-tribe inquiry were already differentiated but coupled within Hodgson's early Residency programme; the 1831–32 papers are the first surviving states in which the later publication-facing classificatory apparatus can be directly inspected.**

Mechanism:

`EARLY_PARALLEL_ADMINISTRATIVE_AND_CLASSIFICATORY_DOCUMENTARY_BRANCHES`.

## 6. Paper-centred significance

The new result makes the paper argument more material, not less.

The central historical problem is now partly one of **survival and identification**:

- a retrospective printed service document remembers two distinct early paper outputs;
- the modern archive preserves a dense rough/fair army family;
- the catalogue identifies a directly classificatory state only from 1831;
- one early classificatory memoir may survive under a broader military label, may have been reworked into another surviving object, or may be absent.

Thus a documentary genealogy cannot be reconstructed by title matching alone.

The material sequence has to distinguish:

`attested historical document`
from
`surviving physical witness`
from
`modern catalogue description`
from
`textual descent`.

Mechanisms:

- `DOCUMENT_ATTESTATION_NOT_WITNESS_IDENTIFICATION`
- `CATALOGUE_TITLE_NOT_DOCUMENTARY_IDENTITY`
- `SURVIVAL_FILTER_RESHAPES_APPARENT_GENEALOGY`
- `HISTORICAL_VERSION_GRAPH_CAN_CONTAIN_MISSING_NODES`.

## Source-control rules

1. Preserve Wave 46's `problem genealogy != textual stemma` distinction.
2. Do not date the attested tribes memoir exactly to February 1825; the retrospective service narrative begins in 1825 but does not date the second memoir at day/month level.
3. Do not identify vol.10/1, vol.9/22 or vol.9/23 as the military-tribes memoir without direct content-level comparison.
4. Treat the 1868–69 service pamphlet, Mitra 1882 and Hunter 1896 as a related retrospective provenance tradition; wording overlap means they are not automatically three independent attestations.
5. Keep vol.7/20 as the earliest currently identified surviving manuscript with directly catalogued thar/clan classificatory content.
6. Keep the missing/unidentified early memoir as an explicit node rather than forcing it into a surviving shelfmark.
7. A direct textual edge to 1832/1833 requires shared wording, ordering, errors, headings or other content-level evidence.

## Bottom line

Wave 46 was right not to turn the 1825 army memorandum into a direct draft of the 1833 article. But the archive should no longer be narrated as if classificatory military-tribe work only became visible historically in 1831. A privately printed 1868–69 service memorandum, closely echoed by Mitra in 1882 and contextualized by Hunter in 1896, retrospectively attests an earlier military-tribes memoir containing race, tribal names/classification and soldier qualities. Its physical identity is unresolved. The correct graph therefore gains a missing early classificatory document node while preserving the separation between retrospective attestation, surviving witness, and proven textual descent.
