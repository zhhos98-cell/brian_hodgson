## 1837 Government inquiry as an operational-use dossier; London despatch indexing; medical/opium afterlives

Status: source-control continuation of `_13`–`_14`. This note records direct primary evidence that materially changes the article architecture. The 1837 Nepal-paper inquiry was not merely a comparison of paper quality or a precursor to the 1841 Darjeeling experiment. Campbell's published reply to Lord Auckland's questions distributes Nepal paper across several concrete operations — public-record preservation, packing medicines, castor-oil manufacture, box lining and postal waterproofing — and records both successful and unsuccessful institutional trials. A separate India Office despatch index shows that the proposal to employ Nepal paper in Government offices entered the Company's London-facing correspondence system.

The note also records a newly precise locator for the 1837 Resident's Diary and a controlled 1851 official opium-manufacture afterlife. These should be used as first-order historical consequences, not as occasions to generalize in advance about materiality or empire.

## Finding 64 — Lord Auckland's 1837 Nepal-paper inquiry can now be dated to 4 October 1837 and reconstructed from the direct published dossier

Evidence status: **SECURE direct primary printed source.**

Primary source:

A. Campbell, `On Nepalese Paper`, *Transactions of the Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India* 5 (1838), 220–229, read 10 December 1837. The published dossier includes Campbell's covering letter to John Bell, T. C. Scott's Government note, Campbell's reply dated 15 November 1837, and an appendix reprinting Hodgson's 1832 manufacturing account.

Full searchable witness:
https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.38231/2015.38231.Transactions-Of-The-Agricultural-And-Horticultural-Society-Of-India--Vol-5_djvu.txt

Campbell states that he is replying to Scott's **note of 4 October**. Scott says he had been directed by Lord Auckland to inquire into paper manufactured in Nepal and asks Campbell to supply information on:

- resistance to insects;
- suitability for stationery and box lining;
- price;
- quantity obtainable.

This closes the Government trigger more precisely than the earlier secondary formulation `Auckland made an inquiry in 1837`.

Correct chronology:

`4 Oct 1837 — T. C. Scott / Government inquiry at Lord Auckland's direction`
-> `Campbell investigates / assembles comparative evidence and specimens`
-> `15 Nov 1837 — Campbell reply`
-> `Campbell sends paper and the Scott correspondence to the Agricultural and Horticultural Society`
-> `10 Dec 1837 — paper read to Society`.

Campbell also suggested that samples be shown to the Calcutta Chamber of Commerce. This is a proposed further route, not proof that the Chamber actually inspected them.

Historical consequence:

The 1837 episode is a Government-directed evaluation of **specific uses and procurement feasibility**, not merely learned-society interest.

## Finding 65 — Campbell evaluates record paper by material wear at folds, using paired twenty-year office records

Evidence status: **SECURE direct primary printed observation.**

Campbell endorses Hodgson's earlier office-record recommendation and supplies his own comparison from the Nepal Residency archive.

He reports that he had before him:

- office records written on Nepal paper in 1817;
- records of the same date written on English foolscap;
- both stored and looked after in comparable fashion.

The Nepal-paper records remained fresh at folds and edges; the English sheets had ragged/torn edges and folds worn through.

Campbell connects this outcome to the paper's toughness, pliability, elasticity and resistance to repeated folding.

This is analytically important because the quality verdict is tied to a **specific mode of archival failure**:

`packet storage / repeated folds`
-> `fold abrasion and tearing`
-> `comparative survival after about twenty years`.

Do not turn this into a general claim that Nepal paper was intrinsically superior to all European paper. The direct comparison is with these office-record packets under this storage/use regime.

## Finding 66 — the 1837 reply turns Government adoption into a procurement, seasonality and market-routing problem

Evidence status: **SECURE direct primary printed source.**

Campbell gives current Kathmandu prices and estimates transport to Patna at about Rs 1-12 annas per maund. He explains that Kathmandu's Government monopoly can make Patna prices unexpectedly competitive because some paper reaches the plains directly from hill manufactories.

If Government adopted Nepal paper in quantity, he proposed either:

- annual purchase in Nepal during the rains followed by forwarding toward the Ganges during the cold months; or
- reliance on border/plains markets if the Durbar or its agents obstructed bulk procurement.

He lists concrete market nodes for Bengal and for Central/Western Provinces, including Patna, Kessariah in Saran, Janikpoor, Darbhanga/Tirhoot, Purnea, Govindgunge and Alligunge in Saran, Gorakhpur-area markets, and markets in Oude.

Keep historical spellings controlled before modern map normalization.

Consequence:

`paper judged durable for records`
-> `possible Government adoption`
-> `quantity/scarcity/monopoly problem`
-> `seasonal procurement and transport plan`
-> `regional market alternatives`.

This is a first-order extension of material performance into supply administration.

## Finding 67 — Campbell's matched paper samples already assign different grades to different operations

Evidence status: **SECURE direct primary printed source.**

Campbell sends graded samples with price information rather than treating `Nepal paper` as one homogeneous material.

The published account distinguishes, among others:

- a grade best suited to office records;
- a coarse large sheet suited to lining boxes and packing medicines, seeds and groceries;
- other grades appropriate to envelopes, rough notes and general packing;
- preserved/coloured/polished papers with different preparation and cost.

Campbell says paper could be made to order in many dimensions and kinds and offers to supply bales if Government or others wished to conduct a larger trial.

Article consequence:

The historical object in 1837 is already a **graded family of papers whose usefulness is operation-specific**. Avoid prose that treats toughness, smoothness, size or price as a single rank ordering across all uses.

## Finding 68 — Nepal paper was already an operative material in medicine packing and cold-drawn castor-oil manufacture

Evidence status: **SECURE for Campbell's own General Dispensary judgement; STRONG reported contemporary testimony for Dr Davies at Patna.**

Campbell says from personal experience that Nepal paper was better adapted for packing medicines than Indian papers or the English blue/brown papers used at the General Dispensary of Calcutta.

He then reports information supplied by Dr Davies at Patna: Davies preferred Nepal paper in the manufacture of **cold-drawn castor oil** and used **several maunds per month** in the operation. The practical criterion was fibre tenacity: soft paper made from cloth could shed shreds into the oil, while Nepal paper resisted this failure.

This is one of the strongest first-order affordance cases in the corpus because the source gives:

`operation = cold-drawn castor-oil manufacture`

`required performance = paper remains intact during use`

`failure mode = fragments/shreds contaminate oil`

`selection = Nepal paper preferred because its fibre resisted shedding`.

Do not convert Campbell's report of Davies into direct inspection of Davies's workshop. It is contemporary reported testimony printed by Campbell.

## Finding 69 — postal waterproofing produced a split verdict: general substitution failed, local Nepal Residency use continued

Evidence status: **SECURE direct primary printed source.**

Campbell describes treated Nepal paper used locally in Kathmandu in two materially distinct ways:

- as a lining under the Post Office roof, pasted over rafters;
- as a waxed-paper substitute for wax cloth in packing letter mails and `bhangy` parcels sent by dak.

Campbell says he had earlier brought the waxed paper to the **Postmaster General** hoping it would enter general Post Office use. The Postmaster General's trials were not satisfactory enough to replace cloth.

Campbell then records the opposite local verdict: the Nepal Residency continued using the treated paper and, in his account, had very few complaints of wet mails even during the rainy season.

This yields a historically unusually clean comparison:

`same proposed substitute`
-> `Postmaster General trial`
-> `general substitution threshold not met`

while

`local Nepal Residency postal use`
-> `continued adoption`
-> `reported satisfactory wet-weather performance`.

Do not resolve this into `the experiment succeeded` or `failed`. The source records different institutional scales and performance thresholds.

This is highly valuable for the article because it shows that a material verdict did not travel automatically from one site or office to another.

## Finding 73 — by 1851 Nepal paper is documented inside an official Bengal Government opium-manufacturing process

Evidence status: **SECURE direct official publication; later than the article's main 1849 endpoint and should be used as controlled afterlife.**

W. C. B. Eatwell, *On the Poppy Cultivation, and the Benares Opium Agency* (Calcutta: Military Orphan Press, 1851), was published as No. 1 of *Selections from the Records of the Bengal Government*.

British Library official-publication locator:

`IOR/V/23/91, No 1`.

Catalogue:
https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/041-000134211

Eatwell describes the preparation of opium for internal/Abkari consumption. The opium was brought to a specified consistency, moulded into one-seer square bricks, then **wrapped in oiled Nepal paper** and packed into compartmented boxes.

A searchable public text witness is currently available through a Harvard scan mirrored at Scribd; the same passage was reproduced in the 1854 *Journal of Botany and Kew Garden Miscellany*. The BL catalogue secures the title, author, publication status and date.

Historical value:

This shortens the previously known opium-paper afterlife from Hunter's 1877 statistical account to an **official 1851 Bengal Government record publication**.

Do not use it to claim unbroken continuity from Campbell's 1837 Patna castor-oil evidence. The secure point is narrower:

- 1837: Campbell reports heavy monthly use of Nepal paper in a Patna castor-oil operation;
- 1851: an official Bengal Government opium-agency account specifies oiled Nepal paper as a packaging layer for Abkari opium bricks.

Together they establish that Nepal paper entered more than one nineteenth-century pharmaceutical/drug-manufacturing operation in the Gangetic administrative economy.

## Revised article consequence after the direct 1837 dossier

The 1837 material should no longer appear as a small bridge between 1833 and 1841. It supplies a compact historical laboratory of **operation-specific paper performance** before makers were moved to Darjeeling.

The source-visible sequence is:

`record packets` -> fold durability / long storage

`graded stationery and packing` -> size / roughness / cost / purpose

`medicine packing` -> toughness in handling

`castor-oil manufacture` -> resistance to fibre shedding into product

`waxed postal wrapping` -> wet-weather protection

`general Post Office substitution trial` -> threshold not met

`local Residency postal use` -> continued because local performance was judged sufficient.

The key historical point is first-order:

**the same paper did not receive one verdict. Different operations selected different properties and produced different adoption decisions.**

This can carry the article toward the later 1841–42 filtration/machine episodes without requiring an abstract theory section. The HPS result can be earned from repeated tests, failures and use decisions.

## Bottom line

The direct 1837 dossier materially enlarges the paper history without adding another disconnected case. Lord Auckland's inquiry asked what Nepal paper could actually do and what it would cost to procure. Campbell answered by testing or reporting it in distinct operations: archival folding, graded stationery, medicine packing, castor-oil manufacture and postal waterproofing. The Postmaster General's unsuccessful general-substitution trial, juxtaposed with continued local Nepal Residency use, gives an unusually clear historical example of one material receiving different practical verdicts at different institutional scales. A separate Company despatch-index entry, `IOR/Z/E/4/15/N140`, shows that employment of Nepal paper in Government offices became a formal London-facing administrative subject. By 1851, an official Bengal Government opium report specifies oiled Nepal paper inside another manufacturing/packing operation. These findings strengthen the article's first-order question from `could Nepal paper be remade elsewhere?` to the connected question `after testing, where was it actually put to work, under what performance criteria, and with what adoption consequences?`
