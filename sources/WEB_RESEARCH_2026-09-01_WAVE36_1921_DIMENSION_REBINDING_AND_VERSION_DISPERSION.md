# Web research 2026-09-01 — Wave 36
## 1921 dimension-based rebinding and the archival dispersion of Hodgson version families

Date: 2026-09-01

Status: paper-centred archival-afterlife follow-up to Waves 34–35. This wave asks how version families that were deliberately co-bundled in the August 1864 India Office transfer became dispersed across the modern `MSS EUR HODGSON` volume sequence.

## Executive result

The missing institutional mechanism is now explicit.

The modern Cambridge/British Library catalogue history states that:

- Hodgson's private papers were deposited in the India Office Library in **1864**;
- in **1921** they were bound into **95 volumes**;
- the resulting numbering runs to 108 because 13 scrolls remained in the sequence;
- decisions about ordering were taken **partly with regard to the physical dimensions of each document**;
- items of similar size were bound together;
- consequently, items that belonged together, explicitly including an **original manuscript and its translation**, were in many cases bound in different places simply because they were written on different sizes of paper.

Source:

Cambridge Hodgson Papers inventory, introduction:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/intro.html

The paper-centred consequence is unusually strong:

`paper format at research use`
-> `physical size at archival custody`
-> `1921 binding decision`
-> `modern shelfmark dispersion`
-> `historical version relation becomes harder to see`.

Thus paper dimensions acquired a second-order archival effect nearly a century after the documents were produced.

Mechanisms:

- `PAPER_DIMENSION_DRIVES_ARCHIVAL_REBINDING`
- `ARCHIVAL_REBINDING_BREAKS_VERSION_FAMILIES`
- `MATERIAL_FORMAT_HAS_LONG_ARCHIVAL_AFTERLIFE`
- `MODERN_SHELFMARK_DISPERSION_IS_PARTLY_SIZE_PRODUCED`
- `ARCHIVAL_CLASSIFICATION_CAN_OVERRIDE_HISTORICAL_RELATION`

## 1. The catalogue history explicitly identifies dimension as a binding criterion

The Cambridge catalogue introduction reports that the 1864 India Office deposit was rebound in 1921. It states that arrangement decisions were taken partly according to physical dimensions, with documents of similar size bound together.

This is not merely a retrospective inference from the volumes.

The catalogue editors themselves explain the resulting relational damage:

related items, such as an original manuscript and its translation, can now be located in different volumes because they were written on different sizes of paper.

This creates a rare documented case in which a material property of the support becomes an archival classification variable.

Mechanism:

`PAPER_DIMENSION_DRIVES_ARCHIVAL_REBINDING`.

---

## 2. 1864 and 1921 encode different archival ontologies

Wave 35 reconstructed the 1864 presentation list as a version-family packing system.

Repeated 1864 custody units preserve together:

- vernacular/source states;
- English translations;
- Persian translations;
- abstracts;
- repeated/copy states;
- multilingual papers on the same work family.

The 1921 operation uses a different criterion.

At least part of the collection is reordered according to **physical format/size**.

Thus:

### 1864 relation

`source + translation + abstract + related derivative -> transfer family`

### 1921 relation

`documents of similar physical dimensions -> bound volume`

These are different ontologies of archival proximity.

Mechanism:

`ARCHIVAL_CLASSIFICATION_CAN_OVERRIDE_HISTORICAL_RELATION`.

## 7. This gives paper dimensions an unusually long causal afterlife

The article's earlier paper model followed:

`manufacture -> stock -> allocation -> documentary versioning -> custody`.

The 1921 evidence adds a feedback effect.

A documentary derivative may be assigned a different paper size because of its operation:

- large chart;
- small table;
- pre-bound notebook;
- broad local sheet;
- imported route paper.

Decades later, those dimensions become relevant to rebinding.

Therefore:

`operation-specific paper format`
-> `different physical dimensions`
-> `archival sorting by dimensions`
-> `version family dispersion`.

This is stronger than saying materiality affects preservation.

The same sheet-format decision can shape both **research use** and **later archival visibility**.

Mechanism:

`MATERIAL_FORMAT_HAS_LONG_ARCHIVAL_AFTERLIFE`.

## 8. The modern catalogue is reconstructive because the 1921 binding obscured relations

The modern descriptive project is itself performing relation repair.

The Cambridge catalogue repeatedly provides cross-references such as:

- `For Nepali original, see ...`;
- `For English summary, see ...`;
- `For Persian summary and translation, see ...`.

This should now be interpreted historically.

Those cross-references are not merely reader conveniences.

They reconstruct documentary relations that the physical volume arrangement often fails to preserve.

Thus there are at least three archival states:

1. **1864 transfer state** — relation preserved by trunks/bundles;
2. **1921 bound-volume state** — physical dimensions partly govern proximity;
3. **2000s–2011 relational catalogue state** — cross-references digitally reconstruct dispersed version relations.

Mechanisms:

- `CATALOGUE_REPAIRS_RELATIONS_BROKEN_BY_REBINDING`
- `DIGITAL_RELATIONAL_RECONSTRUCTION_OF_MATERIAL_DISPERSION`.

## 10. Article consequence

The paper article now has an unusually concrete archival-afterlife claim:

**paper did not cease to act once Hodgson's research was finished. The dimensions generated by earlier documentary operations later became an archival sorting criterion. In 1921 the India Office collection was rebound partly by physical size, and the catalogue explicitly notes that originals and translations were consequently separated because they were written on different-sized paper. Paper format therefore shaped not only how information was produced and converted, but how its historical relations became visible or invisible to later researchers.**

The full sequence is now:

`paper manufacture`
-> `stock and allocation`
-> `documentary versioning`
-> `1864 version-family co-bundling`
-> `1921 size-based rebinding`
-> `modern shelfmark dispersion`
-> `digital catalogue relation repair`.

## Source-control rules

1. 1921 `partly by physical dimensions` does not mean every modern volume was formed solely by size.
2. Modern dispersion should not automatically be attributed to 1921 size sorting unless the specific binding history is physically verified; the general mechanism is explicit, the item-level cause remains a reconstruction target.
3. A Cambridge cross-reference proves a declared relation, not necessarily exact direct copying unless the wording says original/translation/copy explicitly.
4. Pre-bound notebooks with unspecified paper support remain support-unspecified.
5. The 1864 manifest container relation and the 1921 bound-volume relation are separate archival states.
6. Current BL `90 volumes; 15 rolls` and Cambridge historical `95 volumes / numbering to 108 with 13 scrolls` remain an unresolved count-state difference.

## Bottom line

Wave 36 identifies the institutional operation that transformed Hodgson's 1864 version-family archive into the dispersed modern volume sequence. The 1921 binding scheme partly grouped documents by physical dimensions, and the catalogue explicitly acknowledges that originals and translations were sometimes separated simply because they were on different-sized paper. This turns paper format into an archival causal variable. A sheet's dimensions affected not only its nineteenth-century use but its twentieth-century classification, and the modern relational catalogue is now partly repairing the historical connections that size-based rebinding obscured.
