# Web research wave 13 — manuscript procurement as a material production system, 1824–1843

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: catalogue-level reconstruction from the Cambridge inventory of British Library `Mss Eur Hodgson/12`; object-level transcription remains pending.

Primary catalogue:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol012.html

## 1. Why this wave matters

The 1843 Wallich paper bill is not an isolated procurement curiosity. `Mss Eur Hodgson/12` preserves a longer chain in which manuscript acquisition was repeatedly converted into a **priced, authorised, counted, manufactured, bundled, shipped, receipted and audited operation**.

The sequence visible at catalogue level is:

**copy price → government sanction → barter proposal → cash advance → bills for copying/paper/binding/labour/packing → bundle receipt → itemised manuscript list → later paper/material bill.**

This is highly relevant to the repo’s central question because the “manuscript” is not the unit with which the system begins. It is assembled through finance, local paper procurement, scribal labour, binding/bundling, carpentry, porterage, shipment and receipt.

---

## 2. 1824 — copying already has an explicit price unit

### Mss Eur Hodgson/12 item 19, ff. 161–162

William Carey to Hodgson, 26 July 1824.

The catalogue records discussion of Hodgson’s list of sacred writings and **prices paid for copying manuscripts: Rs 2 per 100 stanzas**.

This is a compact but important metrological move. Textual extent (`stanzas`) becomes a labour/payment unit.

### Mechanism

**textual count → copying price → reproducible commission.**

The same intellectual corpus can therefore be measured simultaneously as:

- named work;
- number of leaves;
- number of verses/stanzas;
- monetary cost;
- later bundle/package count.

These are different operational representations of the same manuscript-production project.

## 3. 1825 — procurement begins as an officially authorised acquisition programme

### Item 20, ff. 167–168

A true copy of a Government letter dated 17 February 1825 announces sanction for Hodgson’s proposed purchase of Oriental works for the College of Fort William. A related letter authorises Carey to communicate with Hodgson about the proposal.

The acquisition system is therefore institutionally commissioned rather than merely a personal collecting habit.

### Item 21, ff. 169–173

The May 1825 Carey correspondence records an extraordinary first funding mechanism: the College Council orders Captain Ruddell to send **weapons and equipment worth 1,000 Indian rupees** to Hodgson to barter for Nepali religious manuscripts.

By November 1825 the Council changes the mechanism and decides to send **money** for manuscript purchase instead of weapons for barter.

### Analytical value

The same acquisition goal passes through two proposed exchange infrastructures:

1. **commodity barter** — weapons/equipment as exchange stock;
2. **monetised purchase** — cash remittance.

The shift is useful because it exposes procurement as a designed interface rather than a transparent act of “collection.” The institutional problem is how to transform Calcutta resources into manuscript acquisitions in Kathmandu.

Do not over-interpret why the Council changed its mind until the letters themselves are transcribed.

## 4. 1827 — bills reveal manuscript production as an assembled supply chain

### Item 8, ff. 128–130

Three bills, dated April, June and November 1827, for manuscripts sent to Dr Nathaniel Wallich in Calcutta.

Catalogue support: **Nepali handmade and machine-made paper**.

The catalogue explicitly says the bills include costs of:

- copying;
- binding;
- bundling;
- packaging;
- shipment to Calcutta;
- books and verses copied;
- purchase of local paper and related materials;
- carpenters’ wages;
- regular labourers’ wages;
- porters’ wages;
- local materials.

It interprets the bills as Residency accounts for reimbursement of costs incurred for Wallich and others.

### Core mechanism

This is one of the strongest sources in the entire repo because it makes the material production function explicit:

**commission → local paper/material purchase → copying → binding → bundling → carpenter/labour input → porterage → shipment → reimbursement.**

The source therefore collapses any easy distinction between “intellectual production” and “logistics.” The account treats them as a single costed operation.

### Paper-level importance

The bills themselves are on **mixed Nepali handmade and machine-made paper**. Thus paper appears at two levels simultaneously:

1. as a purchased production input for commissioned manuscripts;
2. as the support on which the production account survives.

This is an ideal object for the sheet-level paper census.

## 5. 11 May 1827 — receipt converts shipment into a custody event

### Item 23, f. 176

Receipt for arrival of **109 bundles of Oriental manuscripts** sent by Hodgson to the College of Fort William for use in its library.

The receipt is on machine-made paper.

### Mechanism

The bills establish expenditure and production; the receipt establishes arrival of a counted shipment unit.

But `109 bundles arrived` still does not prove item-level agreement with the production account. This is exactly the distinction already captured by the repo’s `custody checksum` concept:

**receipt count ≠ internal inventory reconciliation.**

The next question is whether the 1827 detailed list can bridge bundle-level receipt to manuscript-level identity.

---

## 6. 1827 — itemised list converts manuscripts into comparable numerical objects

### Item 25, ff. 179–182

Detailed list of Nepali and Tibetan manuscripts provided for the College Council at Hodgson’s suggestion.

Catalogue support: machine-made paper, with corrections and addenda by Hodgson.

For each manuscript the list records:

- number of leaves;
- number of verses (`ślokas`);
- price paid.

### Mechanism

This list sits between text and account. It creates a common grid in which unlike works can be compared through:

**title / identity + leaves + textual quantity + monetary value.**

Its corrections/addenda also make it a candidate for studying recursive audit rather than passive listing.

### High-value join

Item 8 (bills) and item 25 (list) should be treated together. The bills price production operations; the list prices individual textual objects. Together they may allow a reconstruction of how aggregate labour/material costs relate to work-level price.

## 7. 1829 — a second receipt shows nested units rather than one count

### Item 15, f. 147

Receipt for **seven boxes** of Nepali and Tibetan books sent by Hodgson to Fort William College, March 1829.

The catalogue says the boxes contained:

- 103 loose leaves;
- 13 packages;
- 101 copies.

The receipt was signed by H. Todd.

### Analytical importance

This is better than a simple “safe arrival” notice because it records multiple nested/heterogeneous count units:

**boxes → packages / loose leaves / copies.**

A shipment can therefore be audited at several resolutions, and the units do not map one-to-one.

The document should be compared with the 1827 `109 bundles` receipt to see whether the College’s receipt practices became more granular, or whether the different count structure reflects different cargo organisation.

## 8. 1843 — Wallich paper bill returns paper procurement to the foreground

### Item 3, f. 117

The catalogue describes a Hodgson-signed bill for:

- 1,012 `ḍhyāp` of Nepali paper;
- 10 sheets per `ḍhyāp`;
- two price rates;
- 3 pieces of wax cloth;
- thread and twine;
- total Nepali Rs 79.30.

The catalogue’s literal expansion of the two rates as `Rs 10` and `Rs 14` per bundle is arithmetically incompatible with the total. See:

`notes/WALLICH_1843_BILL_ARITHMETIC_RECONSTRUCTION.md`

The current provisional reconstruction is:

- 955 bundles @ 10 dam;
- 57 bundles @ 14 dam;
- 3 wax-cloth pieces @ Rs 13 each;
- thread/twine = 3 paisa;
- total = Rs 79 / 30 paisa.

This closes exactly under Buchanan Hamilton’s early nineteenth-century Nepalese account system but remains **image-unverified**.

### Historical importance even before image verification

The object puts **paper + waterproof/packing textile + tying material** into one bill. It therefore directly joins paper procurement and container construction.

## 9. A procurement chronology emerges

At catalogue level, the sequence can be rendered as follows:

| Date | Operation | Operational unit |
|---|---|---|
| 1824 | price copying | Rs / 100 stanzas |
| Feb 1825 | Government sanctions acquisition | institutional authorisation |
| May 1825 | proposed barter stock | Rs 1,000 weapons/equipment |
| Nov 1825 | cash replaces barter | money remittance |
| Apr/Jun/Nov 1827 | production bills | paper, copying, binding, labour, porters, packaging, shipment |
| May 1827 | arrival receipt | 109 manuscript bundles |
| 1827 | manuscript list | leaves, ślokas, price per manuscript |
| Mar 1829 | receipt | 7 boxes / 13 packages / 103 loose leaves / 101 copies |
| 1843 | paper/packing bill | 1,012 paper bundles + wax cloth + thread/twine |

This is not simply a chronology of collecting. It is a chronology of **increasingly explicit accounting interfaces** among text, labour, paper, containers, money and institutional custody.

## 10. What must be tested against the actual folios

### Items 8 and 25 are highest priority

For ff. 128–130 and ff. 179–182, extract:

- exact monetary units;
- named scribes or labour categories;
- quantities of paper purchased;
- paper grades / dimensions if stated;
- price per sheet, bundle or other unit;
- binding materials;
- carpenter tasks/materials;
- porter counts and rates;
- package/bundle counts;
- shipment charges;
- named manuscript titles and their leaves/ślokas/prices;
- subtotals and grand totals;
- corrections/addenda by Hodgson;
- any relation between list sequence and bundle sequence.

### Material inspection

Because item 8 is catalogued as mixed Nepali handmade and machine-made support, determine whether the support switches correspond to:

- different dates (April / June / November);
- different bill types;
- different hands;
- appended receipts;
- local versus Residency/accounting origin;
- later archival assembly.

Do not infer this correlation from the catalogue alone.

## 11. Article-level claim now available

A stronger formulation than “Hodgson commissioned manuscripts” is now possible:

> Hodgson’s manuscript projects were sustained by an accounting infrastructure that translated texts into commensurable units of stanzas, leaves, prices, paper purchases, wages, bundles, boxes and receipts. The surviving bills show copying, binding, packaging and shipment as one costed production chain, while later receipts changed the scale of verification from manuscript identity to bundle, package and box counts.

This connects manuscript production directly to the article’s auditability argument without reducing the manuscripts to doctrinal content or to passive cargo.

## 12. Failed-experiment / affordance connection

Wave 12 showed paper properties changing value across experimental interfaces. Wave 13 adds a parallel point from procurement:

**materials are also selected and valued through accounting interfaces.**

A paper grade, bundle size, wax-cloth piece or porter-day becomes actionable because it can be counted, priced and entered into a reimbursement system.

Thus the project now has two complementary routes into affordance:

1. **performance affordance** — what a material can do in an operation, exposed especially by failure;
2. **accounting affordance** — how a material/labour unit can be made commensurable enough to commission, reimburse, ship and audit.

Keep these analytically distinct until object-level evidence shows how strongly they interact.

---

## Bottom line

`Mss Eur Hodgson/12` now looks less like a miscellaneous volume of academic correspondence than an unusually dense record of a **manuscript-production economy**. From 1824 to 1843 it preserves price units, government authorisation, alternative exchange mechanisms, local paper purchase, scribal and manual labour, binding, bundling, porterage, shipment, arrival receipts, itemised manuscript metrics and later paper/packing procurement. The immediate archival payoff is therefore concentrated in just a few folios: **ff. 128–130, 176, 179–182, 147 and 117**.
