# Web research 2026-08-31 — Wave 25
## Calcutta stationery supply, inter-presidency redistribution and imported-paper maker plurality

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: supply-side follow-up to Waves 23–24. Scope remains paper itself. This note asks what `imported paper` in Hodgson's Calcutta/Bengal environment could materially mean, how official paper was supplied and quality-controlled, and whether Whatman can plausibly stand for British paper as a whole.

## Executive result

Calcutta should now be understood as a **stationery aggregation and redistribution node** supplied by multiple routes rather than a single paper market or manufacturing origin.

The strongest combined evidence is:

1. East India Company/Government stationery correspondence shows paper moving **London -> Calcutta**, **Madras -> Calcutta**, and onward from Calcutta into Bengal district offices;
2. shortages of specific paper grades such as watermarked Bill of Exchange paper triggered emergency replenishment from Britain;
3. complaints about the quality of English paper triggered requests for physical `musters`/samples rather than verbal descriptions alone;
4. an actual c.1830 Royal Botanic Garden, Calcutta manuscript survives on a **variety of watermarked papers ranging from Whatman 1826 to Radway 1828**.

Therefore:

`Calcutta imported paper != Whatman paper`.

The historically defensible supply model is:

`British/European makers + Company procurement + maritime shipment + inter-presidency redistribution + Calcutta stores/markets + institutional offices`.

Mechanisms:

- `CALCUTTA_AS_STATIONERY_AGGREGATION_NODE`
- `IMPORTED_PAPER_MAKER_PLURALITY_IN_CALCUTTA`
- `PRESIDENCY_TO_PRESIDENCY_STATIONERY_REDISTRIBUTION`
- `PAPER_MUSTER_AS_QUALITY_CONTROL_SAMPLE`
- `GRADE_SPECIFIC_STATIONERY_SHORTAGE`

# 1. Calcutta's official paper supply was structurally precarious

Tania Sengupta's archival study of colonial Bengal paper bureaucracy reconstructs a central stationery-supply system in which paper was issued to Bengal district cutcherries through Calcutta. She notes repeated London-Calcutta correspondence over shortages and quality, and stresses that official paper supply involved global and regional movement rather than a simple bilateral import stream.

Source:

Tania Sengupta, `Papered spaces: clerical practices, materialities, and spatial cultures of provincial governance in Bengal, Colonial India, 1820s–1860s`, *The Journal of Architecture* 25.2 (2020), 111–137.

UCL record:
https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10091644/

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1080/13602365.2020.1733861

### Specific supply events cited by Sengupta

- In **1809**, the stationery office in Calcutta reported a deficiency of **watermarked paper**.
- In response, **4,000 sheets of Bill of Exchange paper** were dispatched in 1810, with **another 2,000 sheets** to follow.
- In **1805**, Fort St George / Madras Presidency sent **100 reams of consultation paper** to Calcutta for Bengal use.
- Between 1808 and 1810, complaints from Indian Presidencies concerning **inferior-quality paper** caused London to demand actual `musters` of the papers at issue.

Sengupta's cited archival base includes India Office Public Department correspondence. Her notes identify, among related paper-supply evidence:

- Public Department letter, 28 February 1809;
- Fort St George Public Department, 4 September 1811;
- IOR/Z/E/4/39/S776, 1807–1813;
- Order E/4/908, p.39;
- Public Department letter, 11 June 1800, IOR/E/4/887, p.112.

The exact original reference attached to each numerical event should be rechecked in IOR before article quotation. At present the event-level figures are supported by Sengupta's archival synthesis.

# 2. Paper grade, not generic paper quantity, caused specific supply crises

The 1809 event is important because the shortage concerned **watermarked paper**, and the replenishment was described by grade/use as **Bill of Exchange paper**.

This indicates an official paper ontology in which interchangeable sheet count was insufficient.

Relevant variables already included:

- watermark/authentication state;
- paper grade;
- document use;
- sheet count;
- source route.

Thus procurement worked on differentiated paper classes.

Mechanism:

`GRADE_SPECIFIC_STATIONERY_SHORTAGE`.

For Hodgson-era objects, a catalogue label `machine-made` or `imported` is materially much coarser than historical procurement categories.

---

# 3. Inter-presidency redistribution complicates `imported` provenance

The 1805 movement of 100 reams of consultation paper from Madras to Calcutta shows that a paper object in Bengal could have passed through another Presidency supply system before use.

Therefore a Bengal/Calcutta document's route can be:

`Britain/Europe -> Madras -> Calcutta -> Bengal office`

as well as:

`Britain/Europe -> Calcutta -> office`.

A surviving paper's location of use is consequently a weak proxy for its shipping route.

Mechanism:

`PRESIDENCY_TO_PRESIDENCY_STATIONERY_REDISTRIBUTION`.

Database requirement:

- maker/production place;
- first known India port/Presidency;
- redistribution route;
- office of issue;
- document use location.

Do not collapse these into `origin`.

---

# 4. `Musters` made paper quality materially auditable across distance

The complaints of 1808–10 generated demands from London for physical paper `musters`.

This is a useful paper-history mechanism.

A quality complaint sent in prose does not preserve:

- thickness;
- sizing;
- surface;
- strength;
- watermark/formation;
- actual defects.

A physical sample does.

Thus the Company quality-control operation was:

`complaint -> sample/muster -> distant material inspection -> procurement correction`.

Mechanism:

`PAPER_MUSTER_AS_QUALITY_CONTROL_SAMPLE`.

This operation directly parallels Hodgson/Swinton's later use of finished Nepali sheets and pulp material for metropolitan testing.

The Company was already institutionally accustomed to solving paper-quality disputes by moving paper itself.

# 5. Calcutta Botanic Garden gives object-level evidence of maker plurality

The American Philosophical Society preserves a classified alphabetical index of the Royal Botanic Garden, Calcutta, dating to approximately 1830.

Collection:

`Mss.580.7.R81`, Royal Botanic Garden, Calcutta Index.

APS record:
https://as.amphilsoc.org/repositories/2/resources/396

The catalogue explicitly states that the index is written on **a variety of watermarked papers, ranging from Whatman (1826) to Radway (1828)**.

Eleven plant specimens were also folded between its pages.

This is extremely useful because the object is:

- Calcutta-based;
- scientific/administrative;
- almost exactly contemporary with Hodgson's early paper programme;
- physically mixed across multiple British watermarked paper makers/marks.

The secure conclusion is:

`Calcutta scientific office paper supply was materially heterogeneous at maker level`.

Mechanism:

`IMPORTED_PAPER_MAKER_PLURALITY_IN_CALCUTTA`.

The comparison should not be overstated. It does not prove Hodgson used Radway paper. It demonstrates that a nearby Company scientific institution in Calcutta used at least Whatman- and Radway-marked papers in one c.1830 documentary object.

This materially widens the candidate set for Hodgson's `imported` paper.

---

# 6. Whatman is one candidate among multiple British maker families

The APS Botanic Garden index makes a methodological point that supply-history alone could leave abstract.

A single Calcutta manuscript can combine Whatman and Radway paper.

Therefore object identification should proceed from physical evidence outward:

`sheet -> watermark/countermark -> maker chronology -> supply context`

rather than:

`Company/Calcutta context -> assumed Whatman -> sheet`.

This is especially important because:

- East India Company emblems occur on papers from multiple makers;
- successor firms used Whatman marks;
- Whatman brands were imitated in Continental Europe.

Mechanism:

`CONTEXT_NARROWS_CANDIDATES_BUT_DOES_NOT_IDENTIFY_MAKER`.

---

# 7. Calcutta official supply and open-market paper should remain separate

Later government stationery history shows the long-term institutionalisation of Calcutta as a central stationery depot, but the present project should avoid projecting the later `Central Stationery Office` bureaucracy unchanged into the 1820s–40s.

The Government of India Stationery Office's own institutional history dates the formal Stationery Office, Kolkata, to **1850**, with later central-government reorganisation in 1904.

Official history:
https://giso.gov.in/about-department/introduction/

For earlier decades, use historically specific labels from the actual correspondence rather than retroactively calling every supply operation the `Central Stationery Office` in its later bureaucratic form.

This is a source-control correction to broad secondary formulations.

Mechanism:

`LATER_INSTITUTION_NAME_NOT_RETROACTIVE_ORGANISATIONAL_IDENTITY`.

# 8. Supply candidates for Hodgson's imported paper

The current evidence suggests the following candidate categories for Hodgson-period `imported paper` used in Nepal/Calcutta-facing work:

### British handmade paper

Potentially including:

- Whatman/Balston family products;
- Radway-marked paper;
- other British makers supplying Company/private markets;
- EIC-emblem papers by multiple producers.

### British machine-made paper

Increasingly available after early nineteenth-century mechanisation, but not identifiable from `machine-made` in archival catalogue alone to a specific mill.

### Continental European paper

Possible through British and Indian trade networks; British production itself relied on Continental rag supply and European papers circulated widely. Object-level proof is required for specific Hodgson sheets.

### Bengal/Serampore paper

Local mechanised paper existed as a separate production stream. Calcutta purchase/use does not establish Serampore manufacture.

### Indian regional handmade paper

`country paper`, daftari, consultation/office-related and other regional paper markets remain possible in Bengal administrative environments.

The archive needs a candidate ontology wider than:

`Nepali / Whatman / machine`.

# 9. New paper-supply data model

For every imported/machine paper object record:

- `historical_paper_description`;
- `maker_mark_text`;
- `watermark_motif`;
- `countermark`;
- `watermark_date`;
- `laid_or_wove`;
- `laid_line_density`;
- `chain_line_spacing`;
- `machine_formation_evidence`;
- `sheet_dimensions`;
- `whole_half_quarter_sheet_state`;
- `paper_grade_if_known`;
- `supplier_or_stationer`;
- `known_shipping_route`;
- `office_or_market_of_issue`;
- `document_use_place`;
- `maker_attribution_confidence`.

This allows the same physical sheet to be connected to Calcutta supply without confusing location with manufacture.

# 10. Relation to Hodgson's 1831–43 paper programme

The emerging sequence is now:

### 1831–32

Hodgson uses machine-made correspondence paper to describe/send samples of Nepali paper manufacture.

### 1833

Himalayan pulp is tested by Cowan in Britain through a Calcutta–Edinburgh network.

### 1841–42

Skilled makers move to Darjeeling; paper and treatments are evaluated through Government of India/Calcutta; Serampore becomes the mechanised production test site.

### 1843

A machine-made accounting sheet records 1,012 bundles of Nepali paper and packing materials.

The imported papers supporting these operations should now be treated as objects within a heterogeneous Company supply market, not as anonymous European background.

## Article-level formulation

**Calcutta did not receive a homogeneous stream of `English paper`. Company offices obtained differentiated grades through a fragile supply system extending from London and other Presidencies into Bengal, and quality disputes were settled by sending physical paper musters back across the imperial network. A c.1830 index from the Royal Botanic Garden at Calcutta makes this multiplicity visible at object level: its leaves include watermarked Whatman 1826 and Radway 1828 papers within a single scientific manuscript. Such evidence changes how Hodgson's catalogue descriptions of `imported` or `machine-made` paper should be read. Whatman becomes a historically plausible supplier, not a default attribution. The Calcutta environment was a junction where multiple maker papers, regional Indian papers and locally mechanised production could coexist, be redistributed and be compared.**

## Bottom line

Calcutta is now materially legible as a paper-supply junction. Paper reached Bengal through Company imports, emergency replenishment, inter-presidency transfers and local markets; official users distinguished grades and watermarked classes; quality complaints travelled with physical musters; and surviving Calcutta scientific records prove that multiple British maker papers circulated side by side. Hodgson's imported paper should therefore be approached through a supply candidate set and physical identification protocol, not by substituting `Whatman` for `British` or `Calcutta` for `manufactured in Bengal`.
