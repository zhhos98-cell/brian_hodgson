# Web/archive research 2026-09-02 — Wave 65
## Commerce knowledge across heterogeneous paper supports: local source sheets, multilingual recopying, and publication-facing machine paper

Date: 2026-09-02

Status: active paper-history mainline. This wave follows the paper genealogy of Hodgson's 1831 commerce investigation rather than its economic argument. It asks how locally gathered commercial information was written, recopied, translated and stabilized across Nepali handmade and machine-made paper.

## Executive result

The 1831 commerce project is now one of the strongest paper-history cases in the repository because its surviving witness family crosses support, language, hand and documentary stage without a one-to-one mapping.

Current controlled nodes include:

1. **MSS EUR HODGSON/53 item 31, ff.211–216** — import/export goods and customs information collected by Hanumanta Sing; Nepali language; **machine-made and Nepali handmade paper mixed**; directly linked by the inventory to the commerce material later printed in 1874.
2. **MSS EUR HODGSON/60 item 14, ff.157–158** — lists of shops in Kathmandu and Patan; Nepali; **Nepali handmade paper**; explicitly cross-referenced to English note/translation in vol.6/5 and vol.14/16.
3. **MSS EUR HODGSON/53 item 24, ff.130–131** — recognised commercial marts/koṭhi and merchants; Nepali; **Nepali handmade paper**.
4. **MSS EUR HODGSON/94 item 15, ff.89–123b** — list of fabrics and daily commodities purchased at Calcutta and imported to Nepal, dated 13 September 1831; Farsi; **machine-made paper**.
5. **MSS EUR HODGSON/96 item 19, ff.154–161** — extensive Farsi list of commodities imported from Calcutta to Nepal, including Calcutta purchase price, transport cost and Kathmandu final price; **Nepali handmade paper**. Direct copy relation to vol.94/15 is not yet proven.
6. **MSS EUR HODGSON/14 item 18, ff.219–318** — draft of the Report on the Commerce of Nepal; English; **machine-made paper of various types**, multiple hands, Hodgson corrections/addenda.
7. **MSS EUR HODGSON/14 item 16, ff.177a–207** — fair copy of Hodgson's commerce report, including import/export tables; English; **machine-made paper**, excellent condition and fair hand.
8. **MSS EUR HODGSON/6 item 5, ff.42–76** — another English publication-facing copy; **machine-made paper**, very fine condition.

The paper-history result is not simply `local data on local paper -> English report on imported paper`.

The actual ecology is more heterogeneous:

`Nepali handmade source sheets`
+
`mixed Nepali/machine source sheets`
+
`Farsi on machine-made paper`
+
`Farsi on Nepali handmade paper`
+
`English draft/fair copies on multiple machine-made stocks`.

Therefore:

**paper support was allocated by operation within a mixed Residency stock ecology; support origin did not determine language, source status or documentary stage.**

Core mechanisms:

- `COMMERCE_KNOWLEDGE_HAS_A_PAPER_GENEALOGY`
- `SUPPORT_ORIGIN_NOT_LANGUAGE_IDENTITY`
- `SOURCE_STATUS_NOT_SUPPORT_IDENTITY`
- `LOCAL_DATA_CAN_ENTER_IMPORTED_MACHINE_PAPER_BEFORE_ENGLISH_TRANSLATION`
- `SAME_INFORMATION_DOMAIN_CAN_EXIST_ON_BOTH_NEPALI_AND_MACHINE_PAPER`
- `PUBLICATION_FACING_FAIR_COPY_CONCENTRATES_ON_MACHINE_PAPER`
- `PAPER_ALLOCATION_IS_OPERATIONAL_NOT_NATIONAL`.

## 7. The commerce report is a strong negative case against language = paper

Controlled observations:

| Documentary state | Language | Support |
|---|---|---|
| Hanumanta Sing trade/customs data, vol.53/31 | Nepali | mixed machine + Nepali handmade |
| Kathmandu/Patan shop lists, vol.60/14 | Nepali | Nepali handmade |
| commercial marts, vol.53/24 | Nepali | Nepali handmade |
| Calcutta-price list, vol.94/15 | Farsi | machine-made |
| Calcutta-import list, vol.96/19 | Farsi | Nepali handmade |
| English commerce draft, vol.14/18 | English | machine-made, various types |
| English fair report, vol.14/16 | English | machine-made |
| parallel English copy, vol.6/5 | English | machine-made |

The correct model is not:

`Nepali -> Nepali paper`
`Farsi -> intermediate paper`
`English -> European paper`.

It is:

`information operation × available stock × writer/copyist × documentary stage -> selected support`.

Language may correlate with some stages, but support cannot be inferred from language.

## 8. Commerce information moves by material recopying, not abstract translation alone

The project can now be reconstructed as a paper workflow:

`local observation / merchant information`
-> `Nepali source lists and mixed-support working sheets`
-> `Farsi quantitative recopying / tabulation on both support classes`
-> `English rough/fair report on heterogeneous machine stocks`
-> `Government submission`
-> `1857 print`
-> `1874 collected reprint`.

Not every source node can yet be placed on one direct stemma, so this is a **workflow ecology**, not a proven single chain.

The material point is nevertheless secure:

**commercial knowledge became portable by being repeatedly rewritten onto new paper surfaces whose support class and format changed with the operation.**

Mechanisms:

- `RECOPYING_IS_MATERIAL_TRANSLATION`
- `PAPER_CHANGE_CAN_ACCOMPANY_AGGREGATION_AND_LANGUAGE_CHANGE`
- `KNOWLEDGE_PORTABILITY_REQUIRES_NEW_SURFACES`.

## 9. Relation to Wave 64: two paper economies meet inside the Residency

Wave 64 reconstructed local Nepali-paper procurement for manuscript production:

`local paper purchase`
-> `copying`
-> `binding`
-> `bundling/packaging`
-> `shipment to Calcutta`.

Wave 65 reconstructs a second operation:

`local/commercial information collection`
-> `multilingual recopying across mixed paper stocks`
-> `English machine-paper administrative report`
-> `print`.

The two systems intersect at the Residency but use paper differently.

### Manuscript-production circuit

Paper is a purchased raw/working input that becomes copied books.

### Commerce-research circuit

Paper is a sequence of information surfaces across which lists, prices, classifications and translations are repeatedly stabilized.

This gives a stronger article-level formulation:

**the Residency was simultaneously a paper-consuming production office and a paper-converting information office.**

Mechanisms:

- `RESIDENCY_AS_PAPER_ALLOCATION_NODE`
- `RESIDENCY_AS_MATERIAL_INFORMATION_CONVERSION_NODE`.

## 10. Does the 1830–31 import table include paper as a traded commodity?

The 1874 printed commerce text has now been checked through direct Internet Archive OCR at the tariff/list section.

The visible principal import categories include European woollens, cottons, silks and linens; shawls and muslins; cutlery; glassware; mirrors/window glass; groceries/drugs/dyes/spices; peltry; metals/minerals and precious stones.

OCR search does not recover `writing paper`, `stationery` or a clear paper commodity entry in the accessible printed text.

Source:
https://archive.org/stream/essaysonlanguage00hodg/essaysonlanguage00hodg_djvu.txt

This is **not proof that imported paper was absent from Calcutta–Nepal commerce**. The printed trade table is selective, OCR of the numerical tables is badly damaged, and Residency/stationery procurement could operate through channels invisible in a general merchant commodity list.

Current state:

`IMPORTED_DRAWING/WRITING_PAPER_IN_GENERAL_TRADE_TABLE = NOT_YET_DIRECTLY_IDENTIFIED`.

Do not use absence from the printed commodity summary to negate the object-level evidence for imported papers in Hodgson-associated drawings and manuscripts.

## 11. New paper-history distinction: stock source vs information source

The commerce case makes two meanings of `source` collide.

### Information source

Merchant, shop, price list, customs record, local informant, vernacular table.

### Paper-stock source

Local handmade stock, imported/machine-made stock, mixed retained stock.

These variables are independent.

A local information source can be written on imported machine paper; a Farsi recopy can be written on Nepali handmade paper; an English draft can consume several unidentified machine stocks.

Therefore add to the paper census:

- `information_provenance`;
- `support_provenance`;
- `documentary_operation`;
- `language`;
- `writer/copyist`;
- `copy_relation`.

Do not collapse any two into one field.

## Source-control rules

1. `MSS EUR HODGSON/53 item 31` is direct catalogue evidence for mixed machine/Nepali support inside Hanumanta Sing's trade/customs data.
2. `vol.60/14 -> vol.6/5 / vol.14/16` has an explicit catalogue cross-reference and can be used as a controlled source/translation relation.
3. `vol.94/15 <-> vol.96/19` remains a possible copy/abstract family; thematic overlap is not enough to assert direct descent.
4. Do not infer a unique maker or mill from the category `machine-made paper`.
5. Do not infer paper support from language.
6. Do not infer documentary stage from support class.
7. The apparent concentration of English fair-copy states on machine paper is a real local pattern, not a universal rule.
8. Absence of a clearly OCR-readable `paper` commodity in the printed import list is not evidence that imported stationery/drawing paper did not circulate.
9. Treat every recopying event as a potential support-allocation decision.

## Bottom line

The commerce project materially reorganizes the article's paper history. Hodgson's 1831 report did not emerge from one paper stock or one clean vernacular-to-English translation path. Local market data survive on Nepali handmade and mixed supports; Farsi commercial lists survive on both machine-made and Nepali handmade paper; English drafts and fair copies concentrate on machine-made sheets but themselves use multiple unidentified stocks. The operative historical object is therefore a **heterogeneous paper ecology through which information was repeatedly recopied, translated, aggregated and made administratively portable**. Paper support was selected within that workflow, not dictated by language, national origin or a single documentary stage.
