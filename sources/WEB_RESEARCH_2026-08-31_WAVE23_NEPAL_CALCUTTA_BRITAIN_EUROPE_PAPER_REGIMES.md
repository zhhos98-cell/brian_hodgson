# Web research 2026-08-31 — Wave 23
## Nepali, Calcutta/Serampore, British and European paper as material regimes

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: paper-material reset. This wave deliberately suspends the current visual-surrogate and manuscript-format lines and concentrates on paper itself: fibre, pulp, sheet formation, sizing/finishing, mechanisation, watermark/countermark, commodity form, testing, transport and procurement. Geographic labels are treated as historically unstable unless production place is independently established.

## Executive result

The strongest result is that paper properties are not carried intact by fibre identity or geographical name.

The Hodgson paper record now supports a material sequence in which the same Himalayan paper material behaves differently depending on which manufacturing system converts it into a sheet:

`Himalayan bark/fibre -> local pulp preparation -> local hand-sheet formation -> tough/flexible finished sheet`

versus

`Himalayan bark/fibre -> dried pulp brick -> long-distance transport/storage -> British reprocessing/bleaching -> British sheet -> brittle/stiff/poor result`.

This is demonstrated directly by the contrast between Hodgson's 1832 account and the 1833 Asiatic Society report of British trials.

The implication is stronger than a generic affordance claim:

**a finished sheet embodies a production history. Raw material provenance alone does not determine paper performance.**

Mechanisms:

- `PROCESS_HISTORY_EMBODIED_IN_SHEET`
- `FIBRE_IDENTITY_DOES_NOT_PRESERVE_PAPER_PROPERTIES`
- `FINISHED_SHEET_VS_PULP_TRANSFER_DIVERGENCE`
- `MATERIAL_TRANSFER_WITHOUT_PROCESS_TRANSFER`

A second correction follows. `Calcutta paper` should not be used as a paper-origin class unless an object/source explicitly gives a Calcutta manufacture. In the Hodgson material system, Calcutta is primarily a market, government, scientific, postal, printing and distribution hub. Serampore, near Calcutta, is the clearly documented mechanised manufacturing/testing interface.

A third correction concerns `British/European paper`. British Company paper is itself heterogeneous, and European paper is more heterogeneous still. East India Company emblems do not identify Whatman by themselves; `J WHATMAN` likewise requires countermark/date/mould evidence because the mark was continued by successor firms and imitated on the Continent.

# I. Nepali paper: an operative manufacturing system, not an ethnic material label

## 1. Hodgson's 1832 process

Primary source:

B. H. Hodgson, `On the Native Method of making the Paper, denominated in Hindustan, Nipalese`, *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* 1 (January 1832), 8–11; dateline `Nepal Residency, Nov. 1831`.

Digital source:

- Google Books, JASB vol. 1, 1832: https://books.google.com/books?id=K64IAAAAQAAJ
- Pahar scan/extract: https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1832%20Native%20Method%20of%20Making%20Paper%20in%20Hindustan%20Nipalese%20by%20Hodgson%20from%20JASBv1%20s.pdf

Hodgson's account specifies an integrated sequence:

`inner bark -> alkaline ash extract -> boiling -> beating -> dilution/churning -> coarse screening -> floating cloth-bottom frame -> manual pulp distribution -> drainage -> fire drying -> peeling -> folding -> optional polishing`.

The equipment includes stone, hardwood, wicker, ceramic, metal, cloth, clear water, firewood and ash. Paper therefore emerges from an operational ecology rather than fibre alone.

### Thickness as a manufactured variable

Hodgson explicitly states that the quantity of pulp distributed over the frame is made thicker or thinner according to the strength of paper required.

This means `Nepali paper` cannot be treated as one thickness or grade.

Mechanism:

`INTENTIONAL_THICKNESS_CONTROL_AT_FORMATION`.

### Sheet-forming surface

The frame is buoyant and cloth-bottomed rather than a normal European laid/wove wire mould. This predicts a different family of physical traces.

Object inspection should record:

- textile/mesh impression;
- fibre distribution;
- formation-side versus exposed-side texture;
- edge structure;
- absence/presence of European-style laid and chain lines;
- actual surface burnishing.

### Finishing and ink behaviour

Hodgson reports occasional polishing with the convex side of a conch shell or a smooth hardwood tool, especially for larger sheets. He says no size was normally required to prevent ink running.

This is not a licence to call every Hodgson-period Nepali sheet `unsized`. It is a historically specific description of the process he observed.

Mechanism:

`WRITABLE_SURFACE_WITHOUT_REPORTED_ROUTINE_SIZING`.

### Large-sheet capability

Hodgson reports fine smooth sheets several yards square.

This makes large format an indigenous manufacturing affordance rather than something introduced only by imported broad-sheet stock.

### Commodity states

Hodgson reports two purchasable states at Kathmandu:

- finished paper;
- dried pulp formed as bricks/tiles for transport.

Thus the material ontology must distinguish:

`bark != prepared pulp != dried pulp brick != finished sheet != folded/bundled paper commodity`.

---

## 2. `Nipalese` is a market name, not a secure production-place label

Hodgson explicitly says the paper called `Nipalese` was not principally made in Nepal proper. He locates manufacture among Cis-Himalayan Bhote communities east of the Kali River, with substantial manufacture above Nepal proper, while Kathmandu/Nepal supplied a major market.

Therefore code separately:

- historical paper name;
- fibre/raw-material region;
- sheet-production location;
- market/purchase location;
- transport route;
- archive's modern paper label.

Mechanism:

`MARKET_NAME_VS_PRODUCTION_ORIGIN`.

The word `Nepali` in a nineteenth-century catalogue cannot be silently converted into modern national manufacture.

# II. The 1833 British trials: the same material does not reproduce the same paper

## 3. Source-control correction to Hodgson's optimistic 1832 postscript

Hodgson's 1832 postscript says dried pulp had been sent to England and judged excellent for manufacturing paper suitable for proof engravings.

One year later the Asiatic Society printed a much more specific report of actual British manufacturers' trials:

`Read a letter from Mr. G. Swinton, communicating correspondence between Mr. Robison, Sec. Ed. Roy. Society, and several paper manufacturers, on the subject of the Nipal Paper Stuff.`

Primary source:

*Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* 2 (1833), proceedings p. 93.

Digital scan/text:

https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9c/Journal_of_the_Asiatic_Society_of_Bengal_%28IA_journalofasiatic02asia%29.pdf

Pahar mirror:

https://pahar.in/pahar/Journals/Asiatic%20Society%20of%20Bengal%201788-1921/Journal%20of%20Asiatic%20Society%20of%20Bengal%201832-1923/1833%20Journal%20Asiatic%20Society%20of%20Bengal%20Vol%202%20s.pdf

### British remanufacture from dried pulp brick

The dried pulp sent home by Swinton in 1831 was worked by British paper manufacturers. The report says the trials were `not at all successful`.

Charles Cowan's specimens lacked the softness and toughness of paper manufactured in Nepal by the process Hodgson described. They were reported as:

- brittle;
- stiff;
- transparent, as though varnished;
- full of gritty brown spots;
- difficult to whiten satisfactorily.

The report further states that the material retained water obstinately on the sieve and shrank markedly on drying.

A strongly bleached batch consumed much more bleaching chemistry than ordinary material and was thought likely to have been injured in texture. Exact reagent quantities should be image-verified before publication quotation, although the searchable scan reads approximately 10 lb dry chloride of lime and 2 lb sulphuric acid for 90 lb material.

Mechanisms:

- `METROPOLITAN_REMANUFACTURE_FAILURE`
- `BLEACHING_CHEMISTRY_PROPERTY_DAMAGE`
- `DRAINAGE_AND_DRYING_FAILURE`

### Finished Nepal-made sheets sent to Britain

The report then gives an internal control of exceptional value.

Thin sheets made in Nepal and sent home by Swinton in 1829 were described as:

- exceedingly tough;
- flexible;
- not quite white;
- resembling `India paper`;
- capable of taking the minute impression of a bank-note plate with `perfect fidelity`.

The same report says the raw stuff itself had initially been very tough, suggesting that deterioration occurred after preparation, during storage/transport or subsequent processing.

This comparison yields the project's strongest paper-material claim so far:

`same broad fibre/material source + different manufacturing/transport history -> different finished paper properties`.

The operative contrast is not:

`Nepal fibre vs British fibre`.

It is:

`Nepal-formed sheet vs Britain-remanufactured sheet from transported Nepal pulp`.

Mechanisms:

- `PROCESS_HISTORY_EMBODIED_IN_SHEET`
- `FINISHED_SHEET_OUTPERFORMS_METROPOLITAN_REMANUFACTURE`
- `PULP_BRICK_STABILISES_TRANSPORT_NOT_SHEET_AFFORDANCE`

### Historiographical significance

A fibre-centred material history would miss the central phenomenon. Papermaking is a transformation system. Local water, ash liquor, beating, fibre dispersion, cloth-frame formation, drainage, drying and finishing jointly produce the sheet.

Transporting fibre or pulp does not transport this process history.

# III. Darjeeling–Calcutta–Serampore: experimental paper infrastructure

## 4. Darjeeling is a technology-transfer reconstruction site

Primary source already parsed in `data/paper_experiment_census_v0_1.json`:

`Correspondence relative to the Manufacture of Nipal Paper at Darjeeling, with details as to its cost, relative value, mode of bleaching, and rendering water proof`, *Journal of the Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India* 1 (1842), 210–221, sequel 273–274.

The experiment does not merely move a recipe. It moves:

- five skilled Nepali papermakers;
- wage hierarchy;
- bark/raw-material access;
- tools;
- water and workshop siting;
- Hodgson's earlier written specification;
- government supervision;
- finished experimental sheets.

Observed divergences from Hodgson's printed description include flat stone/double-headed mallets and rejection of iron boilers because iron darkened the paper. Tinned copper was preferred.

Mechanism:

`TACIT_CRAFT_KNOWLEDGE_EXCEEDS_PRINTED_RECIPE`.

## 5. Bleaching exposes a property trade-off

Campbell found that treatments capable of whitening the material could reduce the toughness that made Nepali paper attractive.

This is consistent with the British 1833 evidence: whiteness was not a free improvement variable.

Mechanism:

`WHITENESS_STRENGTH_TRADEOFF`.

Colour therefore belongs to a property bundle rather than a simple scale from inferior dark paper to superior white paper.

## 6. Calcutta is a hub, not yet a paper-origin category

In the 1841–42 dossier, paper and instructions move through Government of India and scientific/administrative actors in Calcutta. Calcutta authorities also evaluate treated paper for postal use.

For this project, code Calcutta primarily as:

- government commissioning/evaluation node;
- scientific correspondence node;
- postal-operational test site;
- market/procurement node;
- printing/publication node;
- transit hub between Himalaya, Bengal and Britain.

Do not create a material category `Calcutta paper` from place of purchase, testing or receipt.

Mechanism:

`CALCUTTA_AS_PAPER_EVALUATION_AND_DISTRIBUTION_HUB`.

Required data distinction:

`manufacture_place != purchase_place != test_place != print_place != administrative_destination`.

## 7. Serampore is the nearby mechanised production/interface site

The Government of India's Central Pulp & Paper Research Institute identifies Serampore, West Bengal, as the site of India's first mechanised paper mill, established in 1812.

Official reference:

https://www.cppri.res.in/en/about-us/paper-museum-kagaj-sangralya/historical-perspective

In the Hodgson/Darjeeling 1842 experiments, O'Shaughnessy's bleaching work was passed to Marshman at Serampore Paper Mills for practical larger-scale testing.

The result is an explicit interface failure. On the machine web, the same bark material's mucilaginous behaviour impeded free flow when greater thickness was attempted. Marshman doubted its general economic suitability for machinery then in use, while still identifying strength-dominant niche uses as plausible.

Mechanism:

`SERAMPORE_MACHINE_INTERFACE`

and more specifically:

`HAND_SHEET_STRENGTH_PROPERTY_BECOMES_MACHINE_WEB_DEFECT`.

This is not simply `traditional versus modern` technology. It is a material/interface relation: a property bundle desirable in one production system becomes costly or obstructive in another.

# IV. British paper: rag, mould, wove structure, machine transition and Company supply

## 8. Britain itself contains multiple paper technologies

The British Association of Paper Historians summarises two major eighteenth-century changes:

- the Hollander/rag engine replacing older stamping methods;
- Whatman's wove mould producing a smoother sheet without laid/chain-line relief.

Reference:

https://baph.org.uk/resources/paper-history/history-of-papermaking-in-the-uk/

BAPH dates the first successful British continuous papermachine at Frogmore to 1803 and notes the rapid early nineteenth-century mechanisation of British manufacture.

Therefore `British paper` in Hodgson's lifetime can include:

- handmade laid rag paper;
- handmade wove rag paper;
- machine-made paper;
- writing, drawing, printing, cartridge/filtering and other grades;
- different sizing and finishing systems.

A catalogue phrase `imported paper` cannot be promoted to any one of these without object-level evidence.

## 9. British rag supply was not materially self-contained

BAPH estimates that by 1800 England and Wales had about 430 paper mills and that British production consumed very large quantities of rags; domestic paper demand was supplemented by imports, mainly from the Continent.

This matters conceptually:

`British paper manufacture` is not equivalent to `British raw-material autarky`.

The metropolitan paper system was already a trans-European material economy.

Mechanism:

`BRITISH_PAPER_DEPENDS_ON_TRANSREGIONAL_RAW_MATERIAL_AND_SUPPLY`.

## 10. Bleaching failure has a British parallel

BAPH notes that excessive chlorine bleaching in early British papermaking produced poorer-quality papers that deteriorated rapidly.

This contextualises the Hodgson/Campbell/Cowan bleaching failures without reducing them to a uniquely Himalayan fibre problem.

The relevant general operation is:

`chemical whitening can damage fibre integrity`.

# V. Whatman and East India Company paper: supplier relation without watermark shortcut

## 11. Whatman paper was deliberately connected to Indian service

A modern material-history study of East India Company-associated paper, citing Thomas Balston's histories of Whatman, reports that the Company was among Whatman's best customers. It reproduces a draft letter written on paper countermarked `J WHATMAN 1804` stating that one of Whatman's early objectives was to supply papers able to `stand the Indian climates`, displacing Dutch papers previously used by the Company.

Secondary research source:

UAL thesis, p. 198:
https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/19712/1/871190.pdf

This claim should be cited through Balston when possible in publication. At present its evidential state is:

`secondary quotation of historical business correspondence`.

The same study, again citing Balston, reports an East India Company paper order of £3,116 received on 14 April 1814 and describes Balston's later concentration on high-grade handmade writing/drawing papers using good raw materials and vat production.

This supports a supply context, not identification of any specific Hodgson sheet.

Mechanism:

`BRITISH_EIC_CLIMATE_SPECIFIC_PAPER_SUPPLY`.

## 12. Object-level example: EIC watermark + Whatman countermark

The John Rylands/Manchester Digital Collections record for *Farhang-i Jahāngīrī* describes early nineteenth-century British rebinding paper as:

- English handmade paper;
- approximately 9 laid lines/cm;
- approximately 23 mm chain-line spacing;
- East India Company insignia watermark;
- countermark `J. Whatman 1811`.

Record:
https://www.digitalcollections.manchester.ac.uk/view/MS-PERSIAN-00922/9

A second Rylands manuscript has endpapers bearing a heart-shaped EIC insignia dated 1820 and countermark `J Whatman Balston & Co. 1820`:

https://www.digitalcollections.manchester.ac.uk/view/MS-PERSIAN-00795

These are useful diagnostic comparators for physical inspection of Hodgson papers.

They demonstrate that one sheet can contain both a Company emblem and a maker/successor countermark.

## 13. EIC/VEIC emblem is not maker identity

The Company emblem circulated on papers from more than one British producer. Research on papers in VOC/colonial archives reports British papers bearing `VEIC in a heart` associated with makers including WISE during the British occupation of Java.

Therefore:

`VEIC/EIC watermark -> Company-associated paper context`

but not:

`VEIC/EIC watermark -> Whatman`.

Mechanism:

`COMPANY_WATERMARK_NOT_MAKER_IDENTITY`.

For Hodgson object identification require, ideally:

- watermark motif;
- countermark/name;
- date;
- laid/wove structure;
- chain/laid spacing if laid;
- whole-sheet/half-sheet reconstruction;
- paper dimensions;
- document date;
- known maker chronology.

# VI. European paper: plurality before and inside British supply

## 14. Dutch paper was a major predecessor regime

The Dutch book-history handbook reports that eighteenth-century Dutch paper was a major high-quality export product, with quality associated especially with strong sizing. Important maker families included Blauw, Honig, Van der Ley and Van Gerrevinck.

Reference:
https://www.nederlandseboekgeschiedenis.nl/en/handbook/1725-1830-domestic-orientation/313-1725-1830-paper-incl-production-watermarks-paper-trade

The same source stresses that French paper continued to enter the Dutch market, often in lower-cost grades for cheap print production.

Thus even `Dutch paper` was embedded in European trade and grade differentiation.

The Whatman/EIC historical claim that British paper displaced earlier Dutch Company supply should therefore be understood as a supplier/technical transition inside an already international European paper market.

Mechanism:

`EUROPEAN_PAPER_REGIME_PLURALITY`.

## 15. Wove technology itself crossed borders

The Dutch paper-history handbook notes that Whatman's wove-paper innovation was imitated/adopted in the Netherlands from the early nineteenth century, with Dutch production beginning around 1807 after attempts to learn the technique.

Technology, product style and national origin therefore separate quickly.

A wove sheet is not automatically English.

## 16. `J WHATMAN` is not infallible provenance

The British Association of Paper Historians explicitly lists scholarship on forged Whatman marks and notes that European papermakers appropriated marks associated with quality.

Reference:
https://baph.org.uk/resources/article-archive/1989-2000/1996-2/

Material studies document counterfeit/imitation `J Whatman` papers produced in France, Germany and Austria in the first half of the nineteenth century.

Thus:

`J WHATMAN watermark alone != secure Whatman-mill attribution`.

Mechanism:

`WATERMARK_BRAND_REPUTATION_EXCEEDS_MAKER_IDENTITY`.

This strengthens the repo's existing policy that `J WHATMAN`, `VEIC`, British-looking wove structure or high-quality imported paper must never be used alone to identify a Hodgson sheet as Whatman.

# VII. Comparative material matrix

| Variable | Hodgson-described Nepali handmade | Serampore / Bengal mechanised interface | British handmade/wove | British machine | Dutch / wider European handmade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| principal historical fibre | Himalayan inner bark | experimental Himalayan bark plus mill furnishes | principally rag | initially principally rag | principally rag |
| pulp chemistry | ash-alkali cooking | experimental bleaching / industrial treatments | fermentation/beating; later chlorine bleaching | industrial preparation/bleaching | rag preparation; strong sizing prominent in Dutch quality paper |
| sheet formation | floating cloth-bottom frame | moving machine web | laid or wove hand mould | continuous web | mainly hand mould; laid, later wove |
| thickness control | pulp quantity manually varied | web flow/thickness became problem with Himalayan pulp | mould/vat practice | machine flow/calendering | mould/vat practice |
| routine sizing | Hodgson reports normally unnecessary | industrial sizing tested | common, especially writing papers | industrial sizing/finishing | Dutch paper particularly known for sizing |
| drying | frame near fire | mill process | loft/press/dry system | increasingly continuous | hand-paper drying system |
| diagnostic formation marks | likely textile/frame traces; verify | machine-web structure | laid/chain or wove; watermark/countermark | machine structure; may carry later marks | laid/chain/watermark; wove after adoption |
| commodity state | sheet, bundle, dried pulp brick | manufactured sheets/experimental paper | sheet/ream/grade | roll or cut sheet | sheet/ream/grade |
| strong observed affordance | toughness/durability; broad size range; writable surface | scalability but Himalayan pulp caused web-flow problems | writing/drawing/printing quality; EIC-specific supply | volume, regularity, lower unit cost | strong sized writing/printing/export grades |
| strong observed failure | wet bark/pulp custody; process dependence | mucilage/web flow; bleaching cost; thickness control | chlorine damage possible; rag scarcity | early machine quality often below best handmade | slower adoption of wove/machine technologies in Dutch case |

This table is a comparison of production systems, not a claim that every surviving paper bearing a geographic catalogue label has all these properties.

# VIII. Article-level reformulation

The strongest paper-material thesis now available is:

**Hodgson's paper world joined distinct manufacturing regimes rather than merely distinct geographical papers. Himalayan bark could become a tough, flexible writing and printing sheet when processed and formed locally, yet the same material, stabilised as dried pulp and remanufactured in Britain, produced brittle and disappointing paper. Reconstructing the craft at Darjeeling and feeding the fibre into the Serampore machine web generated further property reversals, while British Company paper itself belonged to a differentiated rag-paper economy of handmade laid and wove sheets, machine papers, maker countermarks and products deliberately adapted to Indian service. The relevant historical unit is therefore not `Nepali`, `Calcutta` or `European` paper as a nationality, but a material-production regime linking fibre, chemistry, formation technology, finishing, commodity state and route of circulation.**

The strongest compact formula is:

`paper properties = fibre × preparation × sheet-formation interface × finishing × transport/storage history × intended operation`.

Not:

`paper properties = geographic origin`.

# IX. Immediate archive tests

The next object-level pass should concentrate only on physical paper diagnostics:

1. `MSS EUR HODGSON/6 ff.77–81` — machine-made covering letter that transmitted the 1831 Nepali paper account/sample: inspect laid/wove state, watermark/countermark, full-sheet relation and whether the actual Nepali sample survives.
2. `MSS EUR HODGSON/12 f.117` — 1843 bill for 1,012 bundles of Nepali paper: identify the accounting sheet's imported/machine paper and compare it materially with the commodity it enumerates.
3. `MSS EUR HODGSON/1 ff.216–223` — two imported papers, thick and thin: measure actual caliper, formation, watermark and conjugacy before inferring functional difference.
4. Hodgson-era Nepali handmade sheets in BL — look specifically for cloth-frame formation traces, burnishing, thickness variation and ink penetration rather than European watermark features.
5. Linnean and Kew Hodgson sheets — inspect watermark/countermark and laid/wove structure before applying `British`, `Whatman`, or `European` maker labels.

## Source-control rule

No object should be called `Whatman` from prestige, whiteness, wove appearance, `imported paper`, or East India Company context alone.

No object should be called `Nepal-manufactured` merely because the archive labels it `Nepali hand-made paper` without distinguishing historical market name from production origin.

No object should be called `Calcutta paper` merely because it was bought, received, printed, tested or shipped in Calcutta.

## Bottom line

The new 1833 control is the hinge. Finished Nepal-made paper crossed to Britain with its toughness and fine-impression capability intact; transported Himalayan pulp did not reproduce those qualities when converted into paper by British manufacturers. Paper therefore carries the history of its making in a way raw fibre provenance cannot substitute for. Calcutta and Serampore then appear not as a vague middle geography but as distinct institutional and industrial interfaces where paper materials were tested, chemically modified, compared, priced and fed into mechanised production. British and European paper must receive the same treatment: maker, mould, sizing, machine state, watermark/countermark and supply route must be reconstructed rather than compressed into national labels.
