# Web research 2026-09-01 — Wave 44
## The `thar` branch of 1864 Item 12: a multilingual material micro-stemma without forced direction

Date: 2026-09-01

Status: paper-centred micro-reconstruction following Wave 43. This wave narrows the probable 1864 Item 12 multilingual family to one classificatory operation: lists and classifications of `thar` / family names / hill groups.

## Executive result

The `thar` problem survives across at least four language/support combinations:

- English + machine-made paper;
- Farsi + machine-made paper;
- Nepali + Nepali hand-made paper;
- Urdu + Nepali hand-made paper.

It also survives in multiple documentary states:

- fair classification copy;
- rough Hodgson working notes;
- local family-name/gotra lists;
- genealogy + family-name composite;
- Farsi fair list;
- Urdu very neat list;
- printed English publication.

This is the strongest compact falsification so far of any model in which language determines support.

More importantly, it reveals a **multilingual material version ecology around one classificatory operation**.

The network is not yet written as a one-way stemma because most Cambridge relations are `see also` / content-family cross-references rather than declared translation directions.

Mechanisms:

- `MULTILINGUAL_THAR_VERSION_ECOLOGY`
- `SAME_CLASSIFICATORY_OPERATION_CROSSES_SUPPORT_REGIMES`
- `VERSION_FAMILY_NOT_LANGUAGE_PAPER_BINARY`
- `CATALOGUE_RELATION_REQUIRES_SEMANTIC_CONTROL`.

## 2. English machine paper also preserves a rough working state

`vol.7/20`, dated 1831, is:

`Castes and clans of various hill ethnic groups, genealogy of ruling Shah dynasty`.

- machine-made paper;
- Hodgson's own rough writing in pen and pencil;
- includes Brahman family names, Khasa thars, high-official castes, Newar castes, Magar family names, Gurung clans and a Shah genealogy;
- old English title: `Castes of Parbattiah army tribes`.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol007.html

It is broader than the fair `Origin and classification` copies, so it should not automatically be called their draft.

But it demonstrates that the same classificatory problem existed as a rough working surface before the fair 1832 state.

Mechanism:

`ROUGH_ANALYTICAL_STATE_WITHIN_CLASSIFICATORY_FAMILY`.

## 4. Farsi machine paper: same classification problem, different language without support switch

`vol.101/8, ff.117v–118` is:

`Principal family names of the Khasas of Nepal`.

- Farsi;
- machine-made paper;
- fair hands;
- a list of Khasa thars from Thapa through Khatri and Bohara;
- Cambridge cross-references `vol.9/10`, `vol.56/2`, `vol.57/2` and `vol.22/9`;
- points to the same Hodgson 1833/1874 publication family.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol101.html

This node is crucial because it supplies a near-control against an English-machine-paper equation.

`English classification -> machine paper`

AND

`Farsi classification -> machine paper`.

Machine support therefore cannot be explained by English language alone.

Mechanism:

`SAME_SUPPORT_ACROSS_DIFFERENT_LANGUAGES_IN_SAME_OPERATION`.

## 5. Urdu handmade paper supplies the opposite control

`vol.103/18, ff.151–155` is:

`Various family names of Hindu Hill Brahmins and Chetris`.

- Urdu;
- Nepali hand-made paper;
- very neat hand;
- Nepali title description;
- explicitly cross-referenced to `vol.58/1`, `vol.51/14`, `vol.56/2`.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol103.html

Together with the Nepali-handmade nodes, this gives the mirror result:

`Nepali classification -> Nepali handmade`

AND

`Urdu classification -> Nepali handmade`.

Thus handmade support is no more a Nepali-language marker than machine support is an English-language marker.

The `thar` network contains two internal controls at once:

`English + Farsi on machine`

and

`Nepali + Urdu on handmade`.

This is much stronger than a miscellaneous list of counterexamples.

Even within one language, support varies.

This further rules out a language-to-paper allocation rule.

## 7. vol.57/2 is a broader Khasa context, not automatically a direct copy

`vol.57/2` is a Nepali-handmade account of the Khasas covering customs, life-cycle rites, ritual, dress, ornaments and social hierarchy.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol057.html

`vol.101/8` explicitly cross-references it.

However, the modern descriptions are different in documentary scope:

`vol.101/8 = short family-name list`

versus

`vol.57/2 = broad customs/manners account`.

Therefore the edge is preserved as:

`C — broader source/context family`,

not a direct translation/copy relation.

This is necessary to prevent the catalogue's `see` relation from becoming a false textual stemma.

## 8. A catalogue cross-reference anomaly: vol.101/8 -> vol.22/9

Cambridge also points from the Farsi family-name list `vol.101/8` to `vol.22/9 ff.186–243`.

But `vol.22/9` is catalogued as:

`Comparative vocabulary and sample sentences of different ethnic dialects of Nepal`.

- machine-made paper;
- English;
- rough tabular vocabulary;
- many corrections.

Source:
https://hodgson.socanth.cam.ac.uk/chunkhtml/vol022.html

On current descriptions, this is not semantically equivalent to a Khasa `thar` list.

Possible explanations:

- family-name material is embedded inside the broad vocabulary object but omitted from summary;
- the folio/item cross-reference is imprecise;
- catalogue relation drift;
- another historical classificatory linkage not obvious from title.

Therefore code:

`CATALOGUE_CROSS_REFERENCE_SEMANTIC_MISMATCH`.

Do not silently delete it, but do not count it as a direct micro-stemma edge either.

Mechanism:

`CATALOGUE_RELATION_REQUIRES_SEMANTIC_CONTROL`.

## 9. Publication is a convergence state, not yet a known endpoint for every manuscript

Multiple nodes point to Hodgson's 1833 publication, `Origin and classification of the military tribes of Nepal`, pp.217–224.

The manuscript evidence strongly suggests a pre-publication English family around vol.7/20, vol.6/1 and vol.9/10.

But the archive does not yet justify:

`every local/Farsi/Urdu node -> directly generated 1833 print`.

The safer model is:

`multiple manuscript classification states`
↔ `published classificatory formulation`.

The exact printer-copy and translation dependencies remain to be reconstructed.

Mechanism:

`PRINT_AS_CONVERGENCE_STATE_WITHOUT_FORCED_STEMMA`.

`vol.101/8` — machine-paper Khasa thar list

### Print

The relation graph is denser than the material graph.

That distinction is analytically useful.

Same content family does not require same material state.

## 11. Connection back to 1864 Item 12

The 1864 transfer's Item 12 explicitly names:

`classification of the people (thar)`

among its English volumes, together with Persian and Khas papers on the same subjects.

Wave 43 connected modern `vol.6` to Item 12 through exact old item number + content overlap.

Wave 44 now shows that the `thar` branch named in that historical custody unit still survives as a multilingual, cross-support relation network.

This strengthens the item-12 hypothesis in a more specific way than generic subject matching.

The relevant operation is literally the one named by the 1864 manifest:

`thar / classification of people`.

Modern states distribute that operation across:

`6 + 7 + 9 + 51 + 56 + 57 + 58 + 96 + 101 + 103`.

Not every node is claimed to have belonged physically to Item 12. The network is ranked by relation evidence.

## 12. Article consequence

The classification micro-stemma gives the paper article a controlled middle-scale scene between single-pair comparison and full-archive statistics.

It shows:

1. one historical classificatory operation;
2. multiple language states;
3. multiple paper regimes;
4. multiple rough/fair/composite version states;
5. a print convergence state;
6. later multilingual co-custody evidence in the 1864 manifest;
7. modern dispersal across volume classes.

The strongest formulation is:

**The material history of classification did not proceed from local handmade source to English machine-paper result in one direction. The same `thar` problem existed as Nepali handmade lists, English machine-paper working and fair copies, Farsi machine-paper lists, Urdu handmade lists and further Farsi handmade composites. Paper support belonged to particular documentary states inside a multilingual classificatory ecology; it was not an index of the language or cultural origin of the knowledge.**

## Source-control rules

- Do not convert cross-reference into copy direction without textual comparison.
- Do not use common publication citation as proof of common immediate source.
- Do not classify `vol.57/2` as direct `thar` copy; it is broader context.
- Preserve the `vol.22/9` mismatch as an anomaly.
- Farsi and Urdu remain distinct catalogue language labels.
- Machine-made paper remains a formation class, not secure national origin.

## Bottom line

The `thar` branch turns the probable 1864 Item-12 family into a material relation network that can be tested line by line. English and Farsi versions share machine support; Nepali and Urdu versions share handmade support; Farsi itself also crosses both support regimes. One classificatory operation therefore repeatedly exceeds the simple binaries of language, paper origin and writer identity. The paper article can now show not merely that documents changed support, but that a single classificatory problem persisted as a multilingual ecology of materially differentiated documentary states.
