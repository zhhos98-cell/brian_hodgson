## Finding 22 — Darjeeling closure was Campbell's economic decision under a specific equipment/labour regime, not a simple paper failure

Evidence status: **STRONG SECONDARY ARCHIVAL EXTRACTION WITH EXACT PRIMARY POINTER.**

Ali states that Campbell's experimental paper factory at Darjeeling continued to work until **16 April 1842**, when Campbell closed it because he found it uneconomical to run **unless the existing local/rude machinery were replaced by better and more expensive factory gear**.

Ali's footnote gives the exact archival basis:

- Campbell to G. A. Bushby, **21 April 1842**;
- `Political O. C., 11 May 1842, no. 70`.

This materially revises the article's ending.

Do not write:

`Darjeeling experiment failed because Nepal paper could not be made economically.`

The source supports a narrower formulation:

`Campbell judged the experimental establishment uneconomical under the existing combination of production gear, labour costs and output.`

The paper-making operation itself had already produced paper in quantity.

## Finding 23 — Military Board / Stationery Department attempted to revive the Darjeeling manufacture after closure

Evidence status: **STRONG SECONDARY ARCHIVAL EXTRACTION; exact underlying record relation beyond No.70 still to be checked.**

Immediately after reporting the 16 April closure, Ali says the **Military Board (Stationery Department)** tried to have paper manufacture renewed by Campbell at Darjeeling.

Campbell again objected on economic grounds. Ali preserves his estimate that, at the then high rate of ordinary labour wages at Darjeeling, Daphne paper probably could not be made below approximately:

- **Rs 10–15 per ream**;
- sheet size approximately **2 ft × 18 in**;
- each sheet calculated to make **four letter envelopes**.

Campbell nevertheless acknowledged that renewing paper-making at Darjeeling might be desirable for various reasons; his objection was that he doubted it would then be profitable.

### Consequence

The 1842 sequence is not:

`experiment -> technical failure -> abandonment`.

It is closer to:

`successful local production / experimental improvement`
-> `Campbell closes the establishment on economic/equipment grounds`
-> `Stationery Department seeks revival`
-> `Campbell resists because Darjeeling labour + production costs remain high`.

This is a much stronger first-order historical problem than an abstract affordance narrative.

### Actor implication

The identities and wages of labour now matter even more. The economic ending explicitly turns on **ordinary labour wages at Darjeeling**. Recovering which Nepalese makers remained, which work was performed by local labour, and what the two Lepcha trainees actually did could alter the cost reconstruction.

## Finding 24 — Political O.C. No.70 contains more than a closure note: it is a candidate dossier for apparatus, labour and cost

Evidence status: **HIGH-VALUE PRIMARY RETRIEVAL TARGET.**

Ali uses `Political O.C., 11 May 1842, no. 70` not merely to date the project. In the surrounding discussion it supports Campbell's report on:

- wire-gauze sieves supplied by the Military Board;
- smoother paper produced through those sieves;
- clarification of alkaline/potash solution through washed sand to obtain lighter-coloured paper;
- the economic status of the Darjeeling factory;
- the 16 April closure;
- the 21 April Campbell -> Bushby explanation;
- the production-cost problem.

This makes No.70 a potential **article-level primary dossier**, not an archival footnote.

Questions for direct inspection:

1. Does it enumerate equipment supplied by the Military Board?
2. Does it itemize labour/wages beyond the five papermakers' initial pay?
3. Does it say which Nepalese makers remained at Darjeeling by April 1842?
4. Does it identify the Lepcha trainees or local workmen?
5. Does it distinguish artisan wages from ordinary labour wages?
6. Does it give output/ream calculations underlying Rs 10–15 per ream?
7. Does it state what `better and more expensive factory gear` Campbell had in mind?
8. Does the Military Board revival proposal envisage hand production, improved hand gear, or mechanisation?

These questions now justify a narrowly targeted NAI/India Office retrieval.

## Finding 25 — article-level ending must split `manufacture` from `paper afterlife`

The emerging chronology is now:

### Darjeeling establishment

`1841 five Nepalese makers + Campbell workshop`
-> `production, process calibration, local training`
-> `chemical / equipment improvement attempts`
-> `Campbell closes experimental factory 16 Apr 1842 on economic/equipment grounds`
-> `Military Board Stationery Department attempts revival`
-> `Campbell doubts profitability at prevailing Darjeeling labour costs`.

### Nepal/Daphne paper as material practice

Meanwhile / afterwards:

- Serampore industrial trials continue;
- Nepal-made paper remains actively procured;
- Hooker in 1849 demands bundles/loads for botanical fieldwork;
- Dhankuta remains a contemporary paper-manufacturing centre;
- `paper made in Nepal and at Darjeeling` appears in the 1851 Great Exhibition catalogue.

Thus:

**closure of the relocated experimental manufactory != disappearance or technical obsolescence of Nepal paper.**

This is now a case-level historical result rather than a theoretical assertion.

## Source interpretation guardrail

Ali's own 1944 conclusion attributes failure partly to Government lukewarmness and argues for rational/scientific organisation of the industry. That is Ali's wartime-nationalist interpretation and should **not** be imported as the nineteenth-century explanation.

Use Ali for:
- archival quotations;
- dates;
- Political O.C. numbers;
- historically attributed statements;
- retrieval guidance.

Keep his 1944 policy/nationalist judgment separate from Campbell's 1842 reasoning and from our analysis.
