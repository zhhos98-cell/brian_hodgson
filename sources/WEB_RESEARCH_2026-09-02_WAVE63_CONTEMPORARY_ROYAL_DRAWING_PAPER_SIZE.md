# Web research 2026-09-02 — Wave 63
## Contemporary trade evidence for Royal 19 × 24 drawing paper strengthens the RAS half-sheet reconstruction

Date: 2026-09-02

Status: paper-history mainline following Wave 62. This wave tests whether the `Royal 19 × 24 in` parent-sheet category used in the RAS dimensional reconstruction was actually a live drawing-paper trade size in the early 1840s, rather than a later standard retrospectively imposed on Hodgson's papers.

## Executive result

The answer is yes at the level of the contemporary drawing-paper trade.

A Library of Congress digitized newspaper page from 1843 carries an advertisement headed `WHATMAN'S DRAWING PAPER`. The stationer states that a large supply had been received direct from the manufacturer and lists the available paper by named commercial sizes. The OCR directly preserves:

- Demy;
- Medium;
- **Royal — 19 × 24 inches**;
- Super Royal;
- Imperial;
- Elephant;
- Columbier;
- Double Elephant;
- Antiquarian.

Relevant OCR lines: approximately 449–464.

The same size nomenclature is independently consistent with modern historical-paper references, including the British Association of Paper Historians table.

A National Portrait Gallery directory of British artists' suppliers also records a c.1840 Norwich trade sheet advertising Whatman's drawing paper among artists' materials, showing that this product category was actively marketed in Britain in precisely the period of the Hodgson/Raj Man Singh architectural programme.

The material conclusion is narrower than maker identification but stronger than Wave 62's modern-reference comparison:

**Royal 19 × 24 inches was a commercially current drawing-paper size in the early 1840s.**

Therefore the RAS c.`30.10 × 48.54 cm` working format can be compared historically, not merely metrically, with a contemporary half-Royal unit:

`19 × 24 in Royal parent`
-> `12 × 19 in half sheet`
-> `30.48 × 48.26 cm theoretical half`

versus

`RAS numbered long-family mean = 30.10 × 48.54 cm`.

Current state:

`HALF_ROYAL_DRAWING_SHEET_CANDIDATE_VERY_HIGH_BY_DIMENSION_AND_PERIOD_TRADE_COMPATIBILITY`.

This still does **not** identify the RAS paper as Whatman.

Core mechanisms:

- `COMMERCIAL_PAPER_SIZE_IS_A_HISTORICAL_MATERIAL_CATEGORY`
- `WORKING_FORMAT_CAN_BE_RECONSTRUCTED_FROM_PERIOD_TRADE_SIZE`
- `PARENT_SHEET_HYPOTHESIS_GAINS_CONTEMPORARY_TRADE_SUPPORT`
- `SIZE_CLASS_IDENTITY_DOES_NOT_EQUAL_MAKER_IDENTITY`.

## 1. The 1843 advertisement matters because it is not a later technical handbook

Wave 60–62 initially used the British Association of Paper Historians reference table to test named English drawing-paper sizes against catalogue dimensions.

That was legitimate as a historical reference but left a chronological question:

**was Royal 19 × 24 actually sold as drawing paper in the early 1840s?**

The 1843 advertisement directly answers that question.

The newspaper OCR describes Whatman's drawing paper as supplied in a ladder of named sizes and directly gives Royal as `19 × 24` inches.

Thus `Royal` was not merely a book-paper term or a later engineers' convention in this context. It was a commercial size class attached specifically to drawing paper at the moment Raj Man Singh's architectural series was being produced.

Mechanism:

`TRADE_NOMENCLATURE_PREEXISTS_RESEARCH_FORMAT`.

---

## 2. The transatlantic advertisement is evidence for the product system, not the Hodgson supply route

The LOC advertisement is from an American retail/import context and states that the drawing paper was received direct from the manufacturer.

It therefore demonstrates:

- Whatman's drawing paper was commercially exported;
- named standard sheet sizes were part of the product specification;
- Royal 19 × 24 was current by 1843.

It does not demonstrate:

- that Hodgson bought from this stationer;
- that RAS 022 paper was Whatman;
- that the paper travelled America -> India;
- that Royal sheets were cut in Kathmandu rather than earlier in the supply chain.

The evidential role is:

`CONTEMPORARY_PRODUCT_AND_SIZE_SYSTEM`.

Not:

`HODGSON_PROCUREMENT_ROUTE`.

---

## 3. British supplier evidence keeps the trade context geographically relevant

The National Portrait Gallery's British artists' suppliers directory records a c.1840 trade sheet produced by Freeman of Norwich. Among the advertised stock were:

- Whatman's drawing paper;
- Turnbull's drawing boards;
- watercolours, crayons, brushes and other artists' materials.

The same directory records Rowney's stocking Whatman's drawing papers by 1846.

This establishes that Whatman's drawing paper belonged to the ordinary British artists' materials market immediately around the Hodgson/Raj Man Singh period.

The combined trade context is therefore:

`British manufacture / artists' supply`
+
`export commerce`
+
`named commercial sheet-size ladder`.

It becomes materially plausible that British drawing stock reaching South Asia retained these named size regimes even though the specific RAS maker remains unknown.

---

## 4. The half-Royal reconstruction becomes historically stronger

Wave 62 recovered 47 numbered Raj Man Singh drawing dimensions:

- 43 long-format sheets;
- mean `30.10 × 48.54 cm`;
- sd `0.21 × 0.25 cm`;
- 23 directly dated-watermark long sheets and 20 catalogue-watermark-not-stated long sheets are dimensionally indistinguishable.

Contemporary trade Royal:

`19 × 24 in = 48.26 × 60.96 cm`.

Simple halving along the 24-inch dimension:

`12 × 19 in = 30.48 × 48.26 cm`.

The result differs from the observed RAS mean by only millimetres.

This now supports a more precise operational hypothesis:

`commercial Royal drawing-paper parent`
-> `division into half sheets`
-> `c.30 × 48.5 cm numbered architectural working format`.

The unresolved question moves from **whether Royal was a period-appropriate category** to **whether physical sheet evidence confirms this particular cutting history**.

That is a substantial narrowing of the research problem.

---

## 5. Half-sheet conversion is materially plausible but its location remains unknown

Even if transmitted-light evidence confirms a Royal parent, several operations remain possible:

### A. paper arrived in Kathmandu as whole Royal sheets

`Royal whole sheet -> local cutting -> working unit`.

### B. paper was cut at Calcutta or another supply node

`Royal whole sheet -> metropolitan/Calcutta stationery conversion -> half sheets -> transport to Kathmandu`.

### C. paper was commercially supplied in half-sheet units

`manufacturer/stationer conversion -> working-sized stock -> Kathmandu use`.

### D. present dimensions partly reflect later trimming

`larger historical working support -> RAS mounting/conservation trim -> present c.30 × 48.5 object`.

Dimensions alone cannot locate the cut in the supply chain.

Physical indicators needed:

- cut versus deckle edges;
- relative watermark position;
- countermark position;
- chain-line orientation;
- paired complementary halves;
- mount overlap/trimming evidence.

Mechanism:

`CUT_LOCATION_IS_A_SUPPLY_CHAIN_VARIABLE`.

---

## 6. Contemporary size evidence also strengthens the short-format test

The same 1843 advertisement lists Demy, Medium and Royal as distinct drawing-paper sizes in one commercial range.

Therefore Wave 62's secondary short-family hypothesis is historically plausible as a second commercial size regime rather than a purely mathematical coincidence.

Recovered short numbered objects:

- 022.012 — 27.1 × 45.4 cm;
- 022.014 — 27 × 43.8 cm;
- 022.018 — 27.8 × 44 cm.

Modern historical-size reference gives Medium around `17.5 × 22.5 in`, with theoretical half about `28.58 × 44.45 cm`.

Because the short dimensions require more trimming than the long Royal family, retain:

`TRIMMED_HALF_MEDIUM_CANDIDATE`.

The contemporary advertisement only strengthens the historical availability of a Medium drawing-paper class; it does not prove parentage for these three sheets.

---

## 7. Maker identification must remain separate

This is the crucial source-control point.

The LOC evidence is specifically Whatman's drawing paper.

The RAS online records consulted so far generally state only a **watermark date** for the architectural sheets, not a maker name.

Therefore:

`ROYAL_SIZE_COMPATIBILITY = STRONG`

while

`WHATMAN_IDENTITY_OF_RAS_LONG_FAMILY = UNPROVEN`.

The next transmitted-light examination must record the actual lettering/device and countermark before any maker is assigned.

Mechanisms:

- `SIZE_CLASS_IDENTITY_NOT_MAKER_IDENTITY`
- `DATE_ONLY_WATERMARK_CANNOT_SUPPORT_MAKER_ATTRIBUTION`.

## 8. Article consequence

Wave 63 allows the article to move from an abstract statement that researchers used standardized paper sizes to a historically specific material proposition:

**The c.30 × 48.5 cm support used for most of Raj Man Singh's numbered architectural drawings reproduces, to within millimetres, one half of a Royal drawing-paper sheet whose 19 × 24 inch size was commercially current in 1843. The paper format of the research programme therefore appears to have been made through a recognizable operation within the contemporary drawing-paper trade system: a named parent size was converted into a repeated task-sized support.**

Keep `appears` / `candidate` until physical sheet topology confirms the cut.

## Source-control rules

1. Use the 1843 advertisement to establish period commercial size terminology, not Hodgson's supplier.
2. Do not identify the RAS sheets as Whatman from size alone.
3. Do not identify the place of cutting from size alone.
4. Preserve the distinction between whole commercial sheet, working half sheet and later mounted object.
5. The screenshot endpoint for the LOC PDF was access-blocked in the present tool environment; the LOC OCR text was directly readable and supplies the size list.
6. Physical watermark morphology and edge state remain decisive.

## Bottom line

The half-Royal model now has contemporary trade support. A commercial advertisement from 1843 lists Whatman's drawing paper in named sizes including Royal 19 × 24 inches, while British supplier evidence shows Whatman's drawing paper actively marketed around 1840. This does not make Hodgson's architectural sheets Whatman. It does show that the parent-size category required by the RAS dimensional reconstruction was commercially real at exactly the relevant moment. The remaining proof is physical: watermark topology and sheet edges must show whether the dominant c.30 × 48.5 cm working format was actually produced by halving Royal drawing-paper stock.
