# Public-web research wave 4 — copy criticism, shipment failure, Tibetan canon provenance, and paper affordances

Date: 2026-08-31

Scope: public web only. No new private/archive scans. This wave continues the evidence-led comparison between Buddhist manuscripts, zoological specimens, and paper/logistical infrastructures.

## 1. Burnouf: manuscript verification required multiple copies, not a single “authentic” witness

A major source is Guillaume Ducœur's 2022 edition/study of Eugène Burnouf's unfinished translation of the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā*, which quotes the Hodgson–Burnouf correspondence and describes the physical witnesses.

Burnouf explicitly wrote to Hodgson in October 1837 that, in order to remedy defects in manuscripts, it was absolutely necessary to have **several copies of the same work**. The practical logic is textual comparison rather than reliance on one supposedly authoritative original.

Two witnesses of the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* are described:

- Burnouf's own manuscript: 302 folios, Nepali paper, 33.5 × 13 cm, nine lines per folio.
- Société Asiatique manuscript: 248 folios, glazed yellow Nepali paper, 35.5 × 10 cm, seven lines per folio.

In a letter of 29 Nov. 1839 Burnouf judged the Société manuscript **generally better because it was older**, but also **very incomplete**: he had found lacunae of 10–15 pages at a time. Crucially, the copyist had copied continuously without marking the interruption, apparently failing to notice that leaves were missing from the exemplar.

This gives a powerful multi-variable verification problem:

**age ≠ completeness ≠ scribal accuracy ≠ textual authority.**

A materially older witness could be preferred in one respect while being much worse for reconstructing continuous text. “Authenticity” should therefore be analysed as a comparative practice rather than a binary property.

Ducœur also notes that Burnouf requested Hodgson to find and have copied a commentary (*vyākhyā*) because difficult technical vocabulary could not be reliably resolved from the available witnesses alone. The supply chain therefore extended beyond collecting texts to **commissioning auxiliary interpretive apparatus**.

## 2. Burnouf's own published criticism makes manuscript defect an epistemic variable

In *Introduction à l'histoire du bouddhisme indien*, Burnouf describes the prose *Karaṇḍavyūha* manuscript as so incorrect that producing an exact extract from it would have been harder than translating the verse version in full.

This is useful because it shows “incorrect manuscript” was not merely later cataloguer language: textual defect directly constrained what kind of scholarly operation Burnouf could perform.

## 3. Hodgson–Paris circulation can be decomposed into donations, commissioned copies, crates, receipt, and safer routing

RAS archival descriptions and later collection histories now allow the Paris movement to be split into distinct operations rather than treated as one generic donation.

Evidence includes:

- Burnouf thanking Hodgson in May 1836 for manuscripts procured for the Société Asiatique.
- A set of Sanskrit Buddhist manuscripts copied in Nepal specifically for the Société Asiatique.
- Burnouf's 15 July 1837 acknowledgement of the **safe arrival of three cases** of manuscripts.
- Julius von Mohl's 29 July 1837 report that **three crates** sent by Hodgson had arrived.
- Burnouf acknowledging copies including a notably fine *Rakṣābhagavatī*.
- Burnouf requesting further copies for his own use.
- Burnouf giving Hodgson a **safer address** for future manuscript shipments.
- The Société's Gold Medal as a reputational/institutional response to successful acquisition and delivery.

Waterhouse's collection survey distinguishes at least:
- 24 works presented earlier to the Société Asiatique;
- c.64 copies made in Nepal for the Société and presented in 1837;
- a separate Burnouf personal collection (c.59 manuscripts).

This suggests a recipient-sensitive copy industry rather than a one-directional “discovery and donation” story.

## 4. Zoological circulation contains a documented shipment failure

RAS committee minutes preserved in the ZSL Hodgson archive report that by Nov. 1837 Hodgson had:

- despatched 26 sheets of mammals and birds;
- intended to continue sending sheets until the series was complete;
- despatched **eight boxes** in January by Captain Robinson, with instructions that they be deposited at the RAS if earlier stores had been removed from the ZSL.

The minute then records that **none of these articles had been received**.

This is a particularly strong counterpoint to the Paris manuscript letters that explicitly certify safe arrival. The material history can therefore compare:

**dispatch → non-arrival / uncertainty → pursuit through institutional custody**

with

**dispatch → receipt → acknowledgement → scholarly use**.

## 5. Numbered catalogues were recovery devices when objects moved or disappeared

A Hodgson/ZSL description states that numbered catalogues could provide a “ready clue” to the drawings that ought to be forthcoming after materials had fallen into various hands.

This means catalogue numbers did not merely describe a stable collection; they were an active **custody and recovery technology** for reconstructing dispersed shipments.

## 6. Sorting and comparison at the British Museum repaired corrupted transport metadata

J. E. Gray wrote Hodgson that:

- copied names were badly wrong because of the copyist;
- some bird numbers appeared to have been duplicated across very different specimens;
- it was impossible to describe new species until the whole collection had been **sorted**, so new and old could be compared;
- some skeletons were still unpacked;
- specimens were then distributed in large series to the British Museum, India House, Leiden, Paris, Berlin, Frankfurt and other institutions.

This is a clear post-arrival verification regime:

**unpack → sort → compare → correct names/numbers → decide novelty → redistribute duplicates.**

The specimen itself, its label/number, and the associated drawing/list formed a compound identification system that could be broken in transit or copying and repaired only by comparison.

## 7. Hodgson's zoological collections were prepared as multiple institution-specific distributions

A 5 Feb. 1845 letter to Leiden records a shipment/distribution of specimens with an attached catalogue; the same template was adapted for Paris, Berlin, Frankfurt, Edinburgh, Dublin, Newcastle, Canterbury, Manchester, Derby and Strickland. Hodgson explicitly distinguished the transmitted subsets from the complete series through marks in the catalogue.

The material object was therefore not just “the Hodgson collection.” It was repeatedly partitioned into institution-specific subsets while retaining a relational catalogue to the larger series.

## 8. Hooker supplies a field-science paper chain that crosses drawing, labelling, drying, protection, and transport

Hooker asked Hodgson to send two kinds of folio white paper:

- one used for **drawings**;
- the other used for **tickets to specimens**.

He instructed that these should be placed between quires of Nepal or brown paper used for **drying plants**, tied tightly, so that the packet would arrive safely.

Another Hooker letter asks Campbell/Hodgson to transmit a **bundle of Nepal paper**.

Thus paper performs at least five field functions within one correspondence network:

1. image-making;
2. specimen identification;
3. plant drying;
4. protective interleaving / stiffening;
5. transport packaging.

## 9. Nepali paper should be analysed through affordances, not symbolic coding

A secondary history of Nepalese paper quotes Hodgson's 8 Dec. 1831 transmission of Nepal-paper samples to H. T. Prinsep, along with an account of the manufacturing method. It states that Hodgson recommended Nepal paper for office records in place of common Hindustan paper.

A historical study of Nepalese paper also gives two contemporary quality/price grades and notes Hodgson/Campbell evidence for a wide trade network.

Taken with Hooker's use of Nepal paper in field botany and the manuscript evidence, the stronger hypothesis is not:

`Nepali paper = indigenous/religious`.

Rather, its relevant properties may include **strength, durability, foldability, surface, absorbency/drying behaviour, resistance to biological damage, availability, price, and suitability for bundling or wrapping**. These affordances could be mobilised differently across administrative, textual and natural-historical work.

## 10. Tibetan canon provenance is currently contradictory and must be reconstructed copy by copy

The public record does **not** yet support a single clean story of Hodgson's Kanjur/Tanjur acquisition.

Current BL catalogue for `Mss Tib X-Y` states:
- Narthang Tanjur;
- produced by order of the 7th Panchen Lama in the 1830s;
- presented to Hodgson in 1838;
- then donated by Hodgson to the India Office Library in 1838.

Source:
https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-004282613

Hodgson's later printed note, however, says he eventually **procured from Lhasa** the complete Kanjur and Tanjur in 327 large volumes; that their catalogue had been obtained earlier; and elsewhere that complete sets were presented to the East India Company and procured for the Asiatic Society.

Hunter's 1881 catalogue gives 334 volumes and describes the edition as procured in 1838 from the “Grand Lama of Tibet,” while later biographical summaries sometimes speak of **two complete copies**, one to Fort William and one to the East India Company.

Sources:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Nepal/1881%20Catalogue%20of%20Sanskrit%20Manuscripts%20collected%20in%20Nepal%20by%20Brian%20Houghton%20Hodgson%20by%20Hunter%20s.pdf

https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1901_supplement/Hodgson%2C_Brian_Houghton

Analytic rule: do not yet write “the Dalai/Panchen Lama gave Hodgson the canon” as a settled fact. Separate:
- Kanjur vs Tanjur;
- Narthang vs other editions;
- India Office set vs Asiatic Society/Fort William set;
- gift vs commissioned/procured set;
- date and route of each copy.

The contradictions themselves may expose how later institutional catalogues compressed multi-stage acquisition histories.

## 11. Paris manuscript criticism supplies a model for “distributed witnesses”

Later philological work continues to identify Hodgson/Burnouf manuscripts as separate witnesses with distinct provenance, foliation, hands, dates and copying errors.

Examples:

- BnF *Mahāvastu* (Burnouf 91 / Hodgson donation): paper, 413 folios, Nepalese script, dated 1800, later bound in three volumes. The 1854 Burnouf sale catalogue records it as sent by Hodgson in May 1841.
- A Nepalese *Ācāryakriyāsamuccaya* from the Hodgson-Burnouf collection contains copyist confusions, duplicated phrases and punctuation errors; later editors establish difficult passages by comparison with another Devanagari witness.
- A modern study of the *Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā* explicitly calls one BnF witness a modern copy made at Hodgson's instance and tracks inherited and newly introduced copying errors.

Sources:
https://iriab.soka.ac.jp/content/pdf/aririab/Vol.%202%20%281999%29%20%5Brev.4Aug2010%5D.pdf

https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/59642.pdf

https://thuvienphatgiao.com/buddhistbook/detail/book-2041/-The-Pancavimsatisahasrika-Prajnaparamita.html

This strengthens a general claim: Hodgson's “collection” often survives not as a single archive but as a **distributed stemma of material witnesses** created through copying, gifting, institutional transfer and rebinding.

## 12. New cross-domain formulation

The strongest comparison now looks like this:

### Manuscript witness
`support → foliation → copyist/hand → title/wrapper → colophon → completeness → crate → receipt → collation with other witnesses → catalogue identity`

### Zoological specimen
`body/skin/bone → preparator → ticket/number → drawing → box → receipt/non-receipt → unpacking → sorting → comparison → taxonomic identity → redistribution`

The homologous operation is not “both use paper.” It is:

> **identity has to be materially maintained across distance, and when that maintenance fails, downstream scholars reconstruct identity by comparing partially independent traces.**

This is currently a stronger history-of-science intervention than a simple material-culture account.
