# Web/archive research 2026-09-02 — Wave 71
## Landon 1928 moves the ten-sheet `dhep` hierarchy back before the late-twentieth-century paper-industry literature

Date: 2026-09-02

Status: active paper-history mainline. This wave adds a substantially earlier printed witness for the `tau -> dhep -> kori` hierarchy while preserving item-level source uncertainty.

## Executive result

Perceval Landon's *Nepal*, vol. II (London: Constable, 1928), Appendix VI `Weights and Measures`, printed p.256, directly prints a paper-count hierarchy:

`10 Tao = 1 Dhep`

`20 Dheps = 1 Kori`.

A footnote attached to the paper section defines a `tao` as **a sheet of any size**.

This is the earliest directly recovered printed witness in the current run for the same ten-sheet `dhep` hierarchy later recorded by Bhupahari Paudel in 1987–88 and stabilized in modern Nepali lexicography.

It changes the temporal evidence state from:

`TEN_SHEET_DHEP = LATE_TWENTIETH_CENTURY_TRADITIONAL_DESCRIPTION + MODERN_LEXICOGRAPHY`

to:

`TEN_SHEET_DHEP = DIRECTLY_PRINTED_BY_1928 + LATER_TRADITIONAL_DESCRIPTION + MODERN_LEXICOGRAPHY`.

This does **not** prove that Landon's table derives from Hodgson, nor that the ten-sheet convention was universal in nineteenth-century Nepal.

Current state:

`LANDON_1928_PAPER_METROLOGY = DIRECT_PRINTED_COMPILATION / CONTEMPORARY_NEPAL_CONTEXT / ITEM_LEVEL_SOURCE_UNRESOLVED`.

Mechanisms:

- `PAPER_METROLOGY_ENTERED_GENERAL_STATE_DESCRIPTIVE_KNOWLEDGE`
- `TEN_SHEET_DHEP_HAS_PRE_1930_PRINTED_ATTESTATION`
- `METROLOGY_TABLE_CAN_PRESERVE_LOCAL_PAPER_VOCABULARY`
- `COMPILATION_SOURCE_PROVENANCE_REMAINS_DISTINCT_FROM_PRINTED_ATTESTATION`.

## 2. Landon treats paper counting as metrology, not as specialist craft vocabulary

The position of the paper table matters.

Appendix VI is headed:

`WEIGHTS AND MEASURES`.

It includes:

- old Nepali weights;
- measures of length;
- measures for cloth;
- land-measure systems;
- time measures;
- grain measures;
- finally, paper counting.

Thus `tao`, `dhep`, and `kori` are presented as part of the country's general practical metrology.

This gives the paper unit a different evidential status from a glossary entry.

The unit hierarchy was sufficiently institutionalized or conventionalized to be printed alongside land, grain, weight and time measures in a general description of Nepal.

Mechanisms:

- `PAPER_COUNTING_IS_PART_OF_GENERAL_METROLOGY`
- `PAPER_BUNDLE_UNIT_IS_AN_ACCOUNTABLE_SOCIAL_MEASURE`
- `SHEET_OF_ANY_SIZE_SEPARATES_COUNT_FROM_DIMENSION`.

The last point is particularly important for the paper-history argument. Landon's footnote means that `tao` is a **count unit independent of sheet dimensions**. This prevents any silent assumption that one `tao` implies a standardized paper size.

Correct separation:

`sheet count != sheet format`.

## 3. Appendix VI itself warns against universalizing numerical systems

Landon's surrounding discussion makes the paper table more useful, not less.

In the same Appendix VI, he says that different systems are in force in Nepal. He notes that Tarai weights had differed according to locality and that this variation caused enough inconvenience for a common standard to be introduced. He also distinguishes hill grain measurement from Tarai sale by weight.

Thus the source that prints a ten-sheet `dhep` also explicitly recognizes **regional variation and metrological standardization elsewhere in Nepal**.

This is consistent with Wave 70's direct western-Nepal evidence for a twenty-sheet `dhep` rather than contradictory to it.

The correct historical model is:

`printed/general convention`
coexisting with
`local/workshop variants`.

Mechanisms:

- `GENERAL_METROLOGY_CAN_COEXIST_WITH_LOCAL_VARIANTS`
- `STATE_STANDARDIZATION_RESPONDS_TO_METROLOGICAL_PLURALITY`
- `PRINTED_EQUIVALENCE_IS_NOT_PROOF_OF_UNIVERSAL_PRACTICE`.

## 4. Landon's source environment is unusually rich, but the paper table has no item-level citation

Landon's preface provides strong context for how the book was produced.

He explicitly acknowledges earlier scholarship including Brian Houghton Hodgson, Sylvain Lévi, Wright, Oldfield, Hunter, Markham, the Survey Department, Vansittart and others.

More importantly, Landon states that Maharaja Chandra Shamsher made the writing of the book possible by:

- providing material for modern Nepalese history;
- giving access to Nepalese archives;
- submitting original documents;
- answering requests for information;
- sending men who had travelled through otherwise unknown districts and instructing them to explain their knowledge fully.

He presents the work as drawing not only on earlier European publication but also on information and documents supplied by the contemporary Nepalese state.

This makes several possible provenance paths for Appendix VI live:

1. a contemporary Nepalese official/administrative metrology table supplied under Chandra Shamsher;
2. information elicited from Nepalese informants or officials;
3. an earlier British/Residency source recompiled by Landon;
4. a combination of current and earlier sources.

The paper lines themselves carry no visible item-level citation in the recovered text.

Therefore:

`LANDON_GENERAL_SOURCE_ENVIRONMENT = STRONGLY_DOCUMENTED`

while

`LANDON_PAPER_TABLE_IMMEDIATE_SOURCE = UNRESOLVED`.

Do not infer a Hodgson source edge solely because Landon acknowledges Hodgson elsewhere in the preface.

Mechanisms:

- `BOOK_LEVEL_PROVENANCE_DOES_NOT_IDENTIFY_ITEM_LEVEL_SOURCE`
- `OFFICIAL_ARCHIVE_ACCESS_CAN_FEED_METROLOGICAL_COMPILATION`
- `COMPILATION_CAN_MIX_LEGACY_AND_CONTEMPORARY_INFORMATION`.

## 5. Why the 1928 date materially narrows the Hodgson f.117 problem

Hodgson/Wallich bill:

`MSS EUR HODGSON/12 f.117 — 1843`.

Cambridge catalogue:

- local paper unit romanized `ḍhyāp`;
- gloss: ten sheets in one bundle.

Wave 69 identified this as a very high lexical candidate for Nepali `ढेप / dhep`.

Wave 70 showed that `dhep` package depth varies in later observed practice and must not be treated as a universal constant.

Wave 71 adds a chronological bridge:

`1843 Cambridge catalogue reports ten-sheet ḍhyāp`

-> `1928 Landon directly prints 10 tao = 1 dhep`

-> `1987–88 Paudel directly prints 10 tau = 1 dhep; 20 dhep = 1 kori`

-> `modern lexicography defines dhep as ten tau of Nepali paper`.

The gap is still substantial, and Landon's table source is unresolved, but the ten-sheet structure is no longer known only from late-twentieth-century industry/lexical sources.

Current inference:

`F117 TEN-SHEET GLOSS HAS STRONG STRUCTURAL CONTINUITY WITH A DIRECT 1928 NEPAL METROLOGY TABLE`.

Do not upgrade to:

`CONTINUOUSLY PROVEN 1843-1928 PRACTICE`.

Mechanism:

`TEMPORAL_ALIGNMENT_IS_NOT_CONTINUOUS_TRANSMISSION_PROOF`.

## 6. The Landon footnote sharpens the format/metrology distinction

Waves 60–63 reconstructed possible commercial parent-sheet formats in Hodgson-associated drawing papers, especially the c.30 × 48.5 cm half-Royal candidate.

Landon now provides an explicit local counting rule:

`tao = one sheet of any size`.

This means the paper history must keep two independent measurement systems:

### count metrology

`tau / dhep / kori`

and

### dimensional/format metrology

`sheet dimensions / parent-size class / cutting relation`.

A `dhep` groups a number of sheets. It does not by itself define the dimensions of those sheets.

Thus paper quantity can remain stable while paper format changes, and paper format can remain stable across stocks counted in the same or different bundle systems.

Mechanisms:

- `COUNT_METROLOGY_DISTINCT_FROM_DIMENSIONAL_METROLOGY`
- `PACKAGE_DEPTH_DOES_NOT_DEFINE_SHEET_FORMAT`
- `PAPER_PROVENANCE_REQUIRES_BOTH_COUNT_AND_DIMENSION_FIELDS`.

This is a useful correction to any dataset that would encode one local bundle merely as `N sheets` without retaining sheet dimensions and production context.

## Source-control rules

1. Cite the Landon paper hierarchy to vol. II, Appendix VI, printed p.256.
2. Keep Appendix VI distinct from Appendix VII; the paper lines immediately precede the Census Returns heading.
3. Record Landon's footnote that a tao is a sheet of any size.
4. Treat the Landon table as direct printed evidence for the ten-sheet convention by 1928.
5. Do not claim its immediate source was Hodgson, Chandra Shamsher's archives, or a specific official table without an item-level citation.
6. Use Landon's preface only to characterize the book-level source ecology.
7. Keep `count metrology` separate from `sheet dimensions/format`.
8. Keep the twenty-sheet western-Nepal field observation live as a locality-specific variant.
9. Do not interpret Landon's printed convention as proof that all Nepalese production used ten-sheet bundles.
10. Keep f.117 original account layout and the provenance of Cambridge's `ḍhyāp`/ten-sheet gloss unresolved.

## Bottom line

Landon's 1928 *Nepal* materially shortens the history of the ten-sheet `dhep` convention. Appendix VI prints `10 Tao = 1 Dhep` and `20 Dheps = 1 Kori`, and explicitly defines a tao as a sheet regardless of size. The table sits among Nepal's general weights and measures and in a book produced through an unusually rich mixture of prior scholarship, Nepalese archives, original documents and contemporary informants, although the immediate source of the paper lines is not identified. This strengthens the historical plausibility of Cambridge's ten-sheet reading of Hodgson's 1843 `ḍhyāp` bill while also sharpening two methodological rules: local package names need contextual numerical values, and paper-count metrology must remain separate from sheet-format metrology.
