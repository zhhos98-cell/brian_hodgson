### Case 1 — 1831–33: material + technical description travel

Hodgson explicitly acknowledges that Wallich had already described the paper plant and positions his own intervention around manufacture.

What Hodgson adds is a much more operational protocol: implements, ash-liquor handling, boiling/beating, churning, screening, floating cloth-bottom frame, manual distribution/thickness control, fire drying, polishing, normally no sizing, large-sheet capability, and dried pulp brick/tile form for transport.

Working interpretation: Hodgson recoded already circulating Himalayan paper knowledge into a more executable, measurable and transportable technical specification.

Cowan's 1833 British remanufacture then tests whether material + written specification were sufficient. They were not: British remade paper was brittle/stiff/inferior while Nepal-made finished sheets remained highly valued and could take a bank-note impression with `perfect fidelity`.

Actor/source caution: the 1832 printed article names no technical informant and does not state that Hodgson personally observed the full process. It does preserve unnamed Nepalese testimony elsewhere in the account. Keep the source of the detailed procedure OPEN until the bounded 1831 retrieval chain below is exhausted.

### Case 2 — 1841: the makers travel

This is the centre of the article and should receive the most space.

Direct 1842 printed correspondence establishes:

- Auckland asks Hodgson for skilled papermakers;
- Hodgson brokers the request through the Nepal Durbar;
- five papermakers arrive in Darjeeling;
- hierarchy/pay: head maker Rs 10/month; mate/first assistant Rs 8; three others Rs 8 each;
- makers prefer Pacheem/Pucheem for paper plant, ash woods, running water and lower carriage;
- Campbell overrides their siting preference to keep manufacture nearer his observation;
- wet bark in carriage mildews and becomes `altogether useless for paper-making`;
- makers modify Hodgson's published apparatus/process: flat stone + double-headed mallets, rejection of iron because it darkens paper, preference for tinned copper, continuous water to remove alkali;
- ash source, bark condition, water and site are active technical judgments;
- two Lepchas learn the manufacture;
- Government orders continuation and asks that necessary manufacturers remain on pay.

First-order question: **Did moving the makers solve what moving paper, pulp/material and written procedure had failed to solve?**

### Case 3 — 1841–49: what happened to the paper afterwards?

Do not make Serampore the endpoint.

Follow concrete paper states and uses:

- Campbell whitening trials trade whiteness against toughness;
- O'Shaughnessy chemical bleaching and handoff to Marshman;
- 1842 journal editors: experimental paper strong but slower/less clarifying in filtration;
- O'Shaughnessy's own 1842 *Bengal Dispensatory*: `paper made in Nipal` is best for bulky deposits because it is strong and permits rapid filtration + pressure;
- preserve this as a difference in operational criteria rather than forcing one verdict on whether Nepal paper was a `good filter`;
- India-rubber-treated Darjeeling paper performs strongly as waterproof postal wrapping;
- Marshman's 1842 sequel is already recovered and parsed: machine-web flow/thickness problem, bleaching/process cost, poor general economic fit, but strength-dominant uses such as bank-note paper remain conceivable;
- Serampore's machine environment is concrete: by 1832 Carey describes continuous pulp flow onto a wire web, several cylinders, final steam-heated cylinder; earlier Serampore also used local papermakers/mat frames and an English hand method with fine-wire frames;
- 1849 Hooker letters prove Nepal paper remained an active scientific consumable, requested by bundle/load and even six loads, constrained by coolies/procurement, and used for drying plants and protecting drawing/specimen-ticket paper;
- Hooker's 1849 field narrative also identifies Dhankuta as an active Daphne-paper manufacturing centre, without proving that his requested paper came from that specific manufactory.

First-order question: **Why was Nepal-made paper still worth procuring by the load for field science even after attempts to bleach, remake and mechanize it?**

## HPS payoff — conclusion-level only

Potential higher-order consequences:

- raw material does not automatically carry the performance of the finished object;
- a written protocol can make work portable without carrying every operative judgment;
- moving skilled makers changes what becomes experimentally visible;
- machines can redefine which material behaviours count as virtues or defects rather than simply scale up hand manufacture;
- `good paper` has no single criterion across records, bank-note printing, whitening, filtration, packing, machine production and field botany;
- colonial improvement can turn an already successful made object back into an experimental raw material;
- mechanisation should not be treated retrospectively as the final tribunal of a technology's future.

These are conclusions to earn through the cases, not opening claims.
