### Case 1 — 1831–33: material + technical description travel

Hodgson explicitly acknowledges that Wallich had already described the paper plant and positions his own intervention around manufacture.

What Hodgson adds is a much more operational protocol: implements, ash-liquor handling, boiling/beating, churning, screening, floating cloth-bottom frame, manual distribution/thickness control, fire drying, polishing, normally no sizing, large-sheet capability, and dried pulp brick/tile form for transport.

Working interpretation: Hodgson reformatted already circulating Himalayan paper knowledge into a more executable, measurable and transport-oriented technical specification.

Cowan's 1833 British remanufacture then tests whether material + written specification were sufficient. They were not: British remade paper was brittle/stiff/inferior while Nepal-made finished sheets remained highly valued and could take a bank-note impression with `perfect fidelity`.

The 8 December 1831 Hodgson-to-Prinsep state also had an India-side administrative consequence. Abdul Ali's archival extraction from **Political Original Consultation, 13 January 1832, No.19** shows Hodgson proposing Nepal paper for important plains office records because of its durability and proposing that people in Kumaon, where the plant was abundant, be entrusted with the art of papermaking. Treat these as proposals, not evidence of implementation.

Actor/source caution: the 1832 printed article names no technical informant and does not state that Hodgson personally observed the full process. It does preserve unnamed Nepalese testimony elsewhere in the account. Keep the source of the detailed procedure OPEN until the bounded 1831 source-state comparison below is exhausted.

### 1837 bridge — Government inquiry turns paper quality into operation and procurement

This is not automatically a fourth case. It can function as a short bridge showing where earlier durability/manufacture questions went before the 1841 maker transfer.

Direct primary dossier: A. Campbell, `On Nepalese Paper`, *Transactions of the Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India* 5 (1838), 220–229.

Secure sequence:

- 4 Oct 1837 — T. C. Scott, at Lord Auckland's direction, asks Campbell about insect resistance, suitability for stationery/box lining, price and quantity;
- 15 Nov 1837 — Campbell replies after assembling comparisons, uses, prices, markets and samples;
- 10 Dec 1837 — the dossier is read at the Agricultural and Horticultural Society.

Important first-order results:

- Campbell compares c.1817 Nepal-paper Residency records with same-date English foolscap: the Nepal paper survives repeated fold/edge wear better under the observed packet-storage regime;
- Government adoption therefore becomes a procurement problem involving quantity, Durbar monopoly, seasonality, transport and alternative plains/border markets;
- Campbell distinguishes paper grades by operation rather than treating `Nepal paper` as one homogeneous object;
- he reports Nepal paper used at the General Dispensary for medicine packing and, through Dr Davies at Patna, several maunds monthly in cold-drawn castor-oil manufacture because weaker cloth paper could shed fragments into the oil;
- treated/waxed Nepal paper yields a split postal verdict: Postmaster General trials do not justify general replacement of cloth, while the Nepal Residency continues local wet-weather mail use with satisfactory reported performance.

`NAI Resident's Diary, 25 Oct–18 Nov 1837, Microfilm No.77 / Roll 8` overlaps Campbell's 15 Nov reply and may preserve handling/forwarding/intermediary detail. Diary content is **not yet inspected**.

London consequence:

### Case 2 — 1841: the makers travel

This is the centre of the article and should receive the most space.

Direct 1842 printed correspondence establishes:

- Auckland asks Hodgson for skilled papermakers;
- Hodgson brokers the request through the Nepal Durbar;
- five papermakers arrive in Darjeeling;
- hierarchy/pay: head maker Rs 10/month; mate/first assistant Rs 8; three others Rs 8 each;
- makers prefer Pacheem/Pucheem for paper plant, ash woods, running water and lower carriage;
- Campbell overrides their siting preference to keep manufacture nearer his observation;
- wet bark in carriage mildews and becomes `altogether useless for paper-making`;
- makers modify Hodgson's published apparatus/process: flat stone + double-headed mallets, rejection of iron because it darkens paper, preference for tinned copper, continuous water to remove alkali;
- ash source, bark condition, water and site are active technical judgments;
- two Lepchas learn the manufacture;
- Government orders continuation and asks that necessary manufacturers remain on pay.

## HPS payoff — conclusion-level only

Potential higher-order consequences:

- raw material does not automatically carry the performance of the finished object;
- a written protocol can make work portable without carrying every operative judgment;
- moving skilled makers changes what becomes experimentally visible;
- machines can redefine which material behaviours count as virtues or defects rather than simply scale up hand manufacture;
- `good paper` has no single criterion across records, bank-note printing, medicine/castor-oil handling, postal waterproofing, whitening, filtration, packing, machine production, field botany and opium packaging;
- an affordance is historically useful only when tied to an operation, a condition and a failure profile;
- tests do not necessarily culminate in one verdict: a material can be rejected for general substitution while retained locally or specified for another operation;
- mechanisation should not be treated retrospectively as the final tribunal of a technology's future.

These are conclusions to earn through the cases, not opening claims.
