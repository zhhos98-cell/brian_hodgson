# Nepali / Himalayan paper — nineteenth-century technical calibration

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: active working note. Initial core is based on B. H. Hodgson's own primary account, *On the Native Method of making the Paper, denominated in Hindustan, Nipalese*, *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* 1 (January 1832), pp. 8–11. The paper is dated internally `Nepal Residency, Nov. 1831`. This note will be expanded with Wallich and Campbell sources.

## 1. Why this source changes the project

The project no longer needs to treat `Nepali handmade paper` or `lokta` as a generic contrast class to Whatman paper.

Hodgson himself records a complete operative system:

**paper plant inner bark → alkaline ash liquor → boiling → beating into homogeneous pulp → dilution/churning → screening → floating cloth-bottom frame → manual pulp distribution → drainage → fire drying → peeling → optional surface polishing → folding / bundling / sale / transport / use**.

This gives the Nepali/Himalayan paper side the same kind of technical resolution we are beginning to obtain for Whatman:

- raw material;
- chemical treatment;
- tools;
- sheet-forming technology;
- thickness control;
- drying;
- finishing;
- supply geography;
- commodity form;
- transport form;
- administrative use.

The correct comparison is therefore **two paper-making/procurement ecologies**, not `European scientific paper` versus `indigenous paper` as symbolic types.

## 3. Implements: a deliberately low-capital paper technology

Hodgson begins by stressing that the necessary implements can be rudely made while still serving the purpose.

He lists:

1. a shallow, broad stone mortar or smoothly excavated stone block;
2. a hard-wood mallet/pestle, scaled to the quantity of boiled bark;
3. a close wicker basket through which water drips slowly through ashes;
4. an earthen receiver for the ash extract;
5. an open-mouthed metal pot for boiling bark (iron/copper/brass acceptable);
6. a coarse/open sieve that retains only lumps while letting usable pulp pass;
7. a buoyant wooden frame with a cloth bottom fine enough to retain pulp while allowing water to drain.

Additional requirements:

- a cistern of clear water;
- abundant firewood;
- ashes;
- a fireplace;
- strips of the inner bark of the paper plant.

### Analytical importance

The productive apparatus is not a single tool. It is an **assemblage of stone, wood, wicker, ceramic, metal, cloth, water, fire, ash and bark**.

Paper quality therefore emerges from relations among materials and operations rather than from fibre identity alone.

---

## 4. Alkali extraction: ash liquor is an active process material

Hodgson specifies an alkaline liquor made by dripping water slowly through oak ashes. If the first percolation is too weak, the liquid is passed through the ashes again.

The resulting strong, dark-red ash extract is heated and used to boil strips of inner bark.

### Process logic

The paper plant is not simply `beaten into pulp`.

There is a distinct chemical/thermal sequence:

**ash → filtered alkaline extract → heated liquor → bark cooking/softening**.

For the paper census this suggests new historically specific observations/questions:

- residual alkalinity / conservation chemistry if ever tested;
- whether surface/fibre appearance differs from untreated bark;
- whether ash species matters;
- whether Campbell's later Darjeeling experiments reproduce the same liquor strength and boiling time;
- availability of suitable firewood/ash as a siting constraint on manufacture.

Do not infer chemical properties of surviving sheets from the recipe alone without testing.

---

## 5. Bark preparation and beating

Hodgson describes strips of inner bark roughly cubit-length and inch-width scale. They are boiled until soft, then beaten in the stone mortar with the hard-wood mallet until converted into a homogeneous dough-like pulp.

The pulp is then mixed with clean water and mechanically agitated/churned until it loses stringiness and disperses readily in water.

### Affordance/failure implications

This is a high-value point for the project's Wilson-derived formula.

The desired state is not merely `pulp` but **pulp with sufficiently broken/dispersible fibre structure to form a uniform sheet**.

Candidate process failures to seek in Campbell or later sources:

- insufficient boiling → bark remains resistant;
- insufficient beating → lumps/strings;
- inadequate dispersion → uneven sheet;
- excessive or insufficient fibre preparation → altered strength/surface;
- wrong liquor concentration → poor softening or fibre damage.

These are hypotheses until primary experimental evidence confirms them.

---

## 6. Screening is explicitly an error-control step

Prepared pulp is first passed through a coarse sieve positioned above the paper frame while both float in water.

The sieve retains the gross/knotty material while useful dispersed pulp passes through into the frame.

This is directly relevant to the repo's existing `recursive auditability / error-control` argument.

The screen is a **material error-control instrument**:

- desired fibre fraction passes;
- lumps/knots are excluded;
- subsequent manual agitation redistributes pulp more uniformly.

Thus paper manufacture itself already contains stages of defect detection/exclusion before any inscription exists.

---

## 7. Sheet formation differs from a Western laid/wove mould grammar

The frame is described as:

- wooden-sided;
- buoyant/floating;
- cloth-bottomed;
- manipulated while floating in a water cistern.

Pulp is manually agitated/spread over the bottom, and the frame is then raised while preserving an even distribution.

### Critical diagnostic consequence

For Hodgson-period `Nepali paper`, do not search only for:

- European laid lines;
- chain lines;
- wire-profile watermark structure.

A cloth-bottom floating frame implies a different family of formation traces may be present.

Potential observations:

- textile/mesh impression;
- criss-cross or woven surface/formation pattern;
- differential fibre distribution;
- irregular inclusions/voids;
- four natural/deckle-like edges if whole sheets survive;
- side-to-side surface difference from frame contact vs exposed face.

These must be checked on actual nineteenth-century Hodgson sheets; later lokta examples are only diagnostic comparators.

---

## 8. Thickness is intentionally adjustable at formation

Hodgson states that the maker varies the thickness/strength of the finished paper by laying a thicker or thinner coating of pulp onto the frame.

This is crucial.

`Nepali paper` is not one thickness or one grade. Variation can be **deliberately manufactured** according to required strength.

### Census consequence

Where multiple Nepali sheets survive, record:

- measured thickness;
- apparent fibre density;
- intended operation;
- bundle/price grade if documented;
- whether thickness correlates with manuscript, wrapper, office-record, natural-history or transport functions.

The 1843 Hodgson/Wallich bill with different prices may eventually provide a commodity-level counterpart to this technical variability.

---

## 9. Drying, peeling and folding

After sheet formation, Hodgson says the frame is set upright near a large fire. Once dry, the sheet is peeled from the cloth/frame and folded.

### Paper-biography implication

The fold can enter the paper biography at the **production/commodity stage**, before manuscript use or archival filing.

Therefore a fold in a surviving sheet cannot automatically be assigned to:

- postal transmission;
- manuscript bundling;
- later archival storage.

Fold chronology needs independent evidence.

### Failure questions

Fire-drying may produce:

- differential drying/tension;
- curling or deformation;
- local overheating;
- surface differences;
- changes in later folding behaviour.

Again, test rather than assume.

---

## 10. Optional polishing, normally no sizing

Hodgson reports that smoothing/polishing is only occasionally considered necessary. When done, dried sheets are rubbed on boards with either the convex side of a conch shell or, for large sheets, a broad smooth hard-wood tool.

He explicitly says that sizing is not normally needed to keep ink from running.

### This is a major Whatman/Nepali comparison axis

British rag/wove papers and Hodgson's described Himalayan bark paper need not achieve writable surfaces through the same finishing system.

Potential comparison:

**British paper:** formation + sizing/finishing tailored to ink/drawing/printing markets.

**Himalayan bark paper:** fibre/process structure + optional burnishing/polishing, with Hodgson explicitly reporting acceptable ink behaviour without sizing.

Do not transform this into `Nepali paper is unsized` as an absolute statement for every surviving sheet. It is a claim about the process Hodgson observed/described.

### High-value physical tests

- ink feathering/penetration;
- burnished vs unburnished surface zones;
- surface gloss under raking light;
- fibre compaction;
- sizing tests if conservation access permits.

## 11. Large-sheet capability

Hodgson notes that makers could produce fine smooth sheets several yards square.

This matters for the project because paper size is an affordance, not only a bibliographical format.

Large-sheet capability could support:

- diagrams;
- maps;
- large manuscript formats;
- wrappers;
- copying inscriptions;
- natural-history tables;
- protective or transport uses.

Any specific Hodgson use must still be demonstrated case by case.

## 12. Pulp bricks: paper can circulate before becoming paper

One of the strongest findings in the article is that prepared pulp could be dried into **brick/tile form for transport**.

Hodgson explicitly treats this as a convenient commodity form and notes its suitability for shipment to England.

### Analytical consequence

The project needs a pre-sheet material state:

**plant → bark → processed pulp → dried pulp brick → rehydrated/formed sheet**.

This is exactly the sort of transformation Wilson's `making/remaking` model prepares us to see.

### Database consequence

Add candidate entity/state:

- `paper_material_state = dried_pulp_brick`

and events:

- `dry_pulp_for_transport`
- `ship_pulp`
- `rehydrate_or_reprocess`
- `form_sheet_from_imported_pulp`

This could be historically significant for experiments in transferring Himalayan fibre into British papermaking systems.

## 13. Production geography: `Nepalese` is a market name, not a simple production-origin label

Hodgson explicitly warns that the paper called `Nipalese` was, in his account, not principally manufactured in `Nepal Proper`.

He locates manufacture among Cis-Himalayan Bhote communities east of the Kali River, especially in tracts above Nepal proper, with Kathmandu/Nepal acting as a major market.

He also describes circulation:

- southward into Nepal and Hindustan;
- northward toward Tibetan markets/monastic centres.

### Critical historiographical rule

`Nepali paper` is therefore already a **circulation/market category** in Hodgson's own terminology.

Do not code:

`historical_category = Nepali paper` → `production_place = Nepal`

without evidence.

Required separation:

- `historical_name_as_written`
- `production_region_claimed`
- `market_of_purchase`
- `route_of_circulation`
- `actor_ethnonym/community_as_written`
- `modern_geographic_normalization`

This also prevents later national categories from being projected backward.

## 14. Ecology and industrial siting

Hodgson describes paper-making sheds situated amid forests where makers had immediate access to:

- paper plants;
- firewood;
- ash source;
- abundant clean water.

Manufacture is therefore ecologically sited.

The productive unit is:

**forest plant ecology + water source + fuel/ash + mobile/simple workshop equipment + skilled labour**.

This contrasts with the capital-intensive mill, mould, sizing, stationer and ream economy of British Whatman paper, while both systems remain highly organized technological ecologies.

---

## 15. Office records: direct historical use claim

Hodgson's postscript is unusually strong on administrative material performance.

He judges the manufactured paper particularly suitable for office records because of its strength, durability and adequate smoothness for writing, and notes limited adoption in offices in the plains. He explicitly contrasts it with weaker/friable record paper then in use.

### Importance

This is direct evidence against a simple opposition:

`European/Indian machine or rag paper = bureaucracy`

versus

`Nepali paper = manuscript/religion/locality`.

For Hodgson, Himalayan handmade paper could be proposed as **bureaucratic record infrastructure** precisely because of perceived material performance.

### Testable project question

Do surviving Hodgson administrative papers show deliberate selection of Nepali support in contexts of:

- long-term record keeping;
- repeated handling;
- legal/official authenticity;
- transport;
- copying of older documents?

Pilot case Mss Eur Hodgson/55 item 20 is especially important here.

## 16. Proof-engraving pulp: cross-system material transfer

Hodgson reports that dried raw pulp had been sent to England and judged highly suitable as a raw material for paper used in proof engraving.

This creates a striking circulation problem:

The relevant transfer is not merely finished `Nepali paper` entering Britain. It is **Himalayan processed fibre/pulp entering British paper-manufacturing evaluation**.

This is potentially a paper analogue to natural-history specimen transfer:

**local material → stabilized transport form → metropolitan expert evaluation → proposed remanufacture/use**.

Immediate search target:

- identify who received/evaluated the pulp in England;
- determine whether trial sheets or proofs survive;
- search papermaking, engraving, EIC, Society of Arts, East India House or stationer records.

## 17. Relation to the 1831 BL letter

BL Mss Eur Hodgson/6, item 6, ff. 77–81 is catalogued as Hodgson's December 1831 letter to H. T. Prinsep transmitting specimens of Nepal paper and enclosing an account of the local manufacturing method.

The journal article closes with `Nepal Residency, Nov. 1831`.

Working hypothesis:

**Nov. 1831 account / samples → Dec. 1831 correspondence/transmission → January 1832 JASB publication**.

This needs page/image comparison and correspondence-level confirmation.

If correct, the paper article itself has a reconstructable paper/material publication genealogy including actual samples.

## 18. Wilson-derived affordance formula applied to Hodgson's primary account

Possible historically grounded instance:

> In record-making and manuscript operations, Himalayan bark paper could be selected because its fibre/process structure yielded a sheet Hodgson regarded as unusually strong, durable and writable; optional polishing modified surface smoothness, while normal manufacture did not, in his account, require sizing to control ink. The same manufacturing system made sheet thickness adjustable and permitted production of unusually large sheets.

Still missing before article-level causal claims:

- surviving sheet measurements;
- direct comparison with alternative record papers;
- ink/media tests;
- contemporary failure reports;
- evidence that Hodgson selected particular grades for specific scientific operations.

## 20. Current methodological conclusion

After Wilson and the 1832 Hodgson article, `Nepali paper` should be analysed as:

**ecology + raw material + chemical processing + tools + skilled labour + sheet-forming technique + finishing + commodity form + transport + market name + operation + failure profile + reuse**.

This is the same resolution at which the Whatman side should be analysed.

The comparison is therefore not between two cultural symbols. It is between two historically connected material systems capable of being compared operation by operation.
