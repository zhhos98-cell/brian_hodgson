# Georgina Wilson, *Paper and the Making of Early Modern Literature* — notes for the Hodgson project

Source used: Library OCR JSON for Georgina Wilson, *Paper and the Making of Early Modern Literature* (University of Pennsylvania Press). These notes extract only the parts relevant to the Hodgson paper-and-science project.

## 1. Why Wilson matters here

Wilson's central methodological move is to refuse paper as a `mute vehicle`. Paper is treated as a three-dimensional, historically produced material whose fibres, mould traces, watermarks, thickness, reuse, cutting, binding, and movement materially participate in the making of texts and knowledge objects.

For Hodgson this suggests a disciplined research rule:

> Do not assign a fixed epistemic meaning to a paper type in advance. Reconstruct the material affordances and production/use history of particular sheets, then ask how those properties entered scientific practice.

This is especially useful for avoiding an over-simple equation such as `machine-made/Whatman = metropolitan science` and `Nepali handmade paper = local/religious knowledge`.

## 2. Paper as artefact, commodity, and evidence

Wilson's bibliography explicitly invokes John Bidwell's formulation of paper as **evidence, artefact, and commodity**. This tripartite division maps unusually well onto the Hodgson corpus.

### Paper as artefact
- fibre and raw material;
- hand-made vs machine/wove manufacture;
- laid/chain lines;
- watermark;
- thickness, colour, surface, sizing, strength, absorbency;
- sheet dimensions and mould format;
- pasted/double sheets and composite supports.

### Paper as commodity
- procurement;
- paper bundles / reams;
- price and quality grades;
- trade and imports;
- paper pulp as a transported commodity;
- packaging and route choice;
- labour of papermakers, porters, dealers, binders and other intermediaries.

### Paper as evidence
- dating from watermark / mould characteristics;
- provenance and mill/maker reconstruction;
- distinguishing original/copy stages;
- identifying paper batches across drawings, notes, lists and correspondence;
- manuscript dating and support identification;
- scientific drawing and label chronology.

This should become the basic coding scheme for the Hodgson paper census.

## 3. Watermark: not merely a maker's mark

Wilson emphasises two linked properties of watermarks:

1. they are physically produced inside the sheet through differential thickness formed around wires on the mould;
2. they preserve a diachronic trace connecting a later written/printed object to an earlier papermaking site, mould and labour process.

For Hodgson, watermarks therefore have at least four possible uses:

- **dating** a drawing or manuscript support;
- **batch reconstruction**: testing whether sheets in different dossiers came from the same stock/mould family;
- **workflow reconstruction**: identifying whether paper was stocked before a drawing/text was executed or acquired for a particular task;
- **network reconstruction**: linking scientific papers to British/Indian paper supply and to named makers such as Whatman or Towgood where marks survive.

A major practical consequence is that every watermark hit should be recorded with orientation, position, countermark, sheet dimensions, laid/wove structure, and the date/content of the inscription. Do not record only the maker name.

## 4. Affordance is preferable to symbolic paper types

Wilson repeatedly treats paper through affordances rather than through one stable cultural meaning. Paper can be cut, folded, layered, pasted, bound, rotated, reused, strengthened, wrapped, or transformed into interactive devices. Its physical properties condition what users can do with it.

This is particularly important for Hodgson because the same Nepali paper already appears in several different operations:

- writing/copying;
- botanical drying/wrapping;
- transport protection;
- office record preservation;
- manuscript production;
- lists and natural-history records.

The correct question is therefore not `What did Nepali paper mean?` but:

> Which properties of this paper mattered in a given operation, to whom, and relative to what alternative support?

Possible affordance variables for the Hodgson project:
- strength / resistance to tearing;
- flexibility / foldability;
- absorbency;
- drying capacity;
- durability in storage;
- suitability for ink, pencil, wash or engraving/proof work;
- availability in large sheets or particular dimensions;
- capacity for binding/pasting;
- low/high cost;
- portability / weight / bulk;
- compatibility with labels, drawings, specimens and manuscript leaves.

## 5. Paper instruments and science

Wilson's chapter on composition discusses volvelles and other paper instruments. The key lesson is not the specific early-modern devices but the analytical point: paper can be an **operative instrument**, not just a recording substrate. Its thickness, flexibility, ability to rotate, be cut and assembled actively shapes the production of knowledge.

For the Hodgson project this helps frame:
- specimen tickets;
- drawing-number systems;
- comparative tables and lists;
- manuscript questionnaires;
- foliation and slips;
- paper surrogates of inscriptions;
- plant-drying sheets;
- drawings tied to zoological specimens by number.

These should be studied as paper technologies with operational affordances.

## 6. Composition and assembly

Wilson stresses that books and texts are made by physically arranging sheets, fragments, gatherings and smaller paper units. `Composition` is therefore both intellectual and material.

For Hodgson this is directly transferable to composite scientific objects:

- specimen + ticket + drawing + number + description;
- manuscript folios + wrapper + title slip + explanatory note;
- drawing + Sanskrit annotation + European identifier;
- box + contents list + receipts + later catalogue.

The important move is to treat these relations as **assembled**, not naturally given. The unit of scientific evidence can be larger than a sheet and smaller than an institutional collection.

## 7. Reuse and paper afterlives

Wilson's chapter on reuse is useful because it refuses to privilege inscription as the endpoint or highest use of paper. Printed or written sheets could later become binding waste, box lining, wrapping, domestic material, or fragments in new constructions.

For Hodgson, this suggests systematic attention to:
- packet sheets reused as catalogue surfaces;
- store lists later converted to donation lists;
- covers/wrappers reused or newly supplied;
- waste or secondary paper used for protection, interleaving or binding;
- later institutional rebinding and mounting;
- surviving paper whose archival function differs from its original scientific function.

This is especially relevant to the India Office/BL rebinding problem: later conservation/reclassification may preserve sheets while destroying their earlier relational order.

## 8. Production history and invisible labour

Wilson argues that attending to the paper sheet pushes interpretation backwards before author/inscriber/press and exposes labour otherwise hidden: rag collection, sorting, vat work, mould work, couching, trade and transport.

The analogous Hodgson move should be to reconstruct the labour below the named scientific author:
- papermakers;
- fibre collectors / paper-plant procurement;
- copyists and pandits;
- artists/draughtsmen;
- writers/translators;
- shikaris and specimen preparators;
- binders;
- carpenters;
- porters;
- forwarding/clearing agents;
- museum/library staff who later sorted, relabelled or rebound material.

Paper becomes a route for seeing the distributed labour of science without reducing the argument to generic `colonial networks`.

## 9. Limits of direct transfer

Wilson's book is about early modern English literature and literary criticism. Several parts should **not** be imported wholesale:
- its literary-theoretical arguments about authorship/form are not our historiographical endpoint;
- early-modern rag-paper production cannot stand in for nineteenth-century Nepali Daphne/Edgeworthia-based papermaking;
- English waste-paper culture cannot be assumed to map directly onto Nepal/India;
- metaphorical claims about paper should be kept secondary to archival/material evidence in the Hodgson article.

The strongest transferable method is narrower:

> reconstruct paper's material biography and affordances, then show the scientific operations those properties enabled or constrained.

## 10. Immediate implications for the Hodgson research design

### Revise the census
Add the following fields where possible:
- support material / fibre if known;
- handmade / machine-made / wove / laid;
- watermark text/device/date;
- countermark;
- chain-line spacing / mould indicators;
- sheet dimensions;
- thickness / colour / sizing / surface quality;
- pasted/double-layer construction;
- media used: ink / pencil / wash / print;
- physical operation: write / draw / label / dry / wrap / paste / bind / copy / collate / transport;
- paper's inferred affordance in that operation;
- procurement/source/batch evidence;
- reuse / later intervention;
- relation to other sheets or objects.

### Testable Hodgson claims after Wilson

1. **Affordance claim**: paper selection in Hodgson's scientific practice followed material performance requirements more often than a fixed symbolic hierarchy of `European` and `Nepali` papers.
2. **Batch/workflow claim**: watermark and sheet-format analysis can reconstruct batches and temporal stages within Hodgson's drawings and manuscripts.
3. **Paper-instrument claim**: labels, questionnaires, lists, slips and drying/wrapping sheets were operative components of scientific work, not merely records of it.
4. **Labour claim**: the material biography of paper reveals distributed scientific labour before and after Hodgson's inscription.
5. **Afterlife claim**: later rebinding, relabelling and reuse changed evidential relationships even when the paper object itself survived.

## 11. Bibliographic leads from Wilson worth following for this project

High priority:
- John Bidwell, `The Study of Paper as Evidence, Artefact, and Commodity`.
- John Bidwell, `French Paper in English Books` — useful model for reconstructing imported paper through material/bibliographic evidence.
- D. C. Coleman, *The British Paper Industry, 1495–1860* — relevant background for nineteenth-century British supply and paper categories.
- Dard Hunter, *Papermaking: The History and Technique of an Ancient Craft* — technical/historical reference, to be used critically.
- Miles Ogborn, *Indian Ink: Script and Print in the Making of the East India Trading Company* — colonial paper/script infrastructure.
- Jonathan Senchyne, *The Intimacy of Paper* — layers, material biography and paper's social forms.
- Daniel Bellingradt on paper networks and paper dealers — commodity/network model.
- Anna Reynolds and Adam Smyth on waste paper/reuse — useful for wrapper, lining, binding and archival afterlives.

## 12. Current judgement

Wilson is not a direct historiographical model for a Hodgson article, because her explanatory object is literary form. But methodologically she is highly useful for preventing the project from slipping into generic `materiality`.

The sharpest formulation to carry forward is:

**Paper should be studied simultaneously as artefact, commodity, evidence, and operative medium. Its scientific significance lies in historically specific affordances and material biographies, not in an assumed symbolic meaning of paper type.**

# Deep-scan addendum — 2026-08-31

## A. Wilson's actual book architecture matters

The book is organized as Introduction + **Authorship / Composition / Form / Reuse** + Coda. For Hodgson, the transferable sequence underneath those literary categories is:

**paper production before inscription → assembly/composition → physical transformation into operative forms → reuse/afterlife**.

This is more useful than importing the literary chapter categories themselves.

## B. Begin with the sheet, not the later archival object

Wilson explicitly returns to the bibliographical principle that printers print sheets, not books, and follows Lothar Müller in taking the sheet as formed from pulp as the most basic stable unit.

For Hodgson this means the default material unit should not be the later dossier, bound volume, portfolio, box, or catalogue entry. The census must be able to descend to:

- sheet;
- leaf;
- slip;
- cut fragment;
- wrapper;
- pasted layer;
- mounted drawing support;
- label/ticket.

Then it can reconstruct how those units were later combined.

## C. The *Essex Champion* case warns against paper-status binaries

Wilson opens with a copy of *The Essex Champion* printed on rough brown paper containing visible plant/rag inclusions. This violates the expected hierarchy in which coarse brown paper is for wrapping and expensive white paper is for writing/printing.

Direct Hodgson implication: paper category cannot stand in for function or epistemic status. Do not infer from `Whatman`, `wove`, `Nepali handmade`, `brown`, or `white` what the sheet was used for or what actors thought it was worth. Reconstruct actual operations.

## D. Watermark analysis must be technologically specific

Wilson's watermark model is based largely on early-modern mould-made paper: differential thickness, laid/chain structures, paired moulds, non-identical twins, and mould deformation over time.

Hodgson crosses a different technological field: handmade Nepali paper, British mould-made papers, wove papers, and increasingly machine-made papers. Therefore:

> never import mould-pair diagnostics automatically into a machine-paper case; identify the production technology first, then select the relevant watermark/formation diagnostics.

This should become an explicit methodological safeguard in the paper census.

## E. Paper evidence has two levels

Wilson reconstructs New Bibliography's use of paper as a source of `fact`: format, folding, watermarks, collation, typesetting, and binding could solve chronology and production problems. But her own intervention is reflexive: she also asks why scholars notice some material qualities and ignore others.

For Hodgson separate:

### forensic/material questions
- date;
- batch;
- mill/maker;
- sheet pairing;
- copy stage;
- route;
- rebinding or remounting event.

### historiographical questions
- why one repository records watermark but not thickness;
- why `European paper` is sometimes named while `native paper` remains generic;
- why manuscript supports receive material description while zoological drawing supports often do not;
- when conservation technologies make previously invisible evidence legible.

Paper is therefore both evidence about the archive and evidence about the changing rules of what archivists/scholars treated as evidence.

## F. Composition/decomposition is better than `original vs later intervention`

Wilson's volvelle cases are built from paper fragments plus thread, pins/other fittings, and bound sheets. Components can be cut out, attached, detached, lost, replaced, or preserved in a state that prevents their original manipulation.

For Hodgson the better model is a chain of states:

**sheet → cut/folded component → attached working unit → detached/lost → replaced/relabelled → rebound/remounted → digitized**.

This should be used for manuscript wrappers, labels, foliation, mounted drawings, pasted notes, inserted slips, and specimen tickets.

## G. Critical refinement: affordance includes a failure profile

This is the strongest new finding from the full scan.

In the volvelle discussion Wilson does not stop at the claim that paper's affordances enable readers to manipulate the device. She notes that a paper dial can:

- drift;
- get stuck;
- tear;
- return to the same position;
- land across two sections;
- otherwise intervene in the user's intended operation.

Thus an analytically useful affordance is not just `what a material lets an actor do`.

For this project use:

**material affordance = enabled operation + constrained operation + characteristic failure behaviour under specific conditions.**

This gives us a clean bridge from paper materiality to the failed-experiment/error-control argument already developing elsewhere in the project.

Potential paper failure variables to test empirically:

- tear at folds;
- cracking/stiffness;
- ink feathering or bleed;
- wash cockling;
- abrasion;
- mould/insect damage;
- water transmission;
- drying speed;
- paste/adhesion failure;
- label detachment;
- opacity/translucency;
- dimensional instability;
- wet strength;
- brittleness with ageing;
- bulk/weight in transport;
- unsuitable surface for pencil, ink, wash, tracing or proof work;
- cutting/mounting damage.

These are candidate variables, not assumed properties.

## H. Paper can become an instrument by changing shape

Wilson's examples include volvelles, but also folded paper funnels for filtration, cones/horns for managing vapour, and rolled paper tubes for medical application. The transferable point is morphological:

> changing a sheet's shape can convert support material into an operative instrument.

Candidate Hodgson paper instruments therefore include:

- specimen tickets;
- labels;
- questionnaires;
- tabular lists;
- folded packets and wrappers;
- drying/interleaving sheets;
- tracing/copying supports;
- mounted comparative sheets;
- numbered drawing systems;
- paper surrogates of inscriptions.

For each instrument ask: what transformation of paper makes the operation possible, what does the material constrain, and how can it fail?

## I. Revised Hodgson working formula after the deep scan

The weak formulation would be:

`Nepali paper afforded durability.`

The stronger form is:

> **In operation X, paper type A was selected or reused because property P enabled action Y under conditions C; the same property created, prevented, or redistributed failure mode F relative to alternative paper B.**

This formulation should guide future archival coding and any eventual article prose.
