# Nepal Paper as an Administrative Material: core prose after Wave 87

This paper follows Nepal paper through a series of changes in use. In 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson sent specimens of Nepal paper to H. T. Prinsep for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. Hodgson described the paper through qualities that mattered to government. It was cheap. It was abundant. It was tough and durable. It was especially suitable for records. The surviving archival address is Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, Nos. 18–20. In 1937 the Imperial Record Department exhibited this same consultation cluster as evidence for the manufacture of indigenous paper in the Himalayan tracts. Paper durability had become an administrative fact because old records and new specimens could be placed in the same argument.

By 1837 the question had changed. Archibald Campbell treated Nepal paper as a possible supply for government offices. The discussion moved through price, market access, carriage and season. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society then asked for “a small supply of each size and quality of paper manufactured at Nepaul for experimental purposes.” Campbell promised to send a quantity. He also proposed that samples of each variety should go to the Chamber of Commerce. The object was being sorted before it was tested. Size and quality became working categories. Government correspondence was copied into a learned-society dossier. Samples moved beside texts.

The same material entered chemical experiment in 1841. T. Maddock sent two packages of Campbell’s Darjeeling paper to W. B. O’Shaughnessy, the Chemical Examiner. O’Shaughnessy reported on a method of bleaching paper manufactured at Darjeeling. The archival address is Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147. The Imperial Record Department displayed this report in 1937. Abdul Ali later cited the same consultation in his 1944 account of Nepal paper. The report therefore had several documentary lives. It began as part of a political consultation. It later became an archival exhibit. It then became evidence in a historical article.

The spring of 1842 shows a further change of scale. On 25 March Hodgson directed the movement of twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp through Champaran toward Calcutta. The route depended on carriage cost. On 21 April Campbell wrote to G. A. Bushby about the Darjeeling paper experiment. That letter was processed in Political Consultation, 11 May 1842, No. 70. A London Board’s Collection, IOR/F/4/2006/89528, later gathered Darjeeling proceedings for the same year. Its catalogue includes a “report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment in making paper from the bark of the paper tree.” The Board’s Collections were made from papers extracted from Indian Proceedings for review in London. Political Collections form a near-complete duplicate of the Indian Political Proceedings. The paper experiment therefore moved through a chain of documentary forms as well as a chain of material forms.

This history is best understood through those changes in state. Nepal paper could be a record support. It could become a specimen. It could become pulp in packages. It could become the subject of chemical treatment. The same event could also become an Original Consultation, a Proceedings entry, a Board’s Collection or a published extract. These forms preserved different parts of the same material history. They also changed what officials could see. Durability became evidence for office use. Variety became a sample set. Bark became an experimental raw material. Freight turned pulp into a logistical problem. Archival rearrangement turned local experiments into London policy papers. Paper did more than carry information here. Its material properties helped produce the administrative categories through which it moved.
