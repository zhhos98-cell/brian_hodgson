# Nepal Paper as an Administrative Material: core prose after Wave 87

This paper follows Nepal paper through a series of changes in use. In 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson sent specimens of Nepal paper to H. T. Prinsep for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. Hodgson described the paper through qualities that mattered to government. It was cheap. It was abundant. It was tough. It was durable. It was especially suitable for records. The surviving archival address is Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, Nos. 18–20. In 1937 the Imperial Record Department exhibited this same consultation cluster as evidence for the manufacture of indigenous paper in the Himalayan tracts. Paper durability had become an administrative fact. Old records supplied one kind of proof. New specimens supplied another.

By 1837 the question had changed. Archibald Campbell treated Nepal paper as a possible supply for government offices. Price mattered. So did market access. Carriage became part of the calculation. Season shaped the movement of paper. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society then asked for “a small supply of each size and quality of paper manufactured at Nepaul for experimental purposes.” Campbell promised to send a quantity. He also proposed that samples of each variety should go to the Chamber of Commerce. The object was being sorted before it was tested. Size became a working category. Quality became another. Government correspondence was copied into a learned-society dossier. Samples moved beside texts.

The same material entered chemical experiment in 1841. T. Maddock sent two packages of Campbell’s Darjeeling paper to W. B. O’Shaughnessy, the Chemical Examiner. O’Shaughnessy reported on a method of bleaching paper manufactured at Darjeeling. The archival address is Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147. The Imperial Record Department displayed this report in 1937. Abdul Ali later cited the same consultation in his 1944 account of Nepal paper. The report had several documentary lives. It began inside a political consultation. It later became an archival exhibit. It then became evidence in a historical article.

The spring of 1842 shows a further change of scale. On 25 March Hodgson directed the movement of twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp through Champaran toward Calcutta. The route depended on carriage cost. On 21 April Campbell wrote to G. A. Bushby about the Darjeeling paper experiment. That letter was processed in Political Consultation, 11 May 1842, No. 70. A London Board’s Collection, IOR/F/4/2006/89528, later gathered Darjeeling proceedings from the same year. Its catalogue includes a “report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment in making paper from the bark of the paper tree.” Board’s Collections were made from papers extracted from Indian Proceedings. They were used in London when draft despatches were reviewed. The Political Collections preserve an almost complete duplicate of the Indian Political Proceedings. The experiment therefore acquired a London documentary form as well as an Indian one.

This history is best understood through changes of state. Nepal paper served as record support. It became a specimen. Pulp became a transport package. Finished paper became an object of chemical treatment. The documents changed state too. A letter could enter an Original Consultation. Proceedings produced another version of the same business. A Board’s Collection rearranged the papers again in London. Publication created another documentary life. Each form preserved a different part of the material event. Each form also changed what officials could see. Durability became evidence for office use. Variety became a sample problem. Bark became experimental raw material. Freight made pulp into a logistical problem. Archival rearrangement made local experiments legible as metropolitan business. Paper did more than carry information here. Its material properties helped produce the administrative categories through which it moved.
