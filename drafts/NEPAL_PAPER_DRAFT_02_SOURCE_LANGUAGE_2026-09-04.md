# “Nipal Paper Stuff”
## Experiments in the Manufacture of Nepal Paper, 1831–1842

## Abstract

In 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson sent specimens of Nepal paper to H. T. Prinsep for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. He recommended the paper as cheap, abundant, tough and durable, with particular value for records. During the next eleven years the same name was attached to finished sheets, dried pulp, bark, experimental samples and twenty-four packages of “Nepalese paper pulp.” The judgments changed with the thing being tested. British manufacturers called paper remade from imported pulp “brittle” and “stiff,” while earlier Nepal-made sheets had taken a bank-note impression with “perfect fidelity.” Archibald Campbell asked how paper could be bought, carried and made at Darjeeling. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society requested “each size and quality” for experiment. Campbell’s papermakers rejected wet bark as “altogether useless for paper-making.” W. B. O’Shaughnessy tried bleaching. Filtering proved slow. India-rubber treatment produced “perfectly waterproof” wrapping. At Serampore the pulp would not run freely on the machine web. In March 1842 Hodgson sent twenty-four packages toward Calcutta and asked that land and water carriage be compared. The records also changed as the experiments moved. Letters entered Political Consultations. Proceedings copied them. A Darjeeling paper report later appeared in a London Board’s Collection among roads, public works and boundary business. This article follows those experiments closely. It asks what officials, papermakers and learned societies meant when they called the material tough, cheap, white, useful or unsuccessful, and what happened when Nepal paper was required to do a different job.

## I. “Not at all successful”

In November 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson sent the Asiatic Society of Bengal an account of the paper “denominated in Hindustan, Nipalese.” It appeared in the first volume of the Society’s journal in January 1832. Hodgson began with manufacture. The inner bark was boiled in an alkaline liquor made from ash. It was beaten and diluted. Coarser matter was removed. The maker spread the pulp by hand on a floating cloth-bottomed frame. Water drained away. The sheet was dried by fire, peeled from the support and folded. Large sheets could be polished with a conch shell or smooth wood. Hodgson added a point that became important in later experiments. The amount of pulp placed on the frame could be varied according to the strength required. A thicker or stronger sheet came from a different quantity of pulp, rather than from a different species of paper.[^1]

The account also separated several things that later records often called Nepal paper. Finished sheets were sold in Kathmandu. Prepared pulp could be dried into brick-like forms for carriage. Hodgson wrote that ordinary writing paper required no sizing because ink did not run on the surface. Fine paper could be made very large. The name was geographical only in a loose sense. Hodgson located much manufacture among Cis-Himalayan Bhote communities east of the Kali River rather than in Nepal proper. Kathmandu functioned as a market. A sheet purchased there could therefore carry the name “Nipalese” without fixing its exact place of manufacture.

A government transaction began at almost the same time. On 8 December 1831 Hodgson sent H. T. Prinsep specimens of Nepal paper for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. A. F. M. Abdul Ali later cited the business under Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, and identified No. 19 as the item from which he drew Hodgson’s argument. The Imperial Record Department’s 1937 exhibition grouped the paper business more broadly as Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, Nos. 18–20. Its exhibit title was “Indigenous method of manufacturing paper in the Himalayan tracts (Nepal, etc.) in the early part of the 19th century.”[^2] The three adjacent numbers matter because Hodgson’s letter, the specimens and the government response belonged to one piece of business even though the record room later divided the papers into numbered items.

Hodgson’s recommendation used words suited to government. The paper was cheap. It could be obtained in quantity. It was tough. It was durable. Records supplied the obvious use. This was already different from describing a craft for the Asiatic Society. A government office wanted to know how a sheet would behave after repeated handling and storage. Later Campbell made this test explicit by pointing to old government records written on Nepal paper. He compared their condition with English foolscap and referred to records dating from 1817 that remained fresh and serviceable. Old office paper had become evidence for buying new office paper.[^3]

The early enthusiasm met a much harder test in Britain. Hodgson’s 1832 postscript mentioned dried pulp sent to England. In 1833 the Asiatic Society printed the report of manufacturers who had actually worked the material. George Swinton had sent “Nipal Paper Stuff” home in 1831. The result was, in the report’s words, “not at all successful.” Charles Cowan’s specimens lacked the softness and toughness of paper made in Nepal. They were “brittle” and “stiff.” Some appeared almost varnished. Brown gritty spots remained. The stuff held water on the sieve and shrank in drying. Strong bleaching required much more chemistry than ordinary paper furnish and was thought to have injured the texture.[^4]

The same report supplied a striking comparison. Thin sheets made in Nepal and sent to Britain in finished form were “exceedingly tough and flexible.” Their colour was imperfect, yet the surface had taken the minute impression of a bank-note plate with “perfect fidelity.” The British paper and the Nepal paper therefore could not be compared simply by fibre source. One had already been made as a sheet in the Himalayan process. The other had crossed the ocean as dried stuff and entered British papermaking. The trials changed the process before they changed the verdict.

The contrast also explains several later disagreements without requiring a new vocabulary for each one. A tough sheet could be valuable for a record. The same stuff could drain badly on a sieve. Dark colour could be tolerated in one use and become a serious defect in another. Finished Nepal paper could take a fine impression, while paper remade from imported pulp disappointed British manufacturers. The words “tough,” “brittle,” “stiff” and “perfect fidelity” described results at particular stages. They did not describe one permanent hierarchy of papers.

The 1833 report did not end government interest. It clarified what had to travel. Pulp alone had failed to reproduce the valued sheet. Hodgson’s printed description could explain operations, but a description did not settle the choice of ash, boiler, water or hand tools. By 1841 the Government of India would try another form of transfer. Instead of sending only pulp or paper, it would move papermakers.

The archive retained these early experiments under dates different from the underlying letters. Hodgson wrote to Prinsep on 8 December 1831. The surviving archival address used by Abdul Ali and the 1937 exhibition is 13 January 1832. In this pre-Mutiny record system, the consultation date belongs to the day on which the business was considered and recorded. “Original Consultation” could contain incoming papers with associated orders or reply drafts. Proceedings produced another copy or account of the same business.[^5] The difference is small in the 1831 case, but it becomes important later. A paper experiment could acquire several dates as it moved through government.

## II. “Each size and quality ... for experimental purposes”

By 1837 Archibald Campbell was asking a different question. Nepal paper had already been praised for durability. Campbell wanted to know whether it could supply government offices at a workable price. His correspondence with T. C. Scott treated the paper as a possible substitute for government stationery. Old records still supplied evidence for durability. Procurement added other numbers. Price mattered. Carriage mattered. Markets mattered. So did season.

Campbell discussed paper moving from the hills toward the plains. Abdul Ali’s later extraction records market points in the Saran and Tirhut region and includes Govindgunge. Campbell estimated carriage toward Patna at roughly Rs 1-12 per maund. He proposed purchasing during the rains, followed by carriage toward the Ganges during the four cold months.[^6] The exact spellings and figures remain tied to Abdul Ali until the underlying Political papers are imaged, but the structure of the calculation is clear. A paper praised as cheap at one market still had to reach the office.

Campbell’s discussion treated finished paper as finished paper. This matters because Hodgson’s 1842 shipment would concern pulp. A price for carrying reams or maunds of sheets cannot simply be transferred to twenty-four packages of pulp. The records themselves distinguish the objects. In 1837 Campbell was trying to make office supply calculable. In 1842 Hodgson would ask a magistrate to compare two routes for an experimental consignment.

The Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India gave the 1837 paper another form. On 20 November it asked Campbell for “a small supply of each size and quality of paper manufactured at Nepaul for experimental purposes.”[^7] The wording does much of the analytical work. The Society did not ask for one representative sheet. It wanted the differences preserved. Size mattered. Quality mattered. The paper was to arrive as a comparative set.

Campbell replied on 28 November. He promised to send a quantity and proposed that “samples of each variety” should also go to the Chamber of Commerce. He enclosed copies of recent correspondence between T. C. Scott and himself “on the subject of this paper, as likely to prove interesting to the Society.”[^8] Samples and correspondence therefore travelled together. One supplied paper that could be handled. The other supplied prices, routes and claims about use.

The Society published this material in its *Transactions* under the title “On Nepalese Paper.” A government exchange had become a learned-society paper. That change did not erase its administrative origin. The copied letters still discussed supply. The physical samples still came from the same market world. Yet the Society’s request gave them a new immediate purpose. The sheets were now “for experimental purposes.” Their internal variety had to be retained long enough to compare.

Campbell’s proposed Chamber-of-Commerce forwarding remained a proposal. The present evidence does not show the Chamber receiving or testing the samples. The distinction matters because a planned route should stay a planned route. The letter still tells us what Campbell expected the samples to do. In the Society they could be experimental specimens. In the Chamber they might become commercial samples. Government offices could ask whether the same paper was durable enough for records and cheap enough for routine supply.

Govindgunge appears repeatedly in these calculations. Company boat-hire material had already made the place legible as a point in river transport. Campbell’s 1837 discussion placed it inside paper-market geography. Hodgson’s 1842 letter would name “Govind Gang” as a possible point from which pulp could go directly to Calcutta by boat. These are separate records. Together they explain why the place could be useful in more than one paper scheme. Markets and river carriage met there.

The move from price to experiment also changed what counted as evidence. An old government record could demonstrate survival over twenty years. A fresh sample could demonstrate finish or strength. A classified set could show variation by size and quality. Campbell’s market information could show price. A carriage estimate could show what the paper cost after movement. The paper did not have to possess one overall value. Officials kept asking narrower questions.

This pattern becomes even clearer in 1841. The question ceased to be whether Nepal paper was durable. Campbell already thought it was. The new questions concerned manufacture at Darjeeling and improvement of the finished sheet. Government therefore asked for people who knew how to make it.

## III. “Altogether useless for paper-making”

Lord Auckland asked Hodgson to send persons skilled in Nepal paper manufacture. Hodgson used the Nepal Residency to obtain them. The Nepal Durbar sent five papermakers. Campbell received the party at Darjeeling in April 1841. The published correspondence gives their pay. The head maker received Rs 10 a month. His mate received Rs 8. Three other workers received Rs 8 each. Total monthly wages came to Rs 42.[^9] The figures make the experiment unusually concrete. Government was paying for skill, not merely importing a material.

The papermakers arrived before a manufactory existed. Campbell needed bark. He needed water and implements. He had to choose a site. The makers preferred the paper-tree jungle near Pacheem. Bark was close there. Woods suitable for ash were close as well. They had clear running water, and carriage would be cheaper. Campbell chose another place because he wanted the process nearer to his own observation. He selected a stream near Auckland Road. A small building went up. A tank was dug. Bamboo conduits carried water in what Campbell called the “Lepcha fashion.”[^10]

Campbell’s choice created a cost immediately. Bark now had to travel farther from Pacheem. A batch that had already been dried became wet during carriage. It mildewed. The papermakers called it “altogether useless for paper-making.”[^11] Their statement placed drying inside manufacture even though the failure occurred on the road. The bark had already undergone preparation. Rain during carriage undid it before the material reached Campbell’s workshop.

The makers also qualified Hodgson’s printed description. Campbell used the 1832 article as a guide, yet the working practice differed in details. Hodgson had described a stone mortar and wooden pestle. The makers at Darjeeling used a flat stone and double-headed hand mallets. They rejected iron boilers because iron darkened the paper. Tinned copper was preferred; brass could serve. Water had to keep flowing through the tank so that alkali left the pulp. A published account had made the process visible in outline. The workers supplied choices that appeared only when a workshop had to function.

Ash was one of those choices. Campbell’s correspondence linked different woods to different results. Poor ash could leave the paper dingy. Fuel and alkaline preparation were therefore connected. The makers associated certain woods with better paper and mentioned occasional use of paper-tree sticks as a craft “secret.” Campbell did not need to settle the chemistry to record the practice. He needed to know what the makers selected when they wanted a particular result.

He also tried to distinguish the plants. Campbell recorded paper trees by features including flower colour and elevation. The makers associated types of tree with different paper qualities. Campbell planted specimens in his garden and proposed using flowering to test the distinctions. He heard of unusually white paper made near Pemiong from another tree and considered bringing in someone familiar with that process. The Darjeeling experiment therefore moved from copying one known procedure toward comparison among plants and makers.

The trial produced 3,300 sheets weighing 1 maund 7 seers. Campbell calculated an experimental cost of about Rs 25-8 per maund and warned against treating the figure as a normal production price.[^12] His first object had been to learn whether the paper could be made at Darjeeling and to report the process to Lord Auckland. The Government ordered the experiment continued. Two Lepchas had learned the work, and Campbell was asked to retain the necessary makers. The workshop had become a training place as well as a manufactory.

The experiment then shifted toward colour. Campbell thought Darjeeling paper compared poorly with English paper in whiteness and smoothness. Finer sieves might remove stringy matter. Whitening proved harder. Immersion followed by exposure to rain and air made the pulp considerably whiter but destroyed the “wonderful toughness” of the untreated material. Exposure on a board preserved more toughness and produced little whitening. Campbell abandoned the immersion method and planned further trials after the rains.[^13]

The phrase “wonderful toughness” should stay beside the failed whitening. The result resembles the 1833 British trial without requiring the experiments to be identical. In Britain heavy bleaching had been blamed for damage to texture. At Darjeeling a treatment that made the pulp whiter also weakened the quality Campbell valued most. Whiteness and toughness could not simply be added to each other.

Government sent the problem to Calcutta. Campbell’s paper travelled in small despatches for W. B. O’Shaughnessy, the Chemical Examiner. O’Shaughnessy reported on bleaching paper manufactured at Darjeeling. The Imperial Record Department later displayed the report under Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147.[^14] A workshop problem had entered a Political Consultation and the Chemical Examiner’s office.

O’Shaughnessy preferred chlorine dissolved in water to chloride of lime. His explanation concerned the colouring matter in the Darjeeling paper and the effect of lime. The published dossier mentions red lead, salt and sulphuric acid among the process materials. It also notes that reagents could be obtained in the Calcutta bazaar. The effort to make Nepal paper whiter therefore required another supply chain and another set of technical decisions.

Other uses placed little value on whiteness. Government considered the paper as a substitute for English filtering paper. Imported filters cost about Rs 9 per ream; the Darjeeling paper was understood to be producible at around Rs 3 for the same purpose. The paper had mechanical strength. A filter might be pressed or twisted without tearing. Actual filtration was less successful. The Society’s editors found it slow and reported that the liquor could be less clear than with good European filtering paper.[^15]

Postal wrapping produced a different verdict. Darjeeling paper treated with India rubber was reported “perfectly waterproof.” The prepared paper weighed about one-third as much as the same area of wax cloth used by the Government Post Office. Post Office authorities considered substitution.[^16] For this use colour mattered little. Strength and low weight mattered directly. Waterproofing added another required property without asking the sheet to resemble white English stationery.

Campbell’s paper therefore accumulated several descriptions in one year. It was tough. It could become whiter at the cost of toughness. It was strong enough to twist as a filter, yet filtration was slow. With India rubber it became “perfectly waterproof.” These results came from separate tests. They make more sense side by side than under a single judgment of success.

O’Shaughnessy then sent his process to Mr Marshman at the Serampore Paper Mills for practical experiment on a larger scale. The Serampore sequel, printed in 1842, again altered the verdict. Heavy bleaching was required. The bark did not promise an attractive general paper beside materials already available to the mill. Strength remained its chief advantage. Marshman could imagine a use such as bank-note paper, where strength might compensate for colour.[^17]

The machine supplied the hardest limit. When Marshman tried to make thicker paper, mucilaginous matter stopped the pulp from running freely on the web. The material would not behave as the machine required. Hodgson’s 1832 hand process had allowed a maker to spread more or less pulp on a floating frame. At Serampore thicker paper depended on regular movement through machinery “then in vogue.” A variable handled by hand became a flow problem on the machine.

Marshman’s result should be placed beside the first British trial. In 1833 dried “Nipal Paper Stuff” had failed to reproduce the valued Nepal-made sheet. In 1842 the material again met a production system that required different behaviour. The paper tree had not ceased to produce strong paper in the hand process. The problem was that a strong hand-made sheet and a furnish that ran well on a machine were different requirements.

The editors were more favourable to packing, especially wet-season dak or bhangy parcels. The paper’s strength and lightness suited this work. Waterproof treatment made the use still more attractive. Nepal paper could therefore seem least successful when required to become ordinary machine paper and more successful when used to carry other things.

## IV. “Twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp”

On 25 March 1842 Hodgson wrote from the Nepal Residency to G. Woodcock, Joint Magistrate of Champaran. A later transcript preserves the letter from the Bihar State Archives register. Hodgson wrote that, “by direction of Mr. Secretary Halliday,” he had sent “twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp” toward Calcutta. His “people” would deliver the packages to Woodcock. Woodcock was to forward them and return a “bill of charges.”[^18]

The count is of packages. Hodgson did not give the mass of pulp. The letter also leaves the form inside the packages open. They may have contained bricks or another prepared state. What the record establishes is enough. Experimental raw material had been divided into twenty-four transport units. Someone had to receive them. Someone had to pay for carriage. The cost had to be reported back.

Hodgson gave Woodcock a choice of routes. The packages could go by land toward Patna. Woodcock was to compare the cost of land carriage from Motihari with water carriage. If the land route proved expensive, he could send the packages from “Govind Gang” directly to Calcutta by “a small safe boat” under a Burkundaz.[^19] Freight cost, river access and security all entered the decision. The experiment had already begun before the pulp reached any paper mill.

The missing papers define the limit of this consignment. The initiating direction from “Mr. Secretary Halliday” has not been recovered. Frederick James Halliday is the likely identification, but the order itself would show how the Government framed the shipment. Woodcock’s bill would reveal the route actually chosen and its cost. A terminal receipt would show who took custody in Calcutta. The present evidence does not identify the twenty-four packages as Serampore feedstock.

The dates make that last distinction especially important. Hodgson’s letter is 25 March 1842. Abdul Ali reports Campbell’s experimental manufactory as closing under existing economic conditions on 16 April. Campbell wrote to G. A. Bushby on 21 April. That paper business entered Political Original Consultation on 11 May as No. 70. A later London Board’s Collection contains a report by Campbell on his experiment with the bark of the paper tree. The events cluster tightly. The sources still do not turn them into one consignment.

The documents themselves show why dates have to be kept separate. Campbell’s underlying letter is dated 21 April. Abdul Ali cited it under Political O.C., 11 May 1842, No. 70. The consultation date records government handling rather than Campbell’s act of writing. The 1831 Hodgson letter had already shown the same pattern. An 8 December letter entered a 13 January consultation. The record office made another chronology as it processed the business.[^20]

The 1937 Imperial Record Department exhibition offers a further checkpoint. It grouped the 1832 Nepal paper business as Nos. 18–20 rather than isolating the number Abdul Ali later quoted. It also displayed O’Shaughnessy’s bleaching report as Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147.[^21] Abdul Ali was Keeper of the Records of the Government of India and signed the complete exhibition list as Secretary to the Indian Historical Records Commission. When he published “The Daphne Paper of Nepal” in 1944 he was therefore writing from close proximity to files that had already been identified and displayed.

A London record gives Campbell’s 1842 experiment another setting. British Library IOR/F/4/2006/89528 is a Board’s Collection dated March to November 1842. Its catalogue includes a “report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment in making paper from the bark of the paper tree.” The same file includes Darjeeling roads, public works and boundary business.[^22] Campbell’s experiment had entered a packet organized around Darjeeling government.

The British Library’s description of IOR/F/4 explains the file. Board’s Collections were made from copies of papers extracted from the Proceedings of Indian governments and from earlier correspondence. They were assembled for Board of Control consideration of draft despatches. The Political Collections survive on a scale that allows the series to be treated as an almost complete duplicate of the Indian Political Proceedings.[^23] “Duplicate” did not mean that the papers remained in the same order. London regrouped them by subject for another act of government.

This later file should not yet be equated item for item with Political Consultation, 11 May 1842, No. 70. The overlap is strong: Campbell, Darjeeling, paper-tree manufacture and the same year. A direct contents cross-reference would close the line. The series relation is already secure enough to explain why the report appears beside roads and boundaries. In London the paper experiment was one part of Darjeeling business.

That change of setting also helps explain later retrieval. A historian searching only for paper might miss a report catalogued under a wider Darjeeling subject. A later exhibition could pull the same technical matter out of a political file and present it as “indigenous method” or paper manufacture. Abdul Ali could then use the consultation number in a historical article. The record acquired new neighbours each time an institution used it for another purpose.

The paper and the papers therefore met at the end of the story in a literal way. Hodgson’s experimental material travelled as sheets, pulp and packages. Reports about those movements travelled as letters and consultations. Proceedings copied the business. Board’s Collections extracted it for London review. Learned societies printed technical correspondence. A twentieth-century exhibition selected the files again. The same material technology carried both the experimental object and much of the evidence now used to reconstruct it.

## Conclusion

Between 1831 and 1842 the phrase “Nepal paper” covered several things that officials themselves kept separating. Hodgson described bark preparation and hand manufacture. He sent finished specimens to government. Swinton sent “Nipal Paper Stuff” to Britain. Campbell calculated the cost of finished sheets. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society requested “each size and quality” for experiment. Five papermakers went to Darjeeling. Bark spoiled in transit. O’Shaughnessy bleached paper. Marshman tried the stuff on a machine. Hodgson later sent twenty-four packages of pulp toward Calcutta. The history becomes clearer when those objects remain distinct.

The judgments remain distinct as well. Finished Nepal-made paper was “exceedingly tough and flexible” and could take a bank-note impression with “perfect fidelity.” Paper remade in Britain was “brittle” and “stiff,” and the trials were “not at all successful.” Campbell valued “wonderful toughness” and found that one whitening treatment destroyed it. Filtering paper could be strong and still work slowly. India-rubber paper could be “perfectly waterproof.” At Serampore the pulp would not run freely on the machine web. None of these phrases requires an overall verdict on Nepal paper. Each answers a practical question.

Government interest also changed its question. In 1831 Hodgson recommended a durable support for records. In 1837 Campbell asked what the paper cost after markets, carriage and season were taken into account. The Society asked for each size and quality. Darjeeling asked whether the process could be reproduced with five makers and local resources. Calcutta asked whether colour could be changed. Serampore asked whether the stuff could enter machine manufacture. The 1842 letter asked which route would carry twenty-four packages most cheaply and safely.

The failures are particularly useful because they preserve the actors’ own distinctions. Mildew made dried bark “altogether useless for paper-making.” Bleaching separated whiteness from toughness. Filtering separated tensile strength from speed and clarity. Machine forming separated the ability to make a tough hand sheet from the ability of pulp to run freely on a web. The British trial separated transportable raw stuff from the finished Nepal-made sheet. Each failure identified another condition of manufacture or use.

The archive followed a similar series of changes without needing to be treated as a second abstract system. Hodgson’s 1831 letter became part of a January 1832 Political Consultation. O’Shaughnessy’s paper entered a November 1841 consultation. Campbell’s April 1842 letter appeared under a May consultation number. A London Board’s Collection later placed a report on paper-tree manufacture beside other Darjeeling business. The 1937 Imperial Record Department exhibition selected paper files again. These later arrangements are part of why the experiments can now be reconstructed and part of why their dates and contexts have to be checked carefully.

Nepal paper never needed to become one stable colonial technology in order to matter. It mattered because officials repeatedly tried to make it do particular jobs. Hodgson’s strongest recommendation concerned records. Campbell’s Society wanted samples “for experimental purposes.” Darjeeling tried to manufacture and improve the sheet. The Post Office valued low weight and waterproofing. Marshman found a problem at the machine web. Hodgson’s March 1842 letter turned pulp into twenty-four packages and a choice between road and river. The paper changed whenever the question changed.
