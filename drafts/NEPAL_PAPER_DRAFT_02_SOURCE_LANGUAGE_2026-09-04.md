# “Nipal Paper Stuff”
## Experiments in the Manufacture of Nepal Paper, 1831–1842

## Abstract

In 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson sent specimens of Nepal paper to H. T. Prinsep for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. He recommended the paper as cheap, abundant, tough and durable, with particular value for records. During the next eleven years the same name was attached to finished sheets, dried pulp, bark, experimental samples and twenty-four packages of “Nepalese paper pulp.” The judgments changed with the thing being tested. British manufacturers called paper remade from imported pulp “brittle” and “stiff,” while earlier Nepal-made sheets had taken a bank-note impression with “perfect fidelity.” Archibald Campbell asked how paper could be bought, carried and made at Darjeeling. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society requested “each size and quality” for experiment. Campbell’s papermakers rejected wet bark as “altogether useless for paper-making.” W. B. O’Shaughnessy tried bleaching. Filtering proved slow. India-rubber treatment produced “perfectly waterproof” wrapping. At Serampore the pulp would not run freely on the machine web. In March 1842 Hodgson sent twenty-four packages toward Calcutta and asked that land and water carriage be compared. The records changed as the experiments moved. Letters entered Political Consultations. Proceedings copied them. A Darjeeling paper report later appeared in a London Board’s Collection among roads, public works and boundary business. This article follows those experiments closely. It asks what officials, papermakers and learned societies meant when they called the material tough, cheap, white, useful or unsuccessful, and what happened when Nepal paper was required to do a different job.

The paper attracted attention because it already worked. Hodgson did not begin from an unknown plant and propose a future technology. He began from a manufactured sheet in routine use. The difficulty came when officials tried to move that success elsewhere. A finished Nepal-made sheet could survive transport and remain tough. Dried pulp could arrive in Britain and fail. Papermakers could move to Darjeeling and rebuild the process, while bark spoiled on the road to their workshop. A sheet praised for toughness could lose that quality when whitened. Strong paper could make a poor filter and excellent wrapping. The history therefore lies in the sequence of questions posed to the material. Those questions were practical and usually recorded in practical language.

## I. “Not at all successful”

In November 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson sent the Asiatic Society of Bengal an account of the paper “denominated in Hindustan, Nipalese.” It appeared in the first volume of the Society’s journal in January 1832. Hodgson began with manufacture. The inner bark was separated from the plant and passed through an alkaline liquor made from ash. It was boiled and beaten. The pulp was diluted. Coarser matter was removed. The maker spread the pulp by hand on a floating cloth-bottomed frame. Water drained away. The sheet was dried by fire, peeled from the support and folded. Large sheets could be polished with a conch shell or smooth wood. Hodgson added a point that became important in later experiments. The amount of pulp placed on the frame could be varied according to the strength required. A thicker or stronger sheet came from a different quantity of pulp rather than from a different species of paper.[^1]

Hodgson’s account was specific about what European papermakers might regard as absent. Ordinary writing paper did not require routine sizing because ink did not run freely into the sheet. Fine paper could be made smooth enough for careful work. Large formats were possible. The frame and pulp quantity gave the maker control over dimensions and strength. These were ordinary observations in a manufacturing description, but later experiments repeatedly returned to the same points. British manufacturers worried about drainage and finish. Campbell worried about colour and smoothness. Marshman worried about whether pulp would run on a machine web. The 1832 account describes a hand process before those later requirements entered.

It also separated several things that later records often called Nepal paper. Finished sheets were sold in Kathmandu. Prepared pulp could be dried into brick-like forms for carriage. Bark could be traded or moved before pulping. Hodgson located much manufacture among Cis-Himalayan Bhote communities east of the Kali River rather than in Nepal proper. Kathmandu functioned as a major market. A sheet purchased there could therefore carry the name “Nipalese” without fixing its exact place of manufacture. The name travelled more easily than the workshop.

The distinction between sheet and stuff already mattered in Hodgson’s own article. A finished sheet carried the results of bark preparation, ash, beating, dilution, hand distribution, drainage and drying. A dried pulp brick carried only part of that history. It could be stored and transported, then subjected to another manufacturing process. Hodgson saw the brick as a practical form of carriage. British manufacturers soon showed that carriage alone did not preserve the qualities that had made the finished sheet attractive.

A government transaction began at almost the same moment. On 8 December 1831 Hodgson sent H. T. Prinsep specimens of Nepal paper for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. A. F. M. Abdul Ali later cited the business under Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, and identified No. 19 as the item from which he drew Hodgson’s argument. The Imperial Record Department’s 1937 exhibition grouped the paper business more broadly as Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, Nos. 18–20. Its exhibit title was “Indigenous method of manufacturing paper in the Himalayan tracts (Nepal, etc.) in the early part of the 19th century.”[^2] The three adjacent numbers indicate that the government treated several papers as one piece of business.

Hodgson’s recommendation used words suited to an office. The paper was cheap. It could be obtained in quantity. It was tough. It was durable. Records supplied the strongest prospective use. A sheet used for records had to survive repeated handling and long storage. The judgment could therefore be tested against paper that had already spent years inside government offices. Archibald Campbell later compared old records written on Nepal paper with English foolscap and referred to government records dating from 1817 whose support remained fresh and serviceable.[^3]

That comparison gave old files a second use. The writing on them recorded earlier business. The paper itself supplied evidence about wear. Campbell did not need a laboratory aging test if he could point to office records that had already been handled for twenty years. The argument moved from surviving paper to future procurement. A paper that remained serviceable in the archive became a candidate for more archives.

The early enthusiasm met a much harder test in Britain. Hodgson’s 1832 postscript mentioned dried pulp that had gone to England. In 1833 the Asiatic Society printed the report of manufacturers who had actually worked the material. George Swinton had sent “Nipal Paper Stuff” home in 1831. British papermakers tried to convert it into saleable paper. The report called the trials “not at all successful.” Charles Cowan’s specimens lacked the softness and toughness of paper made in Nepal. They were “brittle” and “stiff.” Some appeared almost varnished. Brown gritty spots remained in the sheet. The stuff held water stubbornly on the sieve and shrank in drying. Strong bleaching consumed much more chemistry than ordinary furnish and was thought to have injured the texture.[^4]

The report listed several failures because the manufacturers were asking several questions. Could the stuff be washed and drained? Could it be whitened? Could it make a soft sheet? Would the sheet shrink? Would gritty matter remain? A single word such as “unsuccessful” summarized a series of workshop observations. The observations mattered more than the summary because later experiments repeated some of the same difficulties under different conditions.

The 1833 report also contained a direct comparison with finished Nepal-made paper. Thin sheets already manufactured in the Himalayas were “exceedingly tough and flexible.” Their colour was imperfect, yet they had taken the minute impression of a bank-note plate with “perfect fidelity.” The comparison gave British manufacturers a standard they could see and handle. The imported stuff came from the same broad paper tradition but failed to reproduce the visible result.

The difference was not simply national workmanship. The two materials had reached Britain in different states. One arrived after sheet formation. The other arrived before it. The Nepal-made sheet had already passed through its makers’ choices of ash, beating, pulp concentration and drainage. The dried stuff entered another workshop that had its own sieves, bleaching expectations and finishing practices. The trial therefore tested much more than plant fibre.

The bank-note impression is especially useful because it keeps strength beside surface performance. The Nepal-made sheet was praised for toughness and flexibility, but also for receiving fine engraved detail. A strong sheet did not have to be coarse. The British remade sheet was stiff and brittle despite starting from the material expected to produce toughness. These phrases make it difficult to treat one visible characteristic of the plant as the explanation for the finished paper.

The British experiment also established a pattern followed at Darjeeling and Serampore. Whiteness became a recurring demand. In 1833 strong bleaching was associated with damage to texture. At Darjeeling Campbell would again find that a successful whitening treatment weakened the “wonderful toughness” he valued. At Serampore bleaching remained heavy and expensive. The recurrence came from attempts to make a dark, strong handmade paper answer expectations formed around other papers.

The 1833 failure did not close the file. Government interest returned because different uses demanded different things. Records valued durability. Bank-note printing valued a surface that could take detail. Later postal wrapping would value low weight and strength. Machine manufacture would value free flow on the web. The same paper could enter each discussion without requiring one final ranking.

The archive retained these early experiments under dates different from the underlying letters. Hodgson wrote to Prinsep on 8 December 1831. The surviving archival address used by Abdul Ali and the 1937 exhibition is 13 January 1832. In this pre-Mutiny record system, the consultation date belongs to the day on which the business was considered and recorded. “Original Consultation” could contain incoming papers with associated orders or reply drafts. Proceedings produced another copy or account of the business.[^5]

This difference matters because later citations can look like contradictions when they are actually dates for different acts. Hodgson could write in December, government could consider the business in January, the Asiatic Society could print a manufacturing account in the same month, and a later archive catalogue could use the consultation date. The paper had several contemporaneous documentary lives before any later historian began to reconstruct them.

By the end of the first round of experiments, several facts were already clear to the actors. Nepal-made paper could be extremely tough and flexible. It could take a fine impression. Dried stuff could be carried over long distances. British remanufacture could fail badly. The next problem was less whether the paper had useful qualities than how those qualities might enter government supply.

## II. “Each size and quality ... for experimental purposes”

By 1837 Archibald Campbell was asking what Nepal paper would cost as a regular government material. Durability had already been praised. Campbell wanted to know whether offices could obtain enough paper at a workable price. His correspondence with T. C. Scott treated Nepal paper as a possible substitute for government stationery. Old records still supplied evidence for durability. Procurement added markets and freight.

Campbell discussed paper moving from the hills toward the plains. Abdul Ali’s later extraction records market points in the Saran and Tirhut region and includes Govindgunge. Campbell estimated carriage toward Patna at roughly Rs 1-12 per maund. He proposed annual purchasing during the rains, followed by carriage toward the Ganges during the four cold months.[^6] The exact market spellings and figures remain tied to Abdul Ali until the underlying Political papers are imaged, but his account preserves the terms on which the comparison was made.

Season belonged to the calculation because the paper had to move. A price in a hill market did not give the cost at Patna or Calcutta. Rain affected roads and carriage. The cold months created another transport window. The paper therefore entered an annual schedule before it entered the stationery store.

Campbell’s market geography also makes the distinction between manufacture and distribution visible. Kathmandu had functioned as a major paper market in Hodgson’s 1832 account. Campbell looked toward other routes and markets on the approach to the plains. The object under discussion was finished paper, already made and sold. He was calculating how that paper could be aggregated and carried.

This matters because Hodgson’s 1842 shipment concerned pulp. A freight estimate for finished paper cannot simply be transferred to twenty-four packages of experimental raw material. The letters describe different consignments. In 1837 Campbell was trying to make office supply calculable. In 1842 Hodgson would ask a magistrate to compare two routes after the pulp had already been divided into packages.

The Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India gave the 1837 paper another task. On 20 November it asked Campbell for “a small supply of each size and quality of paper manufactured at Nepaul for experimental purposes.”[^7] The request is precise enough to carry its own argument. The Society did not want one representative sheet. It wanted the differences retained. Size mattered. Quality mattered. The paper was to arrive as several papers.

The wording also distinguished a market category from an experimental set. “Nepal paper” was a broad name. “Each size and quality” was a request for internal distinctions. Someone had to select the samples. Someone had to preserve the relation between a particular sheet and the class it represented. Variation was useful because the purpose was comparison.

Campbell replied on 28 November. He promised to transmit a quantity and suggested that “samples of each variety” should also be forwarded to the Chamber of Commerce. He enclosed copies of correspondence recently exchanged with T. C. Scott “on the subject of this paper, as likely to prove interesting to the Society.”[^8] Physical samples and copied letters entered the same learned-society business.

The two forms of evidence answered different questions. A sheet could be handled, folded and compared. A copied letter could provide price, market information and claims about supply. The sample showed a material state. The correspondence described the route and circumstances under which that state might become available in quantity. The Society wanted both.

It then printed the correspondence in its *Transactions* under the title “On Nepalese Paper.” Government papers had become a learned-society publication. The printed text retained Campbell’s administrative concerns while placing them beside the Society’s experimental request. A reader could encounter procurement and experiment in one printed item.

Campbell’s proposed Chamber-of-Commerce forwarding adds a further destination, though only as a proposal. The present record does not show the Chamber receiving the samples or testing them. The proposal still indicates the work Campbell expected different institutions to perform. The Society could compare “each size and quality.” A commercial body might consider marketability or supply. Government offices were interested in durability and cost.

The same sheet could therefore be judged under different headings without changing physically. A thick sheet might be excellent for one purpose and too expensive for another. A finer sheet might take writing well and offer less strength. Campbell’s own correspondence had already linked quality to price and carriage. The Society wanted enough variation to test those relations rather than assume them.

Govindgunge appears repeatedly in these calculations. Company boat-hire material had already made the place legible as a point in river transport. Campbell’s 1837 discussion placed it inside paper-market geography. Hodgson’s 1842 letter would name “Govind Gang” as a possible point from which pulp could go directly to Calcutta by boat. These appearances remain separate events. Their recurrence shows why the place could serve both market movement and forwarding.

The 1837 episode also shows how modest a learned-society intervention could be. The Society did not need to build a paper mill. It could request samples, circulate correspondence and publish results. Its experiment began with selection. The phrase “each size and quality” turned variation into something worth preserving long enough to compare.

The same care should be applied to the proposed Chamber route. Campbell suggested it. That wording should remain visible. A history that turns every proposal into a completed shipment would make circulation look smoother than the records allow. The strongest evidence in this paper history often comes from the difference between a plan and the act that followed.

By 1841 Government had enough confidence in the material to try manufacturing it at Darjeeling. This was a larger undertaking. It required workers rather than samples. It required a site rather than a market table. The process would be reconstructed in front of Campbell, with local bark and water. The transition from 1837 to 1841 therefore changed the object of experiment from finished paper to manufacture itself.

## III. “Altogether useless for paper-making”

Lord Auckland asked Hodgson to send persons skilled in Nepal paper manufacture. Hodgson used the Nepal Residency to obtain them. The Nepal Durbar sent five papermakers. Campbell received the party at Darjeeling in April 1841. The published correspondence gives their pay. The head maker received Rs 10 a month. His mate received Rs 8. Three other workers received Rs 8 each. Total monthly wages came to Rs 42.[^9]

The wage list matters because the skill transfer had a visible labour form. The men were not anonymous appendages to a recipe. Government paid a head maker more than the others. The published account distinguished roles. The workshop depended on people who already knew the sequence of bark preparation, boiling, beating, sheet formation and drying.

The papermakers arrived before a manufactory existed. Campbell needed bark. He needed water and implements. He had to choose a site. The makers preferred the paper-tree jungle near Pacheem. Their reasons were practical. Bark was close. Woods suitable for ash were close. Clear running water was available. Carriage would be cheaper. Campbell chose another place because he wanted the process nearer to his own observation. He selected a stream near Auckland Road. A small building went up. A tank was dug. Bamboo conduits carried water in what Campbell called the “Lepcha fashion.”[^10]

The site decision put Campbell’s need to watch the work against the makers’ need to reduce carriage. Pacheem offered the materials. Auckland Road offered easier inspection. Campbell chose inspection. The consequences appeared almost at once on the route between bark source and workshop.

A batch of bark that had already been dried became wet during carriage. It mildewed. The papermakers called it “altogether useless for paper-making.”[^11] The phrase came before the bark entered the vat. Manufacture therefore included what happened during preparation and carriage. Thorough drying was required after removal of the outer green layer. Once properly dried, bark could be stored. Rewetting created a different material.

The failure was small compared with a failed mill, but it identified a condition that a printed recipe could easily hide. “Dry bark” was not simply a material category at the point of purchase. It was a state that had to be maintained on the road. A transport accident could undo a manufacturing operation already completed.

The makers also qualified Hodgson’s printed description. Campbell used the 1832 article as a guide, yet working practice differed in details. Hodgson had described a stone mortar and wooden pestle. The makers at Darjeeling used a flat stone and double-headed hand mallets. They rejected iron boilers because iron darkened the paper. Tinned copper was preferred. Brass could serve. Water had to keep flowing through the tank so that alkali left the pulp. A published account had made the sequence visible. The workshop made choices visible.

The rejection of iron connected tool material to colour before O’Shaughnessy entered the story. The makers were already controlling darkness through the vessel in which the bark was treated. Later chemical bleaching would approach colour after sheet manufacture. The workers approached it earlier by selecting a boiler.

Ash was another choice. Campbell’s correspondence linked different woods to different results. Poor ash could leave the paper dingy. Fuel and alkaline preparation were connected because wood burned for the process also produced ash. The makers associated certain woods with better paper and mentioned occasional use of paper-tree sticks as a craft “secret.” Campbell recorded the statement as part of the process even though he could not reduce it to a chemical formula.

He also tried to distinguish the paper trees. Campbell recorded types by features including flower colour and elevation. The makers associated different trees with different paper qualities. Campbell planted specimens in his garden and proposed using flowering to test the distinctions. He heard of unusually white paper made near Pemiong from another tree and considered bringing in a person who knew that process. Botanical comparison entered the paper experiment because the makers themselves connected plant difference to paper difference.

The trial reached a substantial scale. Campbell reported 3,300 sheets weighing 1 maund 7 seers. He calculated an experimental cost of about Rs 25-8 per maund.[^12] He warned that the figure came from an intentionally expensive trial. His first object had been to learn whether good paper could be made at Darjeeling and to report the process to Lord Auckland. The calculation therefore measured an experiment before it measured a business.

Government ordered the work continued. Campbell reported that two Lepchas had learned the process. He was asked to retain the necessary makers. The statement is brief, but it shows another movement of skill. Five papermakers had come from Nepal. The Darjeeling workshop now produced local trainees.

The experiment then shifted toward colour and finish. Campbell thought the paper compared poorly with English paper in whiteness and smoothness. Finer sieves might remove stringy matter. Whitening proved harder. Immersion followed by exposure to rain and air made the pulp considerably whiter but destroyed the “wonderful toughness” of the untreated material. Exposure on a board preserved toughness and produced little whitening. Campbell abandoned the immersion method and planned further exposure after the rains.[^13]

The phrase “wonderful toughness” belongs beside the unsuccessful whitening. It tells us what Campbell thought he was losing. The experiment was not a neutral attempt to improve colour. It exchanged one valued quality for another. Campbell stopped when the exchange looked too costly.

The result also recalls the 1833 British manufacturers. Their strong bleaching had been associated with damage to texture. The Darjeeling process was different, yet the same practical conflict reappeared. A treatment that improved colour could weaken the sheet. Whiteness could not simply be added to toughness without attention to the process that produced both.

Government sent the question to Calcutta. Campbell’s paper travelled in small despatches for W. B. O’Shaughnessy, the Chemical Examiner. The correspondence refers to twenty or thirty sheets being sent by dak for treatment. O’Shaughnessy reported on the method of bleaching paper manufactured at Darjeeling. The Imperial Record Department later displayed his report under Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147.[^14]

O’Shaughnessy preferred chlorine dissolved in water to chloride of lime. His explanation concerned the colouring matter in the Darjeeling paper and the effect of lime. The published dossier records red lead, salt and sulphuric acid among the process materials. It also notes that reagents could be obtained in the Calcutta bazaar. Bleaching therefore moved the paper into another workshop with another supply problem.

The Calcutta experiment changed the relation between sample and process. Campbell sent sheets. O’Shaughnessy treated them and could return specimens. His method could then be written down and sent onward. The paper and the written instructions travelled on different routes but belonged to the same experimental sequence.

Filtering supplied a second use. Government considered Darjeeling paper as a substitute for English filtering paper. Imported filters cost about Rs 9 per ream. Darjeeling paper was understood to be producible at around Rs 3 per ream for the purpose. The material had strength. A filter could be pressed or twisted without tearing. Actual filtration complicated the price comparison. The Society’s editors reported slow filtration and found that the resulting liquid could be less clear than with good European filtering paper.[^15]

This test makes the distinction between strength and performance especially plain. A sheet could survive mechanical treatment and still make a poor filter because liquid moved too slowly. The failure was not brittleness. It was the wrong relation between paper structure and flow.

Postal wrapping produced a much more favourable result. Darjeeling paper treated with India rubber was reported “perfectly waterproof.” The prepared paper weighed about one-third as much as the same area of wax cloth used by the Government Post Office. Post Office authorities considered substitution.[^16] The comparison selected strength, waterproofness and low weight. Whiteness scarcely entered.

The wet-season context made the use still more specific. The Society’s editors discussed packing for dak or bhangy parcels. The paper could protect other papers or goods while adding less weight than wax cloth. A material that had resisted improvement as white stationery could be attractive as transport equipment.

Campbell’s paper therefore accumulated several descriptions in one experimental dossier. It had “wonderful toughness.” Whitening could damage that toughness. As filtering paper it remained strong but slow. With India rubber it became “perfectly waterproof.” None of these judgments had to displace the others because they described different work.

O’Shaughnessy then sent his process to Mr Marshman at the Serampore Paper Mills for larger-scale trial. The Serampore sequel, printed in 1842, gave another verdict. Heavy bleaching was required. The bark did not promise an attractive general paper beside other materials available to the mill. Strength remained its chief virtue. Marshman could imagine a niche such as bank-note paper, where strength might compensate for colour.[^17]

The machine supplied the hardest limit. When Marshman tried to make thicker paper, mucilaginous matter prevented the pulp from running freely. The paper could not be made to the desired thickness on the machine web. Hodgson’s 1832 hand process had allowed the maker to distribute more or less pulp on a floating frame. At Serampore the pulp had to move regularly through machinery “then in vogue.” A hand-controlled variable became a machine-flow problem.

The contrast with 1832 is direct enough to describe without naming a general theory of technology transfer. Hodgson had written that strength could be increased by putting more pulp on the frame. Marshman found that the material would not run freely when he attempted thicker machine paper. The same desired outcome, thicker or stronger paper, met different limits in the two processes.

The 1833 British failure and the 1842 Serampore result therefore form a useful pair. In Britain dried stuff did not reproduce the Nepal-made sheet. At Serampore the material did not fit easily into continuous machine production. Both receiving workshops had the plant material. Neither simply reproduced the hand-made result admired in Nepal.

The editors’ enthusiasm for packing brings the section back to use. They were doubtful about filtering and general machine paper. They saw more promise in wet-season wrapping. Strength and lightness worked there. Waterproof treatment worked there. The material’s value depended on the job more than on resemblance to English writing paper.

By early 1842 government attention had therefore spread across manufacture, bleaching, filtering, wrapping and machine production. At almost the same moment Hodgson was sending a large consignment of pulp toward Calcutta. The surviving letter shows another requirement before any of those experiments could begin: the pulp had to be packed and routed.

## IV. “Twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp”

On 25 March 1842 Hodgson wrote from the Nepal Residency to G. Woodcock, Joint Magistrate of Champaran. A later transcript preserves the letter from the Bihar State Archives register. Hodgson wrote that, “by direction of Mr. Secretary Halliday,” he had sent “twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp” toward Calcutta. His “people” would deliver the packages to Woodcock. Woodcock was to forward them and return a “bill of charges.”[^18]

The count is of packages. Hodgson did not give the mass of pulp. The letter leaves the form inside the packages open. They may have contained bricks or another prepared state. Hodgson’s earlier account had already described dried pulp in brick-like form as a transportable commodity. The 1842 letter proves only what it says: twenty-four packages had been made for this journey.

Package formation mattered because custody had to be transferred. Hodgson’s “people” would bring the consignment to Woodcock. Woodcock would take responsibility for onward movement. A bill would return the cost. The pulp therefore travelled with an expected paper trail of its own.

Hodgson gave Woodcock a choice of routes. The packages could go by land toward Patna. Woodcock was to compare the cost of land carriage from Motihari with water carriage. If the land route proved expensive, he could send the packages from “Govind Gang” directly to Calcutta by “a small safe boat” under a Burkundaz.[^19] The adjective “safe” and the guard are part of the instruction. Cost mattered, but so did custody.

The route was therefore conditional rather than fixed. Hodgson did not order one line from Nepal to Calcutta. He delegated a comparison to the magistrate who knew local carriage. The experiment included a transport decision made after the consignment had left Hodgson’s hands.

The missing papers define the limit of this chain. The initiating direction from “Mr. Secretary Halliday” has not been recovered. Frederick James Halliday is the likely identification, but the order itself would show how the Government described the purpose of the shipment. Woodcock’s bill would show the route actually selected and the freight paid. A terminal receipt would identify the next custodian in Calcutta.

Those gaps are important because the dates tempt a stronger claim. Hodgson’s letter is 25 March 1842. Abdul Ali reports Campbell’s experimental manufactory as closing under existing economic conditions on 16 April. Campbell wrote to G. A. Bushby on 21 April. That paper business entered Political Original Consultation on 11 May as No. 70. A later London Board’s Collection contains a report by Campbell on his experiment with the bark of the paper tree. The events cluster within a few weeks. The present evidence still does not identify Hodgson’s twenty-four packages as Campbell’s or Marshman’s feedstock.

The difference between date and connection is exactly what the archive requires. Campbell’s underlying letter is dated 21 April. Abdul Ali cited it under Political O.C., 11 May 1842, No. 70. The consultation date records government handling rather than Campbell’s act of writing. The 1831 Hodgson letter had already shown the same pattern. An 8 December letter entered a 13 January consultation.[^20]

The Political Consultation could also contain adjacent items belonging to one transaction. The 1937 Imperial Record Department exhibition grouped the 1832 Nepal paper business as Nos. 18–20 rather than isolating the one number later quoted by Abdul Ali. A letter, enclosure, order or reply could occupy different numbers within the same sitting. Retrieval therefore has to follow the group of papers rather than assume that one number contains the whole event.

The 1937 exhibition offers another checkpoint for the 1841 experiment. It displayed O’Shaughnessy’s bleaching report as Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147.[^21] The exhibit demonstrates that the report was still identifiable in government custody almost a century later. It also places Abdul Ali close to the material before his 1944 article. He served as Keeper of the Records of the Government of India and signed the full exhibition list as Secretary to the Indian Historical Records Commission.

Abdul Ali’s “The Daphne Paper of Nepal” later brought these consultation references into historical prose. His citations are valuable because some nineteenth-century originals remain difficult to reach. They also have to be read as citations produced in the twentieth century. The 1937 exhibition and his official role help explain how he could identify the files. They do not make his article interchangeable with the original correspondence.

A London record gives Campbell’s 1842 experiment another setting. British Library IOR/F/4/2006/89528 is a Board’s Collection dated March to November 1842. Its catalogue includes a “report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment in making paper from the bark of the paper tree.” The same file contains Darjeeling business about roads, public works and boundaries.[^22]

The British Library’s description of IOR/F/4 explains why those subjects sit together. Board’s Collections were made from copies of papers extracted from the Proceedings of Indian governments, with previous correspondence where relevant. They were assembled for Board of Control consideration of draft despatches. The Political Collections survive on a scale that allows the series to be treated as an almost complete duplicate of Indian Political Proceedings.[^23]

The word “duplicate” can mislead if it suggests an unchanged second copy. Papers were extracted and regrouped around subjects being considered in London. A Darjeeling paper experiment could therefore appear beside a road because both belonged to the same London packet of Darjeeling business. The technical report kept its words while acquiring different neighbours.

This later file should not yet be equated item for item with Political Consultation, 11 May 1842, No. 70. The overlap is strong: Campbell, Darjeeling, paper-tree manufacture and the same year. A direct contents cross-reference would close the line. The series relation is already secure enough to explain the change of setting.

The London file also changes the likely search strategy. A historian looking only under “paper” could miss a report held under Darjeeling. A historian using only Abdul Ali’s consultation number could miss the later Board’s Collection. The experiment survived because government copied and regrouped its records more than once.

The same point applies to the 1841 bleaching report. It entered a Political Consultation even though its subject was chemistry. The paper trial did not have one disciplinary home. It moved through the offices that were required to act on it. The Political Department could forward sheets to a Chemical Examiner. The Chemical Examiner could send a process to a paper mill. The Post Office could consider waterproof wrapping. The archive reflects those actions imperfectly but often preserves their sequence.

The material consignment of March 1842 therefore belongs beside the documentary sequence without needing to be forced into a single programme. Hodgson’s pulp travelled through packages and magistrates. Campbell’s report travelled through consultation and later London review. Both show paper passing through government, but they preserve different chains.

The bill Hodgson requested from Woodcock is a particularly useful missing item because it would join material and record in one document. It would say what the route cost after the choice between road and river. Until it is recovered, the secure claim remains smaller. Hodgson converted the pulp into twenty-four packages and explicitly made onward movement depend on comparative carriage cost.

Govindgunge again stays within that limited claim. Company records had already treated it as a place where river transport could be costed. Campbell had used it in 1837 paper-market geography. Hodgson used it in 1842 as a possible water-forwarding point. The recurrence explains its practical usefulness. It does not prove one continuous paper route running through all three records.

By the end of 1842, Nepal paper had accumulated a long list of specific experiments without becoming one government industry. Finished sheets had been recommended for records. Pulp had gone to Britain. Samples had entered a learned society. Five makers had worked at Darjeeling. Paper had gone to the Chemical Examiner and Serampore. Waterproof wrapping had interested the Post Office. Another pulp consignment had been packaged for Calcutta. Each operation generated its own files and verdicts.

## Conclusion

Between 1831 and 1842 the phrase “Nepal paper” covered several things that officials themselves kept separating. Hodgson described bark preparation and hand manufacture. He sent finished specimens to government. Swinton sent “Nipal Paper Stuff” to Britain. Campbell calculated the cost of finished sheets. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society requested “each size and quality” for experiment. Five papermakers went to Darjeeling. Bark spoiled in transit. O’Shaughnessy bleached paper. Marshman tried the stuff on a machine. Hodgson later sent twenty-four packages of pulp toward Calcutta. The history becomes clearer when those objects remain distinct.

The judgments remain distinct as well. Finished Nepal-made paper was “exceedingly tough and flexible” and could take a bank-note impression with “perfect fidelity.” Paper remade in Britain was “brittle” and “stiff,” and the trials were “not at all successful.” Campbell valued “wonderful toughness” and found that one whitening treatment destroyed it. Filtering paper could be strong and still work slowly. India-rubber paper could be “perfectly waterproof.” At Serampore the pulp would not run freely on the machine web. Each phrase answers a practical question.

Government interest changed its question repeatedly. In 1831 Hodgson recommended a durable support for records. In 1837 Campbell asked what the paper cost after markets, carriage and season were taken into account. The Society asked for each size and quality. Darjeeling asked whether the process could be reproduced with five makers and local resources. Calcutta asked whether colour could be changed. Serampore asked whether the stuff could enter machine manufacture. The 1842 letter asked which route would carry twenty-four packages most cheaply and safely.

The failures are especially useful because they preserve the actors’ distinctions. Mildew made dried bark “altogether useless for paper-making.” Bleaching separated whiteness from toughness. Filtering separated strength from speed and clarity. Machine forming separated the ability to make a tough hand sheet from the ability of pulp to run freely on a web. The British trial separated transportable raw stuff from the finished Nepal-made sheet. Each failure identified another condition of manufacture or use.

The Darjeeling experiment also shows why moving a process required more than moving a written account. Campbell had Hodgson’s 1832 description. He still needed five makers. Their tool choices differed from the printed account. They rejected iron. They selected ash woods. They insisted on keeping bark dry. Campbell had to provide a site and water. Two Lepchas learned the process through the working manufactory. The process existed in actions that a short printed recipe could only partly preserve.

The archive followed the experiments through equally concrete acts. Hodgson’s 1831 letter became part of a January 1832 Political Consultation. O’Shaughnessy’s report entered a November 1841 consultation. Campbell’s April 1842 letter appeared under a May consultation number. A London Board’s Collection later placed a report on paper-tree manufacture beside other Darjeeling business. The 1937 Imperial Record Department exhibition selected paper files again. These arrangements are part of why the experiments can now be reconstructed and part of why their dates and contexts require checking.

Nepal paper never needed to become one stable colonial technology in order to matter. It mattered because officials repeatedly tried to make it do particular jobs. Hodgson’s strongest recommendation concerned records. Campbell’s Society wanted samples “for experimental purposes.” Darjeeling tried to manufacture and improve the sheet. The Post Office valued low weight and waterproofing. Marshman found a problem at the machine web. Hodgson’s March 1842 letter turned pulp into twenty-four packages and a choice between road and river. The paper changed whenever the question changed.
