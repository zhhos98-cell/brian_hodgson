# Paper after Fibre: Nepal Paper, Material Reclassification, and Administrative Experiment, 1831–1842

## Abstract

This article follows Nepal paper through a sequence of material and administrative experiments between 1831 and 1842. It begins with a simple difficulty. The same broad paper material did different things in different places. Sheets made in the Himalayan hand process were praised as tough, flexible, durable and capable of taking fine impressions. Dried pulp sent to Britain and remade by British manufacturers produced paper described as brittle and stiff. At Darjeeling, efforts to whiten the paper exposed a trade-off between colour and toughness. At Serampore, the material’s mucilaginous behaviour obstructed flow on a machine web. In postal wrapping, by contrast, strength and low weight became advantages. These were not inconsistent judgments on a stable material. They were results produced at different operations and interfaces. The article reconstructs those operations closely: bark drying, ash preparation, sheet formation, specimen circulation, procurement, bleaching, filtering, waterproofing, package formation, route choice and machine production. It also follows the records made around those operations. Letters entered Original Consultations. Proceedings copied them. Board’s Collections later regrouped Indian political papers in London around draft despatches. A report on paper could therefore change documentary neighbourhood as thoroughly as pulp changed material state. Nepal paper provides a compact case in which material history and archival history illuminate one another. Its properties became historically consequential when institutions selected them for particular uses.

## 1. Paper did not travel as a stable set of properties

In November 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson described the manufacture of the paper called “Nipalese” from the Nepal Residency. The account appeared in the first volume of the *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* in January 1832. It is tempting to read the piece as a description of a local craft and then to ask how far that craft travelled. The surviving record supports a more exact question. What travelled? A finished sheet could travel. Bark could travel. Prepared pulp could travel. Dried pulp could be made into bricks for carriage. A written account of the process could travel. Skilled papermakers could travel. A sample could move without the process that made it. A process could move with people and still alter when tools, water, boilers or transport conditions changed. Once these objects are kept separate, “Nepal paper” stops looking like a material with a fixed set of qualities. It becomes a chain of transformations.

Hodgson’s 1832 account already makes this clear. Paper began with inner bark. The bark passed through an alkaline liquor made from ash. It was boiled and beaten. The pulp was diluted. Coarser matter was removed. Sheet formation took place on a floating cloth-bottomed frame. The maker distributed pulp by hand. Water drained away. The sheet was dried by fire, peeled from its support, then folded. Larger sheets could be polished with a conch shell or a smooth hardwood implement. Hodgson remarked that the quantity of pulp placed on the frame could be adjusted according to the strength required. Thickness was therefore made. It was a workshop variable. He also reported that routine sizing was unnecessary for ordinary writing because the surface held ink without the running that European papermakers often controlled through size. Fine smooth sheets could be made at very large dimensions. Kathmandu offered both finished paper and dried pulp in brick-like transportable form. The named commodity already existed in several states before it left the hills.[^1]

The geographical name was equally unstable. Hodgson explained that the paper called “Nipalese” was not principally made in Nepal proper. He located much manufacture among Cis-Himalayan Bhote communities east of the Kali River. Kathmandu was a major market. The name therefore travelled more easily than a secure place of manufacture. A sheet bought at Kathmandu might have been made elsewhere. A dried pulp brick sold through the same market was a different object again. This matters because nineteenth-century administrators repeatedly used the name as if it referred to an intelligible paper class. Their experiments then broke that class apart. They separated finished sheet from pulp. They compared large and small sizes. They sorted paper by quality. They asked whether the same material could be bleached, filtered through, waterproofed, packed, machined or bought cheaply enough for office use. The resulting history is not a story in which an indigenous technology moves into a colonial system. The colonial system keeps making new paper objects by selecting a different stage of the same material process.

The strongest early evidence comes from a failed transfer. Hodgson’s 1832 postscript was optimistic about dried pulp that had gone to England. A report printed by the Asiatic Society in 1833 supplied the results of British manufacturers’ actual trials. George Swinton had sent “Nipal Paper Stuff” home in 1831. British papermakers worked it up. The report called the trials “not at all successful.” Charles Cowan’s specimens lacked the softness and toughness of the Nepal-made paper. They were brittle. They were stiff. They appeared transparent, almost as if varnished. Gritty brown spots remained in the sheet. Whitening proved difficult. The material held water stubbornly on the sieve and shrank markedly in drying. Strong bleaching consumed far more chemistry than ordinary furnish and was thought to have injured the texture.[^2] Here the raw material survived the voyage. The valued paper did not.

The same 1833 report contained its own control experiment. Thin finished sheets made in Nepal had reached Britain earlier. They were described as exceedingly tough and flexible. Their colour was imperfect, but their surface could take the minute impression of a bank-note plate with “perfect fidelity.” The contrast is unusually clean. The fibre source did not change in the simple way a comparative history might assume. The production history did. Nepal-made sheets carried the consequences of local bark preparation, beating, dilution, cloth-frame formation, drainage, drying and finishing. The dried pulp bricks carried another history. They had been prepared for storage and long-distance movement. British papermakers then reprocessed them through a different workshop regime. The resulting sheet was judged through British expectations of whiteness, drainage, softness and finish. The experiment therefore produced a material divergence: similar broad fibre provenance, different process histories, different finished papers.

That divergence gives the rest of the story its structure. “Affordance” is useful here only if it remains close to the operation. Toughness was valuable when a sheet had to survive handling or take a fine impression. The same fibrous or mucilaginous behaviour could impede drainage or machine flow. A dark colour mattered greatly when the desired product was a white writing sheet. Colour mattered far less when the proposed use was wet-season packet wrapping. Strength helped a chemical filter survive twisting. Slow filtration still made the filter perform poorly. Each experiment selected a different property from the sheet. No experiment revealed the paper in itself. It revealed paper in use.

## 2. Durability became an administrative fact

The first government paper episode can be reconstructed at almost the same moment as Hodgson’s manufacturing article. On 8 December 1831 Hodgson sent H. T. Prinsep specimens of Nepal paper for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. Abdul Ali later cited the business under Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, and identified No. 19 as the item from which he drew Hodgson’s argument. A 1937 Imperial Record Department exhibition gives the wider archival packet as Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, Nos. 18–20. The exhibition title is revealing: “Indigenous method of manufacturing paper in the Himalayan tracts (Nepal, etc.) in the early part of the 19th century.”[^3] A manufacturing description, a specimen transfer and an argument about official use were already one administrative transaction.

Hodgson described the paper through qualities that mattered to government. It was cheap. It was available in quantity. It was tough. It was durable. Its strongest prospective use was records. This was more than praise of a local product. The argument linked an observed material quality to the time horizon of bureaucracy. A record support mattered because a government expected the sheet to remain legible after repeated handling and long storage. Durability was therefore not simply a property measured at manufacture. It was produced as evidence over years of use. That point became explicit in Archibald Campbell’s later discussion. Campbell compared old office records written on Nepal paper with English foolscap. He pointed to government records dating from 1817 whose Nepal-paper support remained fresh and serviceable. The archive itself became a longitudinal material test. Old files were proof objects for a new procurement argument.[^4]

This use of the archive is important because it joins two temporalities that are often separated in histories of materials. The past record supplied evidence about how a material aged. The new specimen supplied evidence about what could be purchased now. The administrative office brought them together. A durable old register did not merely preserve information about earlier government. Its surviving support became data about paper. That data could then justify future substitution. The argument had a recursive structure: paper had served the archive; the archive now certified the paper.

By 1837 Campbell had pushed the question into procurement. His correspondence with T. C. Scott, preserved in Abdul Ali’s archival extraction and carried into the Agricultural and Horticultural Society’s paper discussion, treated Nepal paper as a possible substitute for government stationery. Durability alone could no longer decide the matter. Price entered. Market access entered. Freight entered. Season entered. Campbell mapped paper markets toward the plains. He discussed supply beyond the Kathmandu monopoly. He referred to places in the Saran and Tirhut region and included Govindgunge among the relevant market nodes. He estimated carriage from Nepal toward Patna at roughly Rs 1-12 per maund. He proposed annual purchasing during the rains, followed by carriage toward the Ganges during the four cold months.[^5]

The proposal converted a material judgment into a logistical calendar. Paper could be durable and still fail as a government substitute if it could not be aggregated at a workable price. Cheap paper at the point of manufacture could become expensive after carriage. A low unit price did not answer the question of supply during the monsoon. The government therefore had to define the paper through a route. The route had a season. It had markets. It had transfer points. The commodity also had a material state. Campbell’s 1837 calculations concerned finished paper sold and carried as paper. They did not describe the cost of moving pulp. This distinction becomes crucial in 1842, when Hodgson sent twenty-four packages of “Nepalese paper pulp” toward Calcutta. The same broad programme had shifted its object from sheets to raw or semi-prepared material.

Govindgunge makes the infrastructure visible. It appears in Company boat-hire metrology in the 1820s. It appears again in Campbell’s 1837 paper-market geography. In Hodgson’s 1842 letter it becomes a possible water-forwarding point for the pulp consignment. These appearances should stay separate as events. Their overlap still matters. Govindgunge was already legible to Company officials as a destination for calculating river movement. It could also become legible as a node in paper supply. When Hodgson later proposed sending pulp from “Govind Gang” directly to Calcutta by boat, he did not invent a paper route from nothing. He selected from an existing transport geography.

This is where a material history can carry a broader administrative argument without much abstraction. Paper quality was repeatedly translated into another quantity. Durability became expected service life. Finished sheets became prices per ream or per maund. Distance became carriage cost. Monsoon and cold season became procurement windows. A government paper was therefore more than a sheet with desirable physical properties. It was a sheet whose properties could survive the institutions required to buy, move and store it.

## 3. A sample was a classification device

The Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India changed the paper again. On 20 November 1837 the Society asked Campbell for “a small supply of each size and quality of paper manufactured at Nepaul for experimental purposes.” The sentence is unusually precise. The Society wanted a small supply, so the material was bounded as a specimen shipment. It wanted each size. It wanted each quality. Variation was to be preserved. The purpose was experimental. The sample therefore existed as a set before any test took place.[^6]

Campbell’s reply of 28 November shows how quickly the set could acquire new destinations. He promised to transmit a quantity. He suggested that “samples of each variety” should be forwarded to the Chamber of Commerce. He also enclosed copies of correspondence that had recently passed between T. C. Scott and himself “on the subject of this paper, as likely to prove interesting to the Society.” Material samples and documentary descriptions moved together. Their routes were parallel rather than identical. The paper went forward as physical evidence. The Scott-Campbell correspondence entered the Society as a copied dossier. That dossier then became the basis of a published item, “On Nepalese Paper,” in the Society’s *Transactions*.[^7]

The sample request tells us more about experimentation than a later report of success or failure would. “Nepal paper” was too coarse a category for the Society’s purpose. A single token could have demonstrated that the material existed. It could have supplied a curiosity for inspection. The Society asked for “each size and quality” because comparison required internal differentiation. The paper had to be sorted before it could be tested. This is a small but important shift in institutional ontology. A market name became a sample series. Variety became something to preserve rather than noise to eliminate.

The proposed Chamber-of-Commerce route sharpened the shift. The same physical sheet could become an experimental specimen in one institution and a commercial sample in another. Nothing in the sheet had to change for its institutional function to change. What changed was the question asked of it. The learned society might compare qualities. A commercial body might ask price, supply and marketability. Government might ask durability or suitability for records. The sample moved between these questions. Campbell’s correspondence moved with it as an interpretive frame.

The Society’s handling of documents matters for the same reason. Government correspondence was copied out of an administrative exchange and inserted into learned-society circulation. Copying preserved some things and changed others. It removed the correspondence from the immediate sequence of office action. It placed the same words beside experimental specimens and society proceedings. Publication changed the audience again. A paper about paper emerged from papers that had already served government procurement. The documentary transformation therefore mirrored the material one. A sheet changed from commodity candidate to specimen. A letter changed from office action to learned-society evidence.

The 1837 episode also supplies a useful limit. Campbell proposed sending samples to the Chamber of Commerce. The current record shows the proposal. It has yet to show Chamber receipt or testing. That gap matters because the article’s argument depends on preserving the difference between potential and realised circulation. Historical paper moved through plans as well as through roads. A proposed route shaped what actors imagined the material could become. It did not by itself make the transition happen.

## 4. Darjeeling rebuilt a paper process around people, water and observation

The Darjeeling experiment of 1841 was more ambitious than specimen circulation. Lord Auckland had asked Hodgson to send people skilled in Nepal paper manufacture. Hodgson used his position at the Nepal Residency. The Nepal Durbar sent five papermakers at his request. Campbell received them in mid-April. The printed correspondence even gives the wage structure. The head maker received Rs 10 per month. His mate received Rs 8. Three ordinary workers received Rs 8 each. The monthly wage bill came to Rs 42.[^8] Technology moved here as paid skilled labour. It crossed a political boundary through administrative brokerage.

The five makers arrived without a ready factory around them. Campbell lacked the full set of implements. Raw material had to be located. Water had to be secured. A workshop had to be built. Campbell’s stated first objective was information. He wanted to learn whether the paper could be made at Darjeeling and to report the process to Lord Auckland. Lowest-cost production could come later. This priority shaped the physical siting of the manufactory. The makers preferred the paper-tree jungle near Pacheem. Their reasons were practical. Bark was near. Suitable woods for ash were near. Clear running water was available. Carriage would be cheaper. Campbell overrode the preference. He wanted the operation closer to his own observation. He selected a small stream near Auckland Road. A small house was built. A tank was dug. Water reached it through bamboo conduits in what Campbell called the “Lepcha fashion.”[^9]

The decision turns observability into infrastructure. Campbell increased his ability to watch the process by accepting a greater carriage burden for raw materials. Inspection had a transport cost. This matters because colonial experimentation is often described as a movement toward standardisation or efficiency. At Darjeeling, the first arrangement deliberately sacrificed one efficiency so that an administrator could see. The workshop had to be close enough to observe. The bark then had to travel farther.

That movement immediately produced a failure. The papermakers insisted that bark should be thoroughly dried after removal of the outer green layer. Once dry, it could be stored. Rewetting was dangerous. Some bark that had already been dried became wet during carriage from Pacheem to Darjeeling. It mildewed. Campbell reported it “altogether useless for paper-making.”[^10] Manufacture therefore began on the road. Drying was part of the process. So was custody. A transport failure became a manufacturing failure before the bark reached the vat.

Other details show how much of the technique remained in bodies and tools rather than in Hodgson’s printed description. Campbell explicitly used Hodgson’s 1832 paper as a baseline. The makers did some things differently. They beat the material on a flat stone with double-headed hand mallets rather than using the stone mortar and wooden pestle Hodgson had described. They rejected an iron boiler because iron darkened the paper. Tinned copper was preferred. Brass could serve. Water had to flow continuously through the tank so that alkali could be carried away from the pulp. The differences do not disprove Hodgson’s earlier account. They show what a portable description could leave open. A printed recipe gave Campbell a list of operations. The makers supplied a working process under local conditions.

Ash makes the point especially well. Campbell’s correspondence treats ash as a quality-bearing process material. Different woods mattered because poor ash could make dingy paper. Fuel and chemistry were coupled. Wood used in drying could also produce the alkaline ash required in processing. The makers associated particular woods with better results. They even described occasional use of paper-tree sticks as a craft “secret” for improving the ash. Whether the chemistry worked exactly as they claimed is a separate question. The historical point is clear. The manufactory depended on a resource ecology. Bark, water, fuel and ash were linked. A change in site altered several parts of the process at once.

Campbell also tried to stabilise the plant itself as an experimental object. He recorded different kinds of paper tree distinguished partly by flower colour and elevation. He associated them with different paper qualities. Rather than simply repeat the makers’ account, he planted specimens in his garden and proposed using flowering to test the distinctions. He heard of unusually white paper made near Pemiong from another tree and considered bringing in someone who knew the process. The paper project therefore expanded beyond copying a known craft. It became a comparative programme of plant identification, cultivation and technical interviewing.

The production figures show that the experiment reached more than a demonstration scale. Campbell reported 3,300 sheets, weighing 1 maund 7 seers. His calculation put the trial cost at roughly Rs 25-8 per maund. He warned that the figure came from an intentionally expensive experiment rather than a contract-production test. The information goals mattered more. Could good paper be made locally? Could it be made in quantity? Could the art be transferred? Campbell reported that two Lepchas had learned the process. Government then ordered the experiment continued and asked him to retain the necessary makers. The workshop had become a local training site as well as an observation site.[^11]

This sequence changes the meaning of “technology transfer.” Hodgson’s 1832 description travelled as text. Five makers travelled as workers. Their wages travelled through an administrative account. Bark travelled from the resource zone. Water was redirected through bamboo. Tools were substituted. Boilers were rejected or preferred. Lepcha trainees learned by watching and doing. Finished sheets then travelled out by dak. The technique existed across all of these movements. Its history cannot be reduced to a recipe moving from Nepal to Darjeeling.

## 5. Whiteness, filtering and waterproofing selected different papers from the same sheet

The Darjeeling experiment soon became a problem of improvement. Campbell identified colour and smoothness as points on which his paper compared poorly with English paper. He considered finer sieves to remove stringy material. Whitening was harder. His local trials exposed a direct trade-off. Immersion followed by exposure to rain and air made the pulp considerably whiter. It also destroyed the “wonderful toughness” of the untreated material. Spreading pulp on a board and exposing it to the weather preserved toughness but produced little whitening. Campbell abandoned the immersion method. He proposed further exposure trials after the rains.[^12]

The experiment matters because the desired properties could not be added independently. Whiteness was not a neutral improvement laid onto an existing paper. The process that increased whiteness could reduce the very toughness that had made the paper attractive to government. In the British trials of 1833, strong bleaching had also been associated with poorer texture. The same conflict reappeared at Darjeeling because colour and strength were effects of one fibrous material history. Officials discovered the bundle by trying to separate its parts.

Government then distributed the problem. Campbell’s finished paper travelled to Calcutta. The correspondence records requests for twenty or thirty sheets by dak in several despatches for W. B. O’Shaughnessy, the Chemical Examiner. The sheet became a mobile experimental token. Darjeeling produced it. Government offices redirected it. O’Shaughnessy treated it. Bleached specimens could then be returned for inspection. The process itself travelled onward as a formula. In November 1841 the Political Department registered the experiment under Political Consultation No. 147. The Imperial Record Department later described the item more precisely in its 1937 exhibition: “Report on the method of bleaching paper manufactured at Darjeeling by Dr. W. B. O’Shaughnessy, M.D., Chemical Examiner of Calcutta.”[^13]

O’Shaughnessy changed the chemistry. He favoured chlorine dissolved in water rather than chloride of lime. His explanation was operational. Lime combined with the colouring matter of the Darjeeling paper in a way that made bleaching difficult. Chlorine solution acted more effectively. The published dossier records red lead, salt and sulphuric acid among the process inputs. It also records the practical availability of reagents in the Calcutta bazaar. A paper-improvement problem had therefore become a chemical supply problem. The material moved into another expert system.

The same sheet was being tested for functions in which whiteness had very different weights. Government considered Darjeeling paper as a substitute for English filtering paper. Imported filtering paper cost about Rs 9 per ream. The Darjeeling product was understood to be producible at around Rs 3 per ream for this purpose. Mechanical strength looked promising. A strong filter could be pressed or twisted without tearing. Actual use complicated the comparison. The Society’s editors reported that filtration was slow and that the resulting liquor could be less clear than with good European filtering paper.[^14] Strength survived the operation. The filter still performed poorly on other criteria.

Waterproof postal wrapping reversed the ranking. Darjeeling paper treated with India rubber was reported “perfectly waterproof.” The prepared paper weighed about one-third as much as the same area of wax cloth used by the Government Post Office. Post Office authorities considered substitution. Here dingy colour had little cost. Strength mattered. Low weight mattered. Waterproofness mattered. The paper’s value increased because the operation selected properties that the material already had or could acquire without needing to become an English-looking white sheet.[^15]

This is the point at which the paper’s history becomes more explanatory than a general language of “superiority” or “inferiority.” The same material could be poor filtering paper and promising postal wrapping. It could be strong writing support yet resist whitening. It could take fine impressions yet perform badly when remade from dried pulp. These judgments do not cancel one another. Each describes a different operation. Historical actors were effectively decomposing the paper into property bundles by moving it through uses.

The O’Shaughnessy report also changed the spatial organisation of the experiment. Campbell’s failure at Darjeeling generated chemical troubleshooting at Calcutta. O’Shaughnessy then sent his process to Mr Marshman at the Serampore Paper Mills for practical testing on a larger scale. The experimental chain became Darjeeling workshop, Calcutta chemical expertise, Serampore industrial machinery. A failed local treatment did not end the programme. It created a new handoff.

## 6. At Serampore, toughness became a machine problem

The Serampore sequel printed in 1842 supplies the clearest interface test in the whole paper history. Marshman tried to adapt the paper-tree material to mechanised production. The bark required heavy bleaching effort relative to ordinary rag materials. Treatments analogous to English paper practice did not yield an attractive general paper. Strength remained the main virtue. Marshman could imagine a niche such as bank-note paper, where strength might outweigh colour. General economic use looked poor beside alternative materials available to the mill.[^16]

The decisive failure came at the machine web. When Marshman tried to make thicker paper, mucilaginous material prevented the pulp from running freely. Thickness control failed. The same broad material system that made a remarkably tough hand-formed sheet was difficult to manage on machinery “then in vogue.” This is an affordance reversal in the strictest sense. Nothing needs to be called intrinsically good or bad. A fibrous or mucilaginous behaviour contributed to one kind of sheet performance. The same behaviour obstructed another production interface.

The contrast with Hodgson’s 1832 floating frame is sharp. In the local process, a maker could distribute pulp manually over a cloth-bottomed frame. More or less pulp could be used according to the strength required. Drainage occurred through the hand-forming support. At Serampore, a continuous web demanded free flow and regular distribution under machine conditions. The material had to behave in time with the apparatus. What had been a manageable variable in hand sheet formation became a flow problem.

The editors’ comments on filtering and packing make the reversal even clearer. They doubted broad claims for Nepal paper as a filter because filtration was slow. They were more enthusiastic about packing, especially wet-season dak or bhangy parcels. Strength and lightness mattered there. Colour did not. Waterproof treatment could further improve the use. The material found its strongest modern-looking application in a function that required neither fine writing surface nor machine-web conformity. It worked as infrastructure for the movement of other things.

The 1833 British failure and the 1842 Serampore failure therefore form a pair. In Britain, dried pulp did not reproduce the valued Nepal-made sheet. At Serampore, the paper-tree material did not easily fit continuous machine production. Both experiments show why raw-material transfer is an incomplete description of technology transfer. The process that made the sheet had to be transferred too. Even then, the receiving interface could demand a different material behaviour. Paper properties were histories of conversion.

## 7. Twenty-four packages made pulp into a route decision

On 25 March 1842 Hodgson wrote from the Nepal Residency to G. Woodcock, Joint Magistrate of Champaran. The letter survives through a Bihar State Archives register and a later published transcript. Hodgson said that, “by direction of Mr. Secretary Halliday,” he had sent “twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp” en route to Calcutta. His “people” would deliver the packages to Woodcock. Woodcock was to forward them and return a “bill of charges.”[^17]

The wording changes the scale of the paper experiment. The object is neither finished paper nor a few specimens. It is pulp divided into twenty-four transport packages. The count refers to containers. It does not supply material mass. The packages could contain bricks, cakes or some other prepared pulp state. Their exact internal form remains open. The letter still gives a material fact that paper histories often skip: experimental raw material had to become a package before it could become a distant experimental input.

The route was also open. Hodgson proposed a land path toward Patna, with the packages going into the Magistrate’s care for further disposal. He asked Woodcock to compare the cost of land carriage between Motihari and Patna with water carriage. If land carriage looked expensive, Woodcock could send the packages directly from “Govind Gang” to Calcutta by “a small safe boat” under a Burkundaz. The route therefore depended on relative freight cost. Security entered through the guarded boat. Custody entered through the handoff to Woodcock. Accounting returned through the bill.[^18]

Material movement generated a documentary counter-movement. The pulp travelled outward from the Residency. The cost account was supposed to travel back. This is the same kind of logistical paper work visible elsewhere in Hodgson’s manuscript production, where paper purchase, copying, binding, packing, porterage and shipment produced bills and receipts. Here the object was experimental pulp. The administrative logic was familiar. A material route had to be converted into a cost record.

The identity of “Mr. Secretary Halliday” is very likely Frederick James Halliday, Secretary to the Government of Bengal. The missing initiating order matters because it would reveal the administrative framing of the shipment. The Woodcock bill would tell us which route was actually chosen and what the movement cost. The terminal receipt would tell us who took custody in Calcutta. Those documents remain to be recovered. Their absence should shape the prose. The secure chain ends at Calcutta as destination. It does not yet extend to Serampore.

The temptation to join the twenty-four packages directly to Campbell’s Darjeeling experiment is strong. The dates cluster. Hodgson sent pulp on 25 March. Campbell’s experimental factory was reported as closing under existing economic conditions on 16 April. Campbell wrote to G. A. Bushby on 21 April. That paper business entered Political Consultation No. 70 on 11 May. A London Board’s Collection for Darjeeling later preserved a report on Campbell’s paper-tree experiment. Yet temporal density is not consignment identity. Campbell’s workshop was at Darjeeling. Hodgson’s packages were routed toward Calcutta. The source has not yet named Campbell, O’Shaughnessy, Marshman or Serampore as their final receiver.

The open edge is analytically useful because it keeps material history honest. Programmes can contain multiple batches. Administrators can run overlapping experiments. One consignment can answer a different question from another. The twenty-four packages still belong in the same paper history because they show a government willing to move Nepalese pulp at scale, compare routes and pay freight. They should not be converted into the feedstock of a specific test until a receipt or forwarding record makes that connection.

Govindgunge again illustrates the difference between infrastructure and event. Its appearance in 1825 boat-hire rates shows a metrised transport node. Campbell’s 1837 market geography gives it a paper-supply function. Hodgson’s 1842 letter gives it a possible forwarding function for this exact consignment. The three records do not create one continuous route. Together they show why the place could repeatedly become useful. Market geography and transport geography overlapped there. That overlap made paper movement calculable.

## 8. The documents were remade too

The paper experiments survive through a documentary system that transformed records almost as aggressively as workshops transformed bark. Abdul Ali’s citations initially look opaque because the date attached to a Political “O.C.” can differ from the date of the underlying letter. The 1831 example makes this visible. Hodgson wrote to Prinsep on 8 December. The paper business entered Political Consultation on 13 January 1832. Campbell wrote to Bushby on 21 April 1842. Abdul Ali cited that letter under Political O.C., 11 May 1842, No. 70. The archival date records a later administrative act.[^19]

Pre-Mutiny archival handbooks explain the structure. In this record system, O.C. means Original Consultation. Original incoming papers could be retained with orders and reply drafts or copies under the date on which the business was considered. Items were numbered within that consultation. Proceedings supplied another documentary state. They copied or narrated the business in register form. Enclosures could disappear from the Proceedings even when they survived with the originals. The date and number therefore form a composite archival address rather than a simple letter date.[^20]

The 1937 exhibition confirms the transaction structure. The Nepal paper-manufacture business of January 1832 was displayed as Nos. 18–20, not as No. 19 alone. Abdul Ali had cited No. 19 because that item supplied a passage he wanted. The archive treated three adjacent numbers as the paper-manufacture cluster. An incoming letter, an enclosure, an order or a reply could occupy separate numbers. Retrieval must therefore follow the transaction rather than a single citation.

The same exhibition provides a survival checkpoint for the 1841 bleaching report. The Imperial Record Department displayed Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147 as O’Shaughnessy’s report on bleaching Darjeeling paper. A. F. M. Abdul Ali signed the complete exhibition list as Secretary to the Indian Historical Records Commission while serving as Keeper of the Records of the Government of India. Seven years later he published “The Daphne Paper of Nepal” and cited the same consultation family.[^21] The relation should be stated carefully. The exhibition proves that the record was identifiable in archive custody in 1937. It places Abdul Ali directly in the curatorial environment of the material. It does not prove that his 1944 prose was copied from exhibition notes.

The 1842 Campbell experiment has an additional London life. British Library file IOR/F/4/2006/89528 is a Board’s Collection dated March to November 1842. Its catalogue scope includes a “report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment in making paper from the bark of the paper tree.” The same file also contains Darjeeling business about boundary marks, Birch Hill road and public works.[^22] This is a striking documentary relocation. A paper experiment that could be read in India as a manufacturing or chemical problem entered London in a broader Darjeeling administrative packet.

The series architecture explains why. The British Library describes Board’s Collections as copies of papers on a given subject extracted from the Proceedings of Indian governments and assembled for Board of Control review of draft overseas despatches. Almost all Political Collections survive. The catalogue says they may be treated as a virtually complete duplicate of the Indian Political Proceedings in IOR/P.[^23] “Duplicate” here does not mean unchanged facsimile. The papers were selected by subject and regrouped around London draft-despatch work. Documentary neighbourhood changed.

That change matters for historical interpretation. Campbell’s report can sit beside road works and boundary administration because the London packet organised Darjeeling as a subject of governance. The paper experiment acquired a metropolitan administrative context that differed from the dated Indian consultation. A historian searching only for “paper” can miss it because locality has become the stronger archival heading. A historian searching only the original consultation can miss later London uses of the same report. The record’s survival is therefore a derivative history.

The exact item-level relation between Political Consultation 11 May 1842 No. 70 and Board’s Collection 89528 remains to be controlled. The overlap is very strong. Campbell is the officer. Darjeeling is the place. The year is 1842. Both records concern his paper-manufacture experiment at the result or evaluation stage. A direct cross-reference, contents list or image would close the line. The series-level relation is already secure: Political Board’s Collections were made by extracting and regrouping Indian Proceedings. The remaining task is to show which consultation items supplied this file.

This documentary history changes the article’s use of evidence. A surviving derivative can preserve an event after an original packet becomes difficult to locate. It can also delete context. An abstract may preserve subject and date while dropping an enclosure. A Board’s Collection may preserve the enclosure while changing its neighbours. Publication may quote the technical result and omit the transmission metadata. An exhibition may reclassify a political transaction as “indigenous method of manufacturing paper.” Abdul Ali’s article may then turn the archive object into a historical narrative. Each state keeps something. Each state makes a different paper history possible.

The object and the record medium finally converge. This article studies paper as bark, pulp, sheet, specimen and package. The evidence for those states also survived as paper objects. Letters were folded, copied, registered, extracted, rebound and catalogued. The material being investigated and the material that made investigation administratively possible were the same broad technology. Paper carried information about paper while undergoing its own changes of format and custody.

## Conclusion

Between 1831 and 1842 Nepal paper became several different administrative materials. Hodgson’s manufacturing account described a local process in which bark, ash, beating, cloth-frame formation and drying made a tough sheet. British manufacturers then showed that dried pulp could cross the ocean without carrying the paper’s valued properties with it. Their remade sheets were brittle and stiff. The process history had been lost or replaced. Campbell’s later procurement scheme translated durability into price, route and season. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society turned internal variation into a sample set. Darjeeling rebuilt production through skilled workers, new tools, water infrastructure and administrative observation. Bleaching made whiteness compete with toughness. Filtering exposed a conflict between mechanical strength and liquid performance. India-rubber treatment made the same paper attractive as light waterproof wrapping. Serampore machinery turned mucilaginous strength into a flow problem. Hodgson’s twenty-four packages of pulp made paper into a freight calculation before any later test could begin.

These episodes make a material argument through their differences. Paper properties were real. Historical actors handled them constantly. Those properties became useful only inside operations that selected some and ignored others. Toughness could support records, bank-note impressions, filters or wrapping. Each use demanded something else as well. Whiteness mattered for some writing papers and scarcely mattered for wet-season packing. Low mass became valuable in postal transport. Free flow mattered on a machine web. Durability mattered only over time. The history therefore lies in the relation between a material state and an interface.

The same principle applies to the archive. An underlying letter and a Political Consultation are related records with different dates. Proceedings make another version. Board’s Collections regroup the papers around London policy work. A later exhibition makes a technological object out of an old political file. A historical article makes evidence out of the exhibition’s archive. Documentary transformation did not merely preserve the material history. It helped determine which material histories could later be seen.

Nepal paper is therefore useful precisely because the experiments failed unevenly. Failure made hidden requirements visible. Mildew showed that bark transport belonged to manufacture. Bleaching showed that colour and toughness could pull against one another. Filtering showed that strength alone could not guarantee performance. Machine forming showed that a hand-paper virtue could become an industrial defect. Route choice showed that pulp had to become a costed transport object before it could become experimental feedstock. Those failures prevent the paper from being reduced to an indigenous craft, a colonial commodity or a successful technology transfer. They reveal a sequence of material reclassifications. Each institution made another paper from the same broad material system by asking it to do something different.
