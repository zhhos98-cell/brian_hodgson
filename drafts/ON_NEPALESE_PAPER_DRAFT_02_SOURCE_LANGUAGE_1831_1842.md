# “On Nepalese Paper”
## Manufacture, Experiment, and Government Use, 1831–1842

## Abstract

Between 1831 and 1842 Nepal paper passed through a series of experiments in manufacture and government use. Brian Houghton Hodgson described its manufacture and sent specimens for official consideration. British papermakers later found imported “Nipal Paper Stuff” difficult to remake, while finished Nepal-made sheets were praised as tough and capable of taking a bank-note impression with “perfect fidelity.” Archibald Campbell then treated the paper as a possible government supply. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society asked for “each size and quality” for experiment. At Darjeeling, five papermakers from Nepal worked with Campbell on bark, ash, water, tools, bleaching, filtering and waterproofing. In March 1842 Hodgson sent “twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp” toward Calcutta, with the route left to comparative freight cost. The records of these episodes also changed form. Letters entered Political Consultations. Proceedings copied them. Board’s Collections regrouped Indian papers in London around the review of draft despatches. Following the words used by Hodgson, Campbell, manufacturers and officials shows why “Nepal paper” could mean a finished sheet in one exchange, pulp in another, a government record support in another and an experimental material somewhere else.

## I. “Not at all successful”

In November 1831 Brian Houghton Hodgson sent the Asiatic Society of Bengal an account of the paper commonly called “Nipalese.” It appeared in January 1832 under the title “On the Native Method of making the Paper, denominated in Hindustan, Nipalese.” Hodgson began with the plant and the manufacture. The inner bark was boiled in an alkaline liquor prepared from ash. It was beaten into pulp. The pulp was diluted and cleared of coarser matter. A maker then spread it by hand on a floating cloth-bottomed frame. Water drained through the cloth. The wet sheet was dried by heat and removed from the support. Larger sheets could be polished with a conch shell or smooth hardwood. Hodgson also remarked that the maker could vary the quantity of pulp according to the strength required.[^1]

The description already distinguished several things that later correspondence often gathered under one geographical name. Finished sheets were sold at Kathmandu. Dried pulp could also be prepared for transport. Hodgson described the pulp in compact brick-like form. He further warned that the paper called “Nipalese” was largely made beyond Nepal proper, among communities east of the Kali River, and brought into Kathmandu as a market. A “Nipalese” sheet could therefore carry a market name that said little about its exact place of manufacture. Pulp sold in the same market was a different article again.

The distinction became important almost immediately. Hodgson’s postscript mentioned material that had already gone to Britain. In 1833 the Asiatic Society printed a report on British attempts to work up “Nipal Paper Stuff” sent by George Swinton in 1831. The manufacturers’ verdict was severe. The trials were “not at all successful.” Paper made from the imported stuff lacked the softness and toughness admired in Nepal-made sheets. Charles Cowan’s specimens were described as brittle and stiff. Some looked nearly varnished. Gritty brown matter remained visible. The pulp retained water on the sieve and shrank heavily in drying. Strong bleaching demanded much more chemical treatment than ordinary furnish, with damage to the texture.[^2]

The same report placed finished Nepal-made paper beside the failed British remaking. Thin sheets already made in the Himalayan process were called exceedingly tough and flexible. Their colour drew criticism. Their surface received minute engraved detail very well. A bank-note plate, the report said, could be printed on the material with “perfect fidelity.” The comparison depended on two different objects. One had crossed the sea as finished paper. The other had crossed as prepared stuff and was made again in British workshops. The British report therefore compared processes as much as fibres.

Hodgson’s account helps explain the difference. In his description, thickness depended on the amount of pulp placed on the frame. A maker could manage distribution by hand. Drainage occurred through a cloth support. Drying and finishing followed in the same workshop sequence. The British manufacturers received a compacted material prepared for carriage. They subjected it to another sequence of soaking, cleaning, bleaching and sheet formation. Their difficulty with drainage belonged to that second workshop. So did the demand for a whiter sheet.

The 1833 report also makes the language of quality very concrete. “Tough” described something the finished Nepal-made sheet did under handling. “Flexible” described another quality of the same sheet. “Brittle” and “stiff” described Cowan’s remade specimens. “Perfect fidelity” referred to printing. None of these terms stood alone. Each came from a trial. The report praised finished paper for one use while rejecting remade pulp as a general paper furnish.

Government interest in the material had begun alongside these experiments. On 8 December 1831 Hodgson wrote to H. T. Prinsep and sent specimens for presentation to Lord William Bentinck. Abdul Ali later cited the business under Political Consultation, 13 January 1832. He identified No. 19 as the paper from which he drew Hodgson’s remarks. A 1937 Imperial Record Department exhibition grouped the transaction more broadly as Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, Nos. 18–20. Its title described an “Indigenous method of manufacturing paper in the Himalayan tracts (Nepal, etc.) in the early part of the 19th century.”[^3]

Hodgson’s argument to government used a different set of words from the British manufacturers. The paper was cheap. It was available in quantity. It was tough. It was durable. He placed special weight on official records. Durability mattered because the sheet would be handled and stored. A government record offered a long test that no short workshop experiment could reproduce.

Campbell later made this point explicit. He compared old records written on Nepal paper with English foolscap and referred to government records dating from 1817 whose paper remained fresh and serviceable. The files themselves supplied evidence. Paper that had already spent years in an office could be inspected for wear. Old government writing became part of a new argument about what government should buy.[^4]

This early sequence prevents “Nepal paper” from settling into one stable object. Hodgson described a manufacture. Bentinck received specimens. British papermakers received “Paper Stuff.” Government officers inspected old sheets in use. Each exchange selected another state of the material. The vocabulary changed with it. Toughness meant one thing under handling, another in printing and another when a record had survived twenty years in an office.

## II. “Each size and quality … for experimental purposes”

By 1837 Archibald Campbell was asking whether Nepal paper could enter regular government supply. Durability remained attractive. Price now mattered. So did the place of purchase. Campbell discussed markets beyond the Kathmandu monopoly and described routes toward the plains. He referred to the Saran and Tirhut region and included Govindgunge among the relevant points. He estimated carriage toward Patna and proposed a seasonal cycle in which paper would be purchased during the rains and moved toward the Ganges during the colder months.[^5]

The argument was practical. A cheap sheet in the hills could become expensive after carriage. A durable sheet could still fail as government stationery if supply remained irregular. The season shaped when transport could take place. Campbell therefore discussed finished paper through prices, markets and carriage. His calculations concerned paper sold and moved as paper. Five years later Hodgson would write about packages of pulp. The distinction between finished sheets and raw or semi-prepared material remained important.

The Agricultural and Horticultural Society of India then asked for a different kind of supply. On 20 November 1837 the Society requested “a small supply of each size and quality of paper manufactured at Nepaul for experimental purposes.”[^6] The sentence deserves to be read literally. The Society wanted a small supply. It wanted each size. It wanted each quality. Experiment began with variety rather than with a single representative sheet.

Campbell answered on 28 November. He promised a quantity of paper. He also proposed that “samples of each variety” should go to the Chamber of Commerce. With the paper he sent copies of correspondence between T. C. Scott and himself “on the subject of this paper, as likely to prove interesting to the Society.” The physical samples and the copied letters therefore entered the Society together. The letters explained price, supply and official interest. The sheets allowed inspection. The Society later published the correspondence as “On Nepalese Paper.”[^7]

The request for “each size and quality” changed the way the paper was handled. A market name had to be divided into specimens. One sheet could show that Nepal paper existed. A set could show variation. Size became something to compare. Quality became something to compare. The Society’s wording kept those differences visible before anyone decided which sheet was best.

Campbell’s suggestion concerning the Chamber of Commerce points to another use for the same sample set. In the Society the sheets were experimental objects. In a commercial body they could be examined as saleable paper. Campbell’s letter shows the proposed route. The present evidence stops short of a Chamber receipt or test. The proposal still shows how easily one parcel of samples could be expected to answer several questions.

Government correspondence changed at the same time. The Scott-Campbell exchange began as office business. Campbell sent copies to the Society. The Society published them. The words therefore reached a new audience through copying rather than through the original file. The paper and the paperwork both moved, although by different routes.

Campbell’s supply discussion also returned to durability. Old Nepal-paper records were still valuable as evidence because they showed what happened after years of use. New samples showed what the market could provide. The Society’s request added a third form of comparison by asking for several sizes and qualities at once. The problem had moved beyond praise of a single local product. Officials and learned societies were asking which paper, in which form, at which price, for which use.

This question became sharper when manufacture itself was moved to Darjeeling. Lord Auckland asked Hodgson to send people who knew the process. Hodgson used his position at the Nepal Residency. The Nepal Durbar sent five papermakers. Campbell received them in April 1841. The head maker received Rs 10 per month. His mate received Rs 8. Three other workers received Rs 8 each. The monthly wage bill was Rs 42.[^8]

The figures matter because this was a transfer of skilled labour, not simply another shipment of samples. The men arrived with knowledge that Hodgson’s printed description could only partly record. Campbell had to build a place in which their work could be watched. Raw bark had to be found. Water had to run continuously. Tools and boilers had to be chosen. The movement of a paper-making process therefore began with five paid workers and a new local workshop.

## III. “Altogether useless for paper-making”

Campbell first wanted to know whether good paper could be made at Darjeeling. The papermakers preferred a site near the paper-tree jungle at Pacheem. Bark was close there. Wood suitable for ash was also close. Clear running water was available. Carriage would be cheaper. Campbell chose a site nearer his own observation. He placed the workshop by a stream near Auckland Road. A small house was built. A tank was dug. Bamboo conduits carried water in what Campbell called the “Lepcha fashion.”[^9]

The choice imposed a cost immediately. Bark had to travel farther. The papermakers said the bark should be dried after the outer green layer was removed. Once dry it could be stored. Some bark that had already been dried became wet during carriage from Pacheem. It mildewed. Campbell reported that it was “altogether useless for paper-making.”[^10] A road accident had entered the manufacturing process before the bark reached the vat.

Campbell used Hodgson’s 1832 account as a guide and also recorded differences in practice. The papermakers beat material on a flat stone with double-headed hand mallets. Hodgson had described a stone mortar and wooden pestle. The workers rejected iron for the boiler because they believed it darkened the paper. Tinned copper was preferred. Brass could serve. Water had to pass continuously through the tank so that alkali washed away from the pulp.

Ash received similar attention. Campbell reported that different woods gave different results. Poor ash could leave the paper dingy. The papermakers associated certain woods with better work and described occasional use of the paper tree itself in preparing ash. Campbell recorded the claim as a craft “secret.” Whether every explanation was chemically exact mattered less to his immediate purpose than the fact that the makers distinguished materials closely. Bark, ash and water were parts of one practice.

The five workers also changed Campbell’s understanding of the paper tree. He recorded different varieties associated with elevation and flower colour. He connected these distinctions to paper quality and planted specimens in his garden so that flowering might test them. He heard of unusually white paper near Pemiong and considered bringing in someone acquainted with that manufacture. The paper experiment therefore led into plant comparison and local testimony without leaving the immediate question of manufacture.

Campbell produced enough paper to calculate cost. He reported 3,300 sheets weighing 1 maund 7 seers. The experimental cost came to about Rs 25-8 per maund. He warned that the figure reflected an intentionally expensive trial. The first aim had been to learn the process. Government then ordered the experiment continued and asked him to retain the makers needed for it. Campbell also reported that two Lepchas had learned the art.[^11]

The training is easy to miss if the experiment is read only as a calculation of cost. Hodgson’s description had travelled as print. Five papermakers had travelled as workers. Campbell’s workshop produced new sheets. Two Lepchas learned the process. The same manufacture therefore passed through text, labour and demonstration. Each form carried something different.

Colour soon became the next problem. Campbell thought Darjeeling paper needed improvement in whiteness and smoothness. He experimented with exposure to weather. Immersion followed by rain and air made the pulp much whiter. It also destroyed the “wonderful toughness” of the untreated material. Spreading pulp on a board preserved toughness better but did little for colour. Campbell abandoned the immersion method and planned further trials.[^12]

The earlier British report makes this tension familiar. Strong bleaching had also been associated with poorer texture in the 1833 remaking of imported paper stuff. At Darjeeling the same problem appeared within a local experiment. A whiter sheet could be obtained, but the process threatened the quality officials valued most.

Campbell’s paper then travelled to Calcutta for chemical work. Government correspondence records requests for sheets to be sent by dak to W. B. O’Shaughnessy, the Chemical Examiner. In November 1841 the Political Department registered his work under Political Consultation No. 147. The Imperial Record Department later described the item as a “Report on the method of bleaching paper manufactured at Darjeeling by Dr. W. B. O’Shaughnessy, M.D., Chemical Examiner of Calcutta.”[^13]

O’Shaughnessy preferred chlorine dissolved in water. He objected to chloride of lime for this material because lime combined with colouring matter and complicated bleaching. The published correspondence records red lead, salt and sulphuric acid among the materials used. It also mentions the availability of reagents in the Calcutta bazaar. A paper problem had entered the Chemical Examiner’s office and acquired another vocabulary.

The same Darjeeling sheet was being tested for uses in which colour mattered much less. Government considered it as filtering paper. Imported filtering paper cost about Rs 9 per ream. Darjeeling paper was expected to cost around Rs 3 per ream for this purpose. Its strength seemed useful because a filter might be pressed or twisted without tearing. Actual tests were less encouraging. Filtration was slow and the resulting liquid could be less clear than with good European filtering paper.[^14]

Waterproof wrapping gave another result. Darjeeling paper treated with India rubber was reported “perfectly waterproof.” The prepared paper weighed about one-third as much as an equal area of wax cloth used by the Government Post Office. Here colour mattered little. Strength mattered greatly. Weight also mattered. Post Office authorities considered substitution.[^15]

The same sheet could therefore receive opposite judgments without inconsistency. A strong paper could still be a slow filter. A dark paper could still be excellent wrapping. A bleaching process could improve colour while damaging toughness. The officers themselves supplied the distinctions. Their reports evaluated particular uses rather than “Nepal paper” in the abstract.

O’Shaughnessy forwarded the question to Mr Marshman at the Serampore Paper Mills. Marshman tried the material under machine conditions. Heavy bleaching remained necessary. General paper production looked uneconomic beside other materials available to the mill. Strength still suggested a possible special use such as bank-note paper.[^16]

The machine produced the sharpest difficulty. Mucilaginous matter hindered the flow of pulp. Thicker paper was especially hard to make because the furnish would not run freely on the machinery then in use. Hodgson’s hand process had allowed a maker to place more or less pulp on a floating frame. Serampore required a regular moving web. The material had to pass through that apparatus at a controlled rate. The mill therefore encountered a problem that the hand maker did not face in the same way.

The contrast with postal wrapping is useful because both judgments came from the same period. The material looked poor when asked to behave like a machine furnish. It looked promising when asked to protect a parcel in wet weather. The paper found no single rank on a scale from inferior to superior. Officials kept assigning it to different tasks and recording the result.

## IV. “Twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp”

On 25 March 1842 Hodgson wrote from the Nepal Residency to G. Woodcock, Joint Magistrate of Champaran. The letter survives through a Bihar State Archives register and a later printed transcript. Hodgson wrote that, “by direction of Mr. Secretary Halliday,” he had sent “twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp” toward Calcutta. His “people” would deliver the packages to Woodcock. Woodcock was to forward them and return a “bill of charges.”[^17]

The count referred to packages. Hodgson gave no total weight. The letter calls their contents “paper pulp” and leaves the internal form undescribed. The material may have been compacted for carriage, as Hodgson’s earlier manufacturing account had described, but this letter does not say so. What it does show securely is a shipment divided into twenty-four units and directed through an official handoff.

The route remained open. Hodgson proposed carriage toward Patna by land. He asked Woodcock to compare the expense with water carriage. If the land charge between Motihari and Patna proved high, Woodcock could send the packages from “Govind Gang” directly to Calcutta in “a small safe boat” under a Burkundaz. Freight would decide the route. Security entered through the guard. The bill of charges would record the expense.[^18]

The outward movement therefore called for a return document. Pulp left the Residency. A cost account was expected back. This kind of exchange is familiar from Hodgson’s other work, where copying, packing and porterage produced their own bills. In this case the material was paper pulp rather than manuscripts or specimens. The accounting procedure remained recognizable.

Govindgunge had already appeared in Campbell’s paper geography of 1837. Earlier Company records also treated it as a place for calculating boat hire. These appearances should stay separate. In 1842 the place had a specific role in Hodgson’s letter: it was one possible point from which the twenty-four packages could go by water to Calcutta. The route was proposed because a price comparison might make it cheaper.

Several documents that would complete the movement remain outside the recovered chain. Halliday’s initiating instruction has yet to be located. So has Woodcock’s bill. The actual route choice is therefore unknown. A terminal receipt in Calcutta would identify the next custodian. None is needed to establish what the letter itself shows. Government was willing to move Nepalese pulp in twenty-four packages and to choose a route by freight cost.

The date places Hodgson’s shipment close to other paper business. Campbell’s Darjeeling experiment was being evaluated in the same spring. He wrote to G. A. Bushby on 21 April. That business entered Political Consultation on 11 May 1842 as No. 70. A London Board’s Collection from the same year later included a “report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment in making paper from the bark of the paper tree.” Temporal proximity encourages comparison, but the present evidence leaves the twenty-four packages on a route to Calcutta. It does not name Serampore or Campbell’s Darjeeling workshop as their destination.

The shipment nevertheless changes the scale of the story. In 1831 Hodgson had sent specimens. British manufacturers had tested paper stuff. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society later asked for small supplies of each size and quality. The March 1842 letter records twenty-four transport packages. Experimental material had become a freight problem before it could become anything else.

The same point appears in Campbell’s earlier procurement proposals. Finished paper had to be priced after carriage. Season affected movement. Market location mattered. The 1842 letter repeats these concerns for pulp in a more immediate form. Woodcock had to decide how the packages should move. Hodgson asked for the cost afterward. The paper experiment had reached the magistrate’s office before reaching its final workshop.

This material scale also clarifies the difference between pulp and sheet. Finished paper had already been praised for durability and used as government records. The packages carried material intended for later work. A package could therefore be counted before a sheet existed. The administrative record preserved that count even though it told us little about the quantity of fibre inside each package.

## V. “Report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment”

The paper experiments survive because office records were copied and regrouped repeatedly. The dates can therefore be misleading if each archival address is treated as the date of the underlying event. Hodgson wrote to Prinsep on 8 December 1831. The business appears under Political Consultation, 13 January 1832, Nos. 18–20. Campbell wrote to Bushby on 21 April 1842. Abdul Ali later cited the paper business under Political O.C., 11 May 1842, No. 70.[^19]

Archival handbooks clarify the abbreviation. In this pre-Mutiny context O.C. means Original Consultation. Incoming papers could be retained with orders and drafts or copies of replies under the date on which government considered the business. Items were numbered within the consultation. Proceedings created another record state. They copied or summarized the transaction and might omit an enclosure that survived with the originals.[^20]

The 1832 cluster gives a direct example. Abdul Ali cited No. 19 because it supplied the passage he wanted. The Imperial Record Department exhibition of 1937 grouped the paper-manufacture business as Nos. 18–20. The transaction therefore occupied several adjacent numbers. A search for a single number could recover one document and miss the rest of the business.

The exhibition also supplies a later point in the record’s life. In 1937 the Imperial Record Department displayed the 1832 cluster under an exhibition title about indigenous paper manufacture in the Himalayan tracts. It separately displayed Political Consultation, 29 November 1841, No. 147 as O’Shaughnessy’s bleaching report. A. F. M. Abdul Ali signed the exhibition list as Secretary to the Indian Historical Records Commission while serving as Keeper of the Records of the Government of India. In 1944 he published “The Daphne Paper of Nepal” and cited these record families again.[^21]

Campbell’s 1842 paper experiment also survives in London. British Library file IOR/F/4/2006/89528 covers Darjeeling business from March to November 1842. Its catalogue description includes a “report by Dr Campbell of the result of his experiment in making paper from the bark of the paper tree.” The same file contains other Darjeeling business, including roads and boundary work.[^22]

The British Library’s description of IOR/F/4 explains why these subjects could sit together. Board’s Collections were made from copies of papers extracted from the Proceedings of Indian governments. They were assembled in London for Board of Control review of draft overseas despatches. The Political Collections survive extensively enough that the library describes them as a virtually complete duplicate of the Indian Political Proceedings in IOR/P.[^23]

“Duplicate” here still involved selection. Papers were gathered by subject for London work. Campbell’s experiment could therefore leave an Indian consultation and appear in a broader Darjeeling packet with public works or boundaries. The report remained about paper. Its neighbours changed.

This matters when reconstructing the experiment. A consultation number can preserve the date on which officials in India considered the business. A Board’s Collection can preserve the same or related papers after London clerks regrouped them. A printed society article can preserve technical detail while dropping some office metadata. An exhibition list can identify a consultation that has become difficult to locate. Abdul Ali’s 1944 article can quote a nineteenth-century file through his work in the record office. These sources overlap, yet they answer different questions.

The exact item-level relation between Political Consultation, 11 May 1842, No. 70 and IOR/F/4/2006/89528 still requires a direct cross-reference or image. The broader relation is already clear from the series description. Board’s Collections were made by extracting papers from Indian Proceedings for London review. The Campbell file is therefore part of the same administrative world as the Indian consultation even while the precise document-to-document path remains to be demonstrated.

The records also show why the history of Nepal paper cannot be written from published technical descriptions alone. Hodgson’s 1832 article gives manufacture. The 1833 proceedings give British failure. Campbell’s Society correspondence gives prices, sample requests and Darjeeling practice. Political Consultations reveal government handling. Hodgson’s letter to Woodcock reveals package count and route choice. Board’s Collections reveal how the paper business entered London policy files.

The material itself changed at every step. Bark became pulp. Pulp became a finished sheet. A sheet became a specimen. A specimen could become office paper or filtering paper. Another sheet could become waterproof wrapping. Pulp could be divided into twenty-four packages for carriage. The documents preserve these changes because officials kept naming the object again.

Their words are often more precise than a later general category. Hodgson distinguished finished paper from prepared stuff. The Society asked for “each size and quality.” Campbell called mildewed bark “altogether useless for paper-making.” O’Shaughnessy reported on bleaching paper “manufactured at Darjeeling.” Hodgson counted “twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp.” Marshman described what happened when the material reached a machine. Each phrase fixes the paper at a particular moment.

## Conclusion

The history between 1831 and 1842 begins with Hodgson’s description of manufacture and ends with several different things still carrying the name “Nepal paper.” Finished Himalayan sheets reached Britain and were praised as tough and flexible. Imported paper stuff reached British workshops and produced sheets that were “not at all successful.” Government officers inspected older Nepal-paper records for durability. Campbell priced paper as a possible office supply. The Agricultural and Horticultural Society requested “each size and quality” for experiment.

At Darjeeling five papermakers brought the practice itself into another setting. They selected bark and ash, rejected some tools and worked with running water. Campbell placed the workshop where he could observe it. Bark spoiled during carriage and became “altogether useless for paper-making.” The experiment produced 3,300 sheets and trained two Lepchas. Attempts to whiten the paper damaged the “wonderful toughness” that had helped make it attractive.

Calcutta and Serampore then asked other questions. O’Shaughnessy treated the sheet chemically. Filtering tests valued strength but found the flow slow. India-rubber treatment made the paper “perfectly waterproof” and light enough to interest the Post Office. Marshman found the material awkward on a machine web. These reports give no single verdict on Nepal paper. They show why actors kept specifying the use.

Hodgson’s letter of 25 March 1842 adds another object. “Twenty-four packages of Nepalese paper pulp” had to move through Champaran toward Calcutta. Woodcock could choose land or water after comparing charges. A Burkundaz could accompany the boat. A bill would return the cost. Pulp had become something government could count and route before any later manufacture began.

The archive preserved these experiments through another series of changes. Hodgson’s 8 December 1831 letter became part of a January 1832 Political Consultation. Campbell’s 21 April 1842 paper business appears under a May consultation. Board’s Collections later extracted Indian proceedings into London subject files used in reviewing draft despatches. The 1937 exhibition and Abdul Ali’s 1944 article made still later uses of those files.

Following these records closely makes the argument simple. The name “Nepal paper” remained stable enough for officials to use. The things named by it kept changing. Sometimes the object was a finished sheet. Sometimes it was dried stuff, bark or pulp. Sometimes it was an old government record. Sometimes it was a set of samples classified by size and quality. Sometimes it was a bleached sheet, a filter, waterproof wrapping or a machine furnish. In March 1842 it was twenty-four packages on a route whose price had yet to be decided.

The paper’s qualities changed meaning with those uses. Toughness could be praised in an old record, a printed impression or a postal wrapper. The same material could be troublesome when bleaching or machine flow became the test. Hodgson and Campbell did not need a general theory of materials to see this. Their letters and reports recorded it each time the paper was asked to do something new.
