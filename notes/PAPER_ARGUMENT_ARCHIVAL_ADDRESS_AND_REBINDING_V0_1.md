# Paper argument module — archival address, version-family custody and rebinding v0.1

Date: 2026-09-01

Status: article-facing synthesis through Wave 43.

## Core proposition

Hodgson's papers carried at least three materially distinct kinds of relation:

1. **documentary relation** — source, copy, translation, abstract, table, report;
2. **historical custody relation** — trunk, box, sack, bundle, item number;
3. **modern archival relation** — twentieth-century volume/item/folio shelfmark.

These relations were never guaranteed to coincide.

The strongest archival mechanism now visible is:

`historical version family`
→ `addressed/co-bundled custody family`
→ `1921 physical-format rebinding`
→ `modern shelfmark dispersion`
→ `reconstruction from surviving old identifiers`.

Paper participates at every stage.

---

## 1. Paper as archival address surface

The Hodgson archive preserves exact or partial old addresses directly on paper objects.

Examples:

- `Sack 8` contents control now in `vol.58/18 f.65`;
- a member manuscript still labelled `original sack number (8)` now in `vol.85/4`;
- `Box 3` contents page now in `vol.55/20 f.129v`;
- `Box 2 / Item 12` preserved in the Nepali title layer of modern `vol.6`;
- original box/sack number on imported-paper English `vol.1/8`;
- original shipment numbers on machine-paper English notebooks in vols.4–5;
- bundle-number/category lists on separated leaves in `vol.94/7`.

Thus a sheet can carry not just text but an **address** within an archival topology.

Mechanism:

`PAPER_AS_ADDRESSABLE_RELATIONAL_SURFACE`.

Historical address must be kept analytically separate from:

- support;
- language;
- textual version;
- modern shelfmark.

---

## 2. Exact address plus content allows historical crosswalks

The strongest current case is modern `MSS EUR HODGSON/6`.

Its old title layer states:

`Box 2 / Item 12`.

Its English title reads:

`Tribes of people, judicial system, trade, army, topography`.

The August 1864 India Office manifest independently lists, under:

`Trunk No.2 -> second large bundle -> item 12`,

three English volumes concerning:

- classification of the people (`thar`);
- judicial system;
- customs (`Jagat-bhansar`);
- army;

plus Persian and Khas papers on the same subjects.

The overlap in both **number** and **content vocabulary** makes this the strongest manifest-to-modern mapping yet.

Current status:

`A_NEAR_CERTAIN_CANDIDATE`.

It is not diplomatically proven until the event-near RAS witnesses (`BHH/11/14`, `BHH/19/5`) and the vol.6 old cover/title layer are directly inspected.

Mechanism:

`HISTORICAL_ADDRESS_TO_TRANSFER_MANIFEST_CROSSWALK`.

---

## 3. Multilingual companions reveal the historical family behind the address

Modern Farsi states reproduce three of the four semantic categories named in 1864 item 12:

- `vol.101/4` — Gorkhali courts;
- `vol.101/8` — Khasa family names (`thar`);
- `vol.101/9` — customs duties on traded commodities.

Khas/Nepali relatives survive elsewhere:

- `vol.56/2` and `vol.56/14` — family-name/classification material;
- `vol.60/7` — law, courts, crimes and punishment.

Urdu army states survive in vol.103, although their relation to the manifest's historical label `Persian` must remain unresolved.

The modern archive therefore still contains the semantic architecture of the 1864 multilingual family, but distributed across separate volumes.

Mechanism:

`SEMANTIC_RECURRENCE_ACROSS_DECLARED_MULTILINGUAL_VERSION_FAMILY`.

This is not yet a direct stemma for every sheet. It is a ranked reconstruction.

---

## 4. The 1921 rebinding supplies a historically explicit disruption mechanism

Cambridge's collection history states that in 1921 the Hodgson papers were bound into 95 volumes and that arrangement was partly governed by physical dimensions.

It adds explicitly that related objects such as an original manuscript and its translation could be bound in different places simply because they were written on different sizes of paper.

Thus there is no need to infer a generic archival disruption mechanism.

The institution itself records one:

`sheet dimensions -> binding compatibility -> modern volume assignment`.

Mechanism:

`PAPER_DIMENSION_DRIVES_ARCHIVAL_REBINDING`.

This changes the historiographical status of paper size.

Size is not only:

- a manufacture result;
- a writing-space affordance;
- a format choice.

It can become a later **classification variable**.

---

## 5. Paper can first help break a relation and later help reconstruct it

This is the strongest archival paradox in the current article.

At the 1921 stage:

`physical paper dimension`
can separate historically related versions.

At the reconstruction stage:

`old numbers/titles written on paper`
can reconnect them.

So the same class of material object contributes both to:

- archival dispersion;
- archival recovery.

Mechanism:

`PHYSICAL_SUPPORT_CAN_BOTH_DISPERSE_AND_RESTORE_RELATION`.

This is narrower and stronger than a generic claim that archives are material.

---

## 6. A historical address is not a symbolic identity

The old address system cuts across material and linguistic divisions.

Examples:

- Sack 8 survives on Nepali handmade local-source paper;
- Box/sack metadata survives on `vol.1/8`, an English copy on imported paper;
- shipment numbers survive on English machine-paper notebooks in vols.4–5;
- Box 2 / Item 12 survives on an English machine-paper copy/report volume.

Therefore:

`historical custody identity != support identity`;

`historical custody identity != language identity`;

`historical custody identity != documentary version identity`.

Mechanism:

`CONTAINER_IDENTITY_ORTHOGONAL_TO_SUPPORT_IDENTITY`.

## 7. The strongest article scene

A compact scene can join the archive's nineteenth- and twentieth-century states.

**Scene:**

An old Hodgson leather/title layer in modern vol.6 says `Box 2 / Item 12`.

The 1864 transfer list has a Trunk 2, item 12 family of English, Persian and Khas papers on classification, justice, customs and army.

Modern Farsi courts, thar and customs states survive in vol.101; Khas/Nepali judicial and classification states survive in vols.56 and 60; related army states are dispersed further.

Then the Cambridge catalogue explains why original/translation families became separated in 1921: paper size was used as a binding criterion.

The chain is therefore potentially:

`multilingual knowledge production`
→ `addressed version family`
→ `1864 co-custody`
→ `1921 size sorting`
→ `modern dispersion`
→ `twenty-first-century reconstruction through old paper addresses`.

This is an archival history generated from paper itself.

## 8. What still must be physically measured

The public catalogue does not provide usable item-level dimensions for the modern vol.101/56/60 item-12 candidates.

Therefore do not yet write:

`these particular item-12 papers were separated in 1921 because their measured dimensions differed`.

The allowed claim is narrower:

1. 1864 documents a multilingual version/custody family;
2. modern catalogue shows plausible dispersed states;
3. Cambridge independently documents size-based rebinding as a general mechanism capable of separating originals/translations;
4. physical measurement must test whether that mechanism explains **this specific family**.

Required measurements:

- vol.6 old English states;
- vol.101/4, /8, /9;
- vol.56/2 and /14;
- vol.60/7;
- candidate army states in vol.103.

Record:

- sheet height/width;
- folded/notebook format;
- trimming;
- binding edge;
- laid/wove/machine formation;
- paper thickness;
- watermark/countermark where present.

If dimensions cluster by modern volume and differ across historical companion states, the causal argument strengthens substantially.

If they do not, search for other bindery variables.

---

## 10. Article-facing formulation

**Paper did not simply carry Hodgson's archive into institutional custody. It supplied both archival addresses and later principles of rearrangement. Nineteenth-century covers and title leaves could locate a document within boxes, sacks and numbered items; twentieth-century binders could ignore those relations and regroup papers by physical size. The result was not the disappearance of the older archive but its partial superimposition by a new one. Because the old addresses remained inscribed on surviving sheets, the material that helped determine later dispersion also preserved the evidence needed to reverse-engineer it.**

## Bottom line

The paper article now has an archival afterlife mechanism precise enough to be tested object-by-object. Historical paper surfaces carried hierarchical addresses; the 1864 transfer preserved multilingual version families; the 1921 institution explicitly used paper dimensions in rebinding; and surviving old identifiers make the pre-rebinding topology recoverable. The decisive next task is physical comparison of the item-12 family rather than further thematic searching.
