## 3. Active article architecture

### Short antecedent — 1818–1830: Nepal paper before Hodgson

Purpose: establish that Hodgson did not discover Nepal paper or first imagine remanufacture.

Current evidence:

- 1818: strong lead for a public paper specimen in the Calcutta Asiatic Society/Wallich network; original newspaper still desirable before exact body-level use.
- 1820: Wallich published a paper-plant/manufacture account. Near-contemporary evidence attributes the practical manufacturing particulars to Lt. H. R. Murray's official correspondence with the Military Board, based on Kumaon observation.
- c.1820: Raffles/Cartwright/Wallich correspondence explicitly distinguishes finished Nepal paper from `the material for making it` and imagines sending both to the Court of Directors.
- the security-paper/bank-note problem predates Cowan: Nepal paper was proposed as a difficult-to-procure distinctive paper that might frustrate forgery.

Do not turn this antecedent into a separate long article. Its function is to sharpen what Hodgson actually added.

### Case 1 — 1831–33: material and technical description travel

Hodgson explicitly acknowledges that Wallich had already fully described the paper plant and positions his own intervention around the method of manufacture.

Compared with the concise Murray/Wallich chain, Hodgson's 1831/32 account expands manufacture into an operational protocol including:

- implements;
- ash liquor strength/handling;
- boiling and beating;
- churning/dispersal;
- coarse screening;
- floating cloth-bottom frame;
- manual distribution and thickness control;
- fire drying;
- optional polishing;
- normally no sizing;
- large-sheet capability;
- dried pulp in brick/tile form for transport;
- administrative/record use and actual material transmission.

Working interpretation: Hodgson's distinctive move may have been to recode already circulating Himalayan paper knowledge into a more executable, measurable, transportable technical specification.

Cowan's 1833 British remanufacture then becomes a direct historical test of whether material + written specification were sufficient. They were not: remade paper was brittle/stiff/inferior while Nepal-made finished sheets remained highly valued and could take a bank-note impression with `perfect fidelity`.

Open P0:

- identify the actor/source chain behind Hodgson's 1831 manufacturing knowledge. Who did he observe, question, or rely on?
- do not assume a direct Raffles/Wallich -> Hodgson/Cowan institutional chain until a bridge is found.

### Case 2 — 1841: the makers travel

This is the centre of the article and should receive the most space.

Direct 1842 printed correspondence establishes:

- Lord Auckland asked Hodgson for people skilled in Nepal papermaking;
- Hodgson brokered the request through the Nepal Durbar;
- five papermakers arrived in Darjeeling;
- labour hierarchy/pay: head maker Rs 10/month; mate/first assistant Rs 8; three additional workers Rs 8 each; total Rs 42/month;
- head maker + first assistant were distinguished from the other three in the period's social/geographical vocabulary;
- the makers preferred Pacheem/Pucheem because it reduced carriage and sat near paper plant, suitable ash woods and running water;
- Campbell overrode this preference to keep manufacture nearer his own observation;
- Campbell built a house/tank and brought water through bamboo conduits;
- bark previously dried but rewetted in carriage mildewed and became `altogether useless for paper-making`;
- the makers qualified Hodgson's published tool/process description: flat stone + double-headed mallets rather than stone mortar + pestle; rejection of iron because it darkened paper; preference for tinned copper; flowing water to remove alkali;
- ash source, bark condition, water and site were active technical judgments;
- two Lepchas were induced to learn the manufacture;
- Government ordered continuation and asked that necessary manufacturers be kept on pay.

First-order question:

Did moving the makers solve what moving finished paper, pulp/material and written procedure had failed to solve?

Actor gaps remain article-changing:

- names of the five papermakers;
- division of tasks/authority within the group;
- names and later trajectory of the two Lepcha trainees;
- which makers were retained, for how long, and on whose payroll.

### Case 3 — 1841–49: what happened to the paper they made and to Nepal paper after the experiments?

Do not make Serampore machine incompatibility the ending.

Follow particular paper states into particular operations and hands:

- Campbell whitening trials: more whiteness could destroy toughness;
- O'Shaughnessy chemical bleaching and the handoff to Marshman;
- filtering: the 1842 journal editors found Darjeeling/Nepal experimental paper strong but slower and less clarifying than good European filter paper;
- O'Shaughnessy's own 1842 *Bengal Dispensatory*, by contrast, says `paper made in Nipal` is the best substance for bulky deposits because it is strong and permits rapid filtration followed by pressure;
- do not normalize this contradiction. Distinguish finished Nepal-made paper from experimental Darjeeling/bleached/remade states and distinguish filtration criteria;
- India-rubber waterproofing made Darjeeling paper a strong candidate for wet-season postal wrapping because it was waterproof, strong and much lighter than wax cloth;
- Marshman's 1842 sequel is already recovered: the problem on the machine web was specific to continuous industrial formation, including poor flow of mucilaginous material when greater thickness was attempted, bleaching/process cost, and comparison with cheaper alternative furnish;
- Serampore's machine context is now concrete: by 1832 Carey described pulp running continuously onto a wire web, over several cylinders, with the last cylinder steam-heated. Earlier Serampore production had also included local papermakers using mat frames and an English hand method using fine-wire frames;
- therefore the `machine web` was a specific continuous-feed, wire-web, cylinder/steam-drying production regime, not an abstract symbol of industrialization;
- 1849 Hooker correspondence proves active field-science procurement/use of Nepal paper by bundle/load, including requests for another load and six loads, coolie constraints, Nepal procurement, and a failed supply episode in which men, paper and money failed to return;
- Hooker explicitly used Nepal/brown paper for drying plants and to protect/transport white drawing paper and specimen-ticket paper.

First-order question:

Why did Nepal-made paper remain worth procuring by the load for field science even after attempts to bleach, remake and mechanize it?

## 4. Afterlife status

Do not claim a simple `1842 failure -> end` sequence.

Secure afterlife:

- active Nepal-paper procurement and expedition use in 1849 is CLOSED enough for article design.

P1 / unresolved:

- later Darjeeling Gazetteer claims Campbell's factory supplied strong writing/packing paper `for some years` before cheap plains paper displaced it. This remains retrospective until one contemporaneous witness confirms continued manufacture/supply.

- 1851 Great Exhibition material appears to include Nepal/Darjeeling paper; page-level catalogue control should be completed before using this as a firm claim.

Search rule: give the continued-Darjeeling-production question only one focused contemporaneous-print pass. If it does not close, leave it unresolved rather than opening a broad archive campaign.

## 6. HPS payoff — keep for conclusion, not opening problem

Possible higher-order consequences, to be earned through the cases:

- a `raw material` is not necessarily a portable bundle of the properties visible in the finished object;
- a written recipe/protocol can make work more portable without carrying every operative judgment needed to reproduce performance;
- moving skilled makers changes what becomes experimentally visible;
- machines do not merely scale up hand manufacture: they can redefine which material behaviours count as virtues or defects;
- `successful paper` has no single historical criterion across records, bank-note printing, whitening, filtration, waterproof packing, machine production and field botany;
- colonial improvement can turn an already successful made object back into an experimental raw material;
- mechanisation should not be treated retrospectively as the final tribunal determining whether a technology had a future.

Use these as conclusion-level claims, not as the article's stated first-order problem.
