# Paper cross-support argument v0.2

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: article-integration note after RAS support census correction and Cambridge exact-work comparison Waves 19–20.

## Revised argumentative ladder

The support section should now be written in four evidential layers rather than as one palm-leaf/paper contrast.

### Layer 1 — corpus association

Cowell and Eggeling’s 79-entry RAS Hodgson catalogue gives a measurable association:

- 12 palm-leaf manuscripts;
- 67 paper manuscripts under the catalogue’s declared Indian-paper baseline;
- palm-leaf median short axis c.2 in.;
- paper median short axis c.3 in.;
- palm-leaf median aspect ratio c.6.7;
- paper median aspect ratio c.3.64.

Claim:

`paper corpus occupies a broader and less extremely elongated format regime`.

Limit:

`cross-sectional association != support-conversion history`.

### Layer 2 — internal counterexamples

RAS MSS 34–35 share a Nāmasaṅgīti root-text ecology but are different commentaries:

- MS 34 palm leaf: 12 × c.2¼–2½ in., 7 lines;
- MS 35 paper: 12 × 2 in., 10 lines.

Paper is not wider here.

MSS 59–60 show explicitly identical textual content occupying different paper geometries.

Claim:

`support does not determine individual format`.

### Layer 3 — exact-work cross-support controls

Cross-collection comparisons now include:

- Bodhicaryāvatāra: strong short-axis expansion;
- Gaṇḍavyūha: extreme short-axis expansion plus long-axis reduction;
- Ekallavīra/Caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa: short axis stable, long axis expands;
- Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā: early paper remains extremely narrow; later paper becomes broad.

Claim:

`paper changes the feasible format space, not the direction of change in every witness`.

This is stronger than a simple median comparison because the proposition survives exact-work counterexamples.

### Layer 4 — production regime

Bendall’s Cambridge preface states that the Wright collection was assembled in Nepal and received in Cambridge from 1873–76. On Cowell’s suggestion, Wright was initially asked to procure copies made to order from works still extant in Nepal; specimen transcriptions became Add. 1042. Originals later also became procurable.

Claim:

`paper geometry is selected within historically specific acquisition, copying, scribal and institutional regimes`.

The late nineteenth-century Cambridge collection is therefore itself part of the history of paper reproduction after Hodgson.

Mechanisms:

- `INSTITUTIONAL_RECOPYING_AS_MANUSCRIPT_PRODUCTION`
- `PAPER_COPYING_AS_COLLECTION_BUILDING`
- `HODGSON_TO_WRIGHT_COLLECTION_SUCCESSION`

## Strongest current thesis for the support section

**Paper did not replace palm leaf with a single new page form. In the Hodgson RAS corpus it is associated with a broader short axis and lower aspect ratio, but exact-work comparisons show several different outcomes: some paper witnesses widen dramatically, some lengthen without widening, and some preserve or intensify an extreme pothi-like geometry. Paper is therefore best treated as an expanded material possibility space whose realised form depended on chronology, scribal density, textual programme, binding and the institutional conditions under which a manuscript was copied or acquired.**

This formulation should supersede any sentence that implies a unidirectional `palm leaf -> wider paper page` transition.

## Narrative sequence

Recommended paragraph order:

1. present the RAS corpus medians;
2. immediately qualify with RAS 34–35 to reject support determinism;
3. use Bodhicaryāvatāra and Gaṇḍavyūha for visually dramatic widening;
4. use Ekallavīra to show that paper can instead extend the long axis;
5. use Aṣṭasāhasrikā as the decisive temporal/formal bifurcation: early paper remains narrow, later paper broadens;
6. move into Bendall’s made-to-order copying programme to explain why chronology and production regime need to be historicised rather than treated as statistical nuisance variables.

This yields a clean conceptual progression:

`association -> counterexample -> controlled comparison -> temporal divergence -> institutional production`.

## New historiographical payoff

The support section can now avoid two standard material-history traps.

First, it avoids **substrate determinism**: a support does not dictate one form.

Second, it avoids **pure cultural persistence**: narrow paper pothi forms need not be treated as inert survivals of palm-leaf convention. They are choices within paper’s expanded material capacities and may coexist with broad paper forms of the same named work.

The historically interesting object is therefore not simply `paper` or `palm leaf`, but a realised manuscript state:

`support + dimensions + line density + script/hand + date + textual extent + container/binding + acquisition/copying regime`.

## Next evidential target

The next search should prioritise witnesses where copy lineage can be strengthened beyond work-level identity. The highest-value result would be a catalogue statement such as:

- copied from a named Hodgson manuscript;
- copied from the same Nepal exemplar;
- transcript/replacement expressly made from an older support;
- original and institutional copy both survive.

That would move the argument from `text-controlled support comparison` to `demonstrated support conversion within a copy chain`.
