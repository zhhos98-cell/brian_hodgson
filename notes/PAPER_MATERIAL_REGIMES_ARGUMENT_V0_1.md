# Paper material regimes argument v0.1

Date: 2026-08-31

Status: article-argument reset following Wave 23. Scope restricted to paper manufacture, paper properties, paper procurement and identifiable supply regimes. Visual surrogation and manuscript support-format comparisons remain separate evidence banks.

## Core proposition

The paper article should no longer be organised around a binary such as:

`Nepali paper vs European paper`

or a geography such as:

`Nepal -> Calcutta -> Britain`.

The stronger historical object is a **material-production regime**:

`fibre -> preparation chemistry -> beating/dispersion -> sheet-formation interface -> drying -> sizing/finishing -> commodity state -> transport/storage -> intended operation`.

A geographic label becomes analytically meaningful only after these operations are reconstructed.

## The decisive experiment

The 1833 JASB report creates a near-controlled material comparison.

### Route A

`Himalayan material -> paper made in Nepal -> finished thin sheets shipped to Britain`

Reported result:

- exceedingly tough;
- flexible;
- slightly off-white;
- able to take the minute impression of a bank-note plate with very high fidelity.

### Route B

`same broad Himalayan paper material -> processed/dried as pulp bricks -> shipped/stored -> reworked by British paper manufacturers -> sheet`

Reported result:

- brittle;
- stiff;
- transparent/varnish-like;
- gritty brown inclusions;
- hard to bleach;
- poor drainage;
- marked shrinkage on drying.

The contrast is not fibre provenance. It is **process history**.

This gives a particularly strong HPS claim:

**material identity is not preserved across technological translation.**

The material can remain nominally the same `paper stuff` while its operational properties change because the sequence that makes it into a sheet has changed.

Mechanisms:

- `PROCESS_HISTORY_EMBODIED_IN_SHEET`
- `MATERIAL_TRANSFER_WITHOUT_PROCESS_TRANSFER`
- `FINISHED_SHEET_VS_PULP_TRANSFER_DIVERGENCE`

## Why Darjeeling matters

The 1841–42 Darjeeling episode becomes a deliberate attempt to solve the 1833 problem by moving not just material but makers.

Sequence:

`1831/33 fibre/pulp sent to Britain -> failed remanufacture`

then

`1841 skilled Nepali papermakers moved to Darjeeling -> process reconstructed under observation`.

This sequence should be foregrounded.

It distinguishes:

- transporting material;
- transporting written description;
- transporting embodied skilled practice.

Campbell's reconstruction immediately reveals differences from Hodgson's printed recipe and identifies process-sensitive failures such as iron darkening the paper and bleaching destroying toughness.

The paper story therefore contains a hierarchy of portability:

`fibre/pulp is portable`

`recipe is portable`

`finished sheet is portable`

but

`the manufacturing system is only partially portable`.

## Calcutta should be treated as an interface city

Calcutta is crucial but should not become a fictive paper type.

Within the current evidence it concentrates:

- Government of India requests and decisions;
- scientific evaluation;
- chemical experimentation;
- postal trials;
- pricing/comparison;
- printing/publication;
- shipment/receipt;
- forwarding toward Britain.

The paper-history formulation should therefore be:

`Calcutta = evaluation/procurement/distribution interface`

not:

`Calcutta = paper origin`.

Serampore should remain separately coded as a manufacturing site.

## Serampore gives the machine-interface experiment

At Serampore, Himalayan bark material meets mechanised paper formation.

The strongest observation is an affordance reversal:

- long/mucilaginous material contributes to toughness in hand-made sheets;
- the same pulp behaviour obstructs free flow and thickness control on the machine web.

Thus material properties cannot be ranked abstractly.

`property value = operation × interface`.

A fibre can be excellent for one paper technology and troublesome for another.

## British paper should be disaggregated

For Hodgson's period `British paper` potentially means:

- handmade laid rag paper;
- handmade wove rag paper;
- early machine-made rag paper;
- different writing/drawing/filtering/cartridge grades;
- Company-associated supply paper;
- Whatman/Balston/Hollingworth and other maker products.

This disaggregation matters because the British paper industry was itself undergoing a transition from hand-vat production to machine webs while remaining dependent on rag and continental material supply.

There is no single British technological baseline against which Nepali paper can be measured.

## Whatman should be a paper-provenance problem, not a prestige label

Context supports a serious East India Company/Whatman relationship. Secondary scholarship citing Balston describes Whatman paper as deliberately designed to withstand Indian climates and to displace earlier Dutch Company paper supply.

That context is historically important.

Object attribution is separate.

Required rule:

`EIC context != Whatman identity`.

`VEIC/EIC watermark != Whatman identity`.

`J WHATMAN watermark alone != secure maker identity`.

Use:

`watermark motif + countermark + date + laid/wove structure + whole-sheet relation + dimensions + maker chronology`.

The reason is historical, not merely methodological: Company emblems occur with multiple makers, successor firms retained the Whatman mark, and Continental papermakers imitated/forged the Whatman brand.

## Europe should not be a residual category

British Company supply itself replaced or competed with earlier Dutch papers. Dutch eighteenth-century paper had its own material reputation, especially strong sizing and high-quality export grades. French and other Continental papers occupied other price/quality positions. Wove technology then crossed national borders.

Therefore `European paper` compresses exactly the differences this article is trying to recover.

Better fields:

- maker/mill;
- production region;
- laid/wove/machine state;
- fibre;
- sizing/finish;
- watermark/countermark;
- grade/use;
- supplier/merchant;
- EIC/other procurement route.

## Revised article sequence

### 1. 1831–32: one paper regime describes another

Hodgson describes a complete Himalayan manufacturing process and sends samples/pulp through British imperial communication.

### 2. 1833: metropolitan remanufacture fails

The same material produces different paper under a different manufacturing sequence.

This is the key empirical scene.

### 3. 1841: move makers, not only matter

Five skilled makers are transferred to Darjeeling; written recipe meets embodied craft.

### 4. 1841–42: property optimisation generates failures

Whitening damages toughness; iron changes colour; wet bark mildews; transport and chemical custody become part of manufacture.

### 5. 1842: Serampore machine interface

Hand-paper fibre becomes troublesome machine furnish.

### 6. Calcutta: compare, price, test and distribute

The city coordinates tests rather than defining material origin.

### 7. British/EIC paper: reconstruct the competing supply system

Whatman and other British papers should be analysed through manufacture, marks, size, grade and Company procurement.

### 8. Dutch/European paper: restore the preceding and competing paper market

This prevents `Europe` from appearing as a technologically homogeneous source.

## Strong article paragraph

**The most revealing transfer in Hodgson's paper history was not a successful one. In 1831 dried Himalayan paper pulp was formed into transportable bricks and sent to Britain, where manufacturers attempted to remake it into paper. The result, reported in Calcutta in 1833, was brittle, stiff, difficult to bleach and unlike the tough flexible sheets produced in the Himalaya. Yet finished Nepal-made sheets sent earlier to Britain had retained enough strength and surface fidelity to take the minute impression of a bank-note plate. The fibre had travelled in both cases; the paper-making system had not. Paper quality was therefore not an intrinsic property of a plant moving between regions. It was the material residue of a sequence of alkaline preparation, beating, dispersion, sheet formation, drainage, drying and finishing. The later Darjeeling and Serampore experiments made this difference experimental: moving skilled makers could reproduce parts of the hand process, while the same bark material acquired new failures when chemical whitening or the machine web became the operative interface.**

## What this does to the article's historiography

The intervention becomes narrower and stronger than `paper matters`.

It argues that paper is a **process-retentive material**.

A sheet carries the consequences of operations that may no longer be visually obvious once it enters an archive. Recovering paper history therefore requires reconstructing manufacture and circulation together.

This connects material history with HPS through a specific epistemic mechanism:

scientific and administrative uses depend on properties such as strength, absorption, smoothness, filtration rate, dimensional stability and printable fidelity, but these properties are outcomes of manufacture rather than stable qualities of named substances.

## Bottom line

The paper article's central comparison should now be **manufacturing systems acting on material**, not nations supplying paper. The 1833 British failure and the 1829 Nepal-made success form the cleanest empirical demonstration: paper retains process history, and fibre provenance alone cannot reproduce it.
