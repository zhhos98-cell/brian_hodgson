# Scientific-history reframing

## Editorial decision

The old BASAS/Buddhist Studies framing is not the argumentative spine of this project. It is retained as a source of recoverable observations about annotation, classification, paper techniques, institutions, and self-archiving.

The new project should read as history of science before it reads as the history of Buddhist Studies.

## What to cut or compress

### Cut as governing claims

- Hodgson as primarily an early European Buddhist scholar.
- Buddhist doctrine as the central object of reconstruction.
- “self-postcolonial” as a leading interpretive category.
- a narrative in which later Buddhist marginalia are chiefly evidence of ideological or hermeneutic deconstruction.

### Compress to contextual evidence

- Buddhist manuscripts, taxonomies, and correspondence with Orientalists.
- Hodgson's classifications of Buddhist materials.
- metropolitan Orientalist reception.

These survive only when they reveal a knowledge operation: selection of paper, copying, inscription, table-making, list-making, comparison, evidential hierarchy, authentication, revision, or circulation.

## New central problem

How did paper, annotation, classification, copying, drawing, and institutional circulation make Himalayan natural history into portable scientific knowledge, and how did Brian Houghton Hodgson use those operations to manage authority from a colonial periphery?

This formulation makes the project simultaneously a history of natural history, a history of material knowledge, and a history of scientific infrastructure.

## Anchor case: Zoology of Nipal

The unpublished *Zoology of Nipal* should become a primary anchor rather than a peripheral comparison. A 2026 SHNH/ZSL circular describes Hodgson's manuscripts and papers as a major ZSL archival collection and summarizes David A. Lowther's 2019 study of the work as a project built over more than twenty years from extensive notes and drawings. This gives the paper a clearly scientific object: the making, failure, and archival afterlife of a large natural-historical knowledge system.

Questions to pursue:

- What kinds of paper carried field observations, classifications, fair copies, drawings, and correspondence?
- Were different paper types associated with different stages or statuses of knowledge?
- How were local observations converted into forms legible to ZSL, Linnean, Kew, RAS, or other metropolitan institutions?
- Where did drawings, labels, specimen descriptions, lists, tables, and letters diverge from one another?
- What happened when the projected book failed to stabilize as a publication but survived as an archive?

## Paper technologies as a testable archival hypothesis

A 2025 project description already distinguishes British machine-made wove papers (including Whatman/Towgood), Nepali handmade lokta, machine-produced papers from Calcutta, and administrative blue papers. It proposes that these material differences indexed authenticity, bureaucratic authority, and localized knowledge.

Treat this as a hypothesis to verify rather than a conclusion already established. Build a paper census with fields such as:

- repository / shelfmark
- date
- genre
- paper support
- watermark / countermark
- dimensions and format
- ink / pencil / colour
- hand(s)
- annotations and later additions
- drawing / table / list / prose
- associated specimen or taxon
- sender / recipient
- copy / draft / fair copy / office copy
- institutional destination

The payoff is strongest if material differentiation can be shown to correspond to evidential or institutional differentiation.

## Institutional and infrastructural axes

### Zoological Society of London

Use *Zoology of Nipal* and the manuscript/drawing archive to reconstruct classification, illustration, publication failure, and metropolitan validation.

### Linnean Society and Kew

Retain these from the old abstract as institutions through which classificatory forms and botanical/zoological standards circulated. Verify precisely which Hodgson papers, transactions, or correspondence support each connection.

### Royal Geographical Society

The 2025 archive enquiry identifies two useful leads:

- `rgs317723`: *Route from Cathmandu in Nepal, to Tazedo on the Chinese Frontier*.
- `rgs810460`: Trigonometrical Survey of India / discovery and naming of Mount Everest, including letters by A. Scott Waugh and Brian H. Hodgson.

These materials can extend the argument from natural-historical paperwork into geographical survey, routes, naming, priority, and imperial spatial knowledge. They may become a second case if they share material or evidential operations with the zoological archive.

## Scientific persona, recoded

The old “Self as Archive” insight remains useful after recoding. Late annotations should be analyzed as practices of scientific revision and archival self-management:

- correcting earlier claims;
- managing priority and reputation;
- changing classifications;
- marking obsolete information;
- creating cross-temporal layers within a single document;
- producing an archive designed for later readers.

This allows the paper to connect scholarly persona with material practices without making personality or ideology the causal centre.

## Possible article architecture

### 1. An unfinished zoology as a scientific problem

Begin with the material abundance and publication failure of *Zoology of Nipal*. The paradox is productive: a project that failed as a book may have succeeded as an infrastructure of notes, images, specimens, and correspondence.

### 2. Making Himalayan observations portable

Reconstruct lists, drawings, descriptions, specimens, copies, and paper supports as conversion devices between local observation and metropolitan taxonomic systems.

### 3. Paper regimes

Demonstrate, where the archive permits, the division of labour among British wove paper, locally produced paper, Calcutta paper, office blue paper, and other supports. Move from watermark description to epistemic function.

### 4. Institutions of validation

Track how claims moved through ZSL, Linnean, Kew, RAS and related networks. Focus on acceptance, delay, rejection, redescription, priority, and publication.

### 5. Revision and self-archiving

Use later annotations to show how Hodgson re-entered his earlier paperwork. Treat the archive as a temporally layered scientific instrument.

### 6. Buddhism as controlled comparison, if needed

At most one compressed section or comparative passage: manuscript and Buddhist classificatory work demonstrates that the same paper operations crossed the conventional boundary between “Orientalism” and “natural history.” The point is not Buddhist doctrine but the mobility of a repertoire of knowledge techniques.

## Strongest historiographical intervention

The project can challenge a familiar metropolitan model of colonial science in which information simply travels from field collector to centre. Hodgson's archive suggests that circulation depended on differentiated paper regimes and recursive acts of copying, annotation, classification, and self-archiving. “Centre” and “periphery” were partly produced through those material operations.

The most promising version therefore does not ask whether Hodgson was properly a naturalist, Orientalist, administrator, or Buddhist scholar. It asks how the same repertoire of paper operations allowed heterogeneous objects - animals, routes, manuscripts, names, observations - to become commensurable forms of colonial knowledge.
