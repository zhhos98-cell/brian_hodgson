# Paper, proof, and passage — expansion of the Hodgson project

## Core shift

The project should no longer treat paper merely as a differentiated writing support. The stronger object is the **material infrastructure that made heterogeneous Himalayan knowledge objects credible and mobile**.

Provisional formulation:

> Hodgson's archive was produced through operations that made knowledge both **provable** and **transportable**: paper selection, copying, drawing, collation, seals and copy-state, counting and pricing, binding, bundling, packaging, shipment, receipt, sorting, rebinding and redistribution. Manuscripts, administrative records and zoological specimens differed materially, but they passed through overlapping systems of labour, accounting, custody and verification.

This keeps natural history at the centre while allowing Buddhist/Sanskrit/Tibetan manuscript material to become a powerful comparative domain rather than the historiographical driver.

## 1. Why the scripture/manuscript material now matters

The manuscript corpus is useful precisely because it makes normally invisible material operations unusually explicit.

British Library inventory evidence already gives:
- locally produced Nepali paper and imported/machine-made paper in mixed use;
- bills for 1,012 bundles of Nepali paper plus wax cloth, thread and twine;
- copying, binding, bundling, packaging and shipment as separately costed operations;
- carpenters, labourers and porters;
- prices per copied stanza;
- loose leaves reorganised into packages, bundles and boxes;
- Lhasa–Kathmandu copying and forwarding through Tibetan businessmen;
- originals, copies, sealed royal orders, inscription copies and palm-leaf documents recopied onto paper;
- original bundle covers and original-shipment box contents;
- Hodgson's own Darjeeling archive issue/borrowing records;
- later British institutional rebinding partly by paper dimensions.

The Buddhist content itself therefore need not carry the argument. The manuscripts act as a **high-resolution archive of knowledge manufacture and circulation**.

## 2. Authentication: use a broader category than “forgery” for now

The archive already supports a strong problem of **evidentiary reliability**, but explicit forgery disputes have not yet been demonstrated.

Immediate material variables:
- original vs copy;
- fair vs rough copy;
- seal and administrative format;
- colophon;
- old palm leaf vs nineteenth-century paper copy;
- inscription vs portable paper surrogate;
- hand/script;
- paper type and condition;
- provenance / sender / intermediary;
- bundle or box membership;
- successive catalogue and archive labels.

A period formulation close to Hodgson's own scholarly world is “proof” or “original authority”. In `Quotations in Proof of his Sketch of Buddhism`, Hodgson explicitly presented translated extracts from Sanskrit books still extant in Nepal as evidence demanded by Orientalist readers. That rhetoric should be tested against the physical practices by which an alleged original, copy, extract or translation was identified and made credible.

The later Cowell–Eggeling RAS catalogue likewise stresses manuscript identification through comparison with other copies and notes material/palaeographical peculiarities in individual Hodgson manuscripts. Modern paper-fibre/codicological scholarship shows that paper analysis can be combined with palaeography and textual evidence to investigate production region and dating. This modern methodology should be used as an analytical aid, not projected anachronistically onto Hodgson.

## 3. Packaging is epistemic, not merely logistical

The BL bills allow a reconstruction of the transformation:

**loose leaves → counted/collated text → bound or bundled unit → protective materials → package → box/sack → porter/shipment → receipt → institutional unit**.

Each stage changes what can be counted, owned, identified, exchanged, insured/accounted for, compared and cited. Packaging therefore participates in the production of an epistemic object.

The especially useful archival traces are:
- bundle numbers;
- original bundle covers;
- original shipment box-content pages;
- receipts recording numbers of bundles / packages / leaves / copies;
- lists attaching page/leaf/verse counts and prices to individual manuscripts;
- downstream institutional rebinding that can separate items originally belonging together.

## 4. “Filling/padding” is a live question, not yet a finding

Direct evidence has been found for:
- wax cloth;
- thread and twine;
- binding;
- bundling;
- packaging;
- boxes and sacks;
- carpenters and porters;
- shipment.

No direct Hodgson-period evidence has yet been found for cushioning/filling materials such as straw, cotton, wool, waste paper, shavings or sawdust. This should become a targeted search variable across:
- manuscript shipment bills and correspondence;
- Residency procurement records;
- East India Company freight/packing rules;
- ZSL / British Museum specimen correspondence;
- contemporary natural-history collecting manuals;
- book/manuscript trade and library shipping instructions.

A negative result would still matter: different classes of objects may have demanded different regimes of protection.

## 5. Natural history now gives the decisive comparison

ZSL material shows a strikingly parallel chain for zoological objects. In 1844–45 J. E. Gray and Edward Gerrard discussed packing Hodgson's specimens, choosing how to send them, receiving and sorting them, selecting those wanted for the British Museum, describing new forms, and packing/forwarding duplicates elsewhere. ZSL inventories also record mammal skins “sent home”, lists of mammal and bird skins in boxes, a box of drawings and memoranda, and distinctions between original and fair copies of zoological drawings.

The BL Hodgson papers meanwhile record Rajman Chitrakar selecting/recommending birds, drawing them, and arranging shipment of living and dead birds from Tibet to Hodgson in Darjeeling; lists of bones, horns and skulls were kept on Nepali hand-made paper.

This makes possible a genuinely integrated history-of-science claim:

> The unit of colonial natural history was not the specimen alone. It was a compound object made from specimen + drawing + memorandum/list + label/number + box/package + chain of custody. Manuscripts were produced through an analogous compound of text + support + copy-state + collation/count + wrapper/bundle + receipt/provenance. Hodgson's scientific practice can be reconstructed through the operations shared by these material assemblages.

## 6. Do not assume a simple paper hierarchy

The catalogue already falsifies an easy mapping:

- imported/machine-made paper is used for official correspondence, routes, drafts, Buddhist studies, drawings and administrative tables;
- Nepali hand-made paper is used for Buddhist manuscripts, administrative records, routes, ethnographic notes, animal/bone lists and bird-collection correspondence;
- some dossiers mix the two paper regimes.

So the question is not “European paper = authoritative science; Nepali paper = local manuscript tradition”. Instead ask when support mattered and what work it performed in a particular chain of copying, circulation or preservation.

## 7. A stronger article architecture

1. **Opening object: a shipment, not a biography.** Start with a bill/receipt where paper, wax cloth, thread/twine, copying, carpenters, porters, boxes and destination appear together.
2. **Making paper / choosing support.** Nepali paper manufacture, sample circulation and the 1841 transfer of papermakers to Darjeeling; mixed imported/local paper regimes.
3. **Making a reliable document.** Originals/copies, seals, colophons, palm-leaf-to-paper conversion, inscriptions transformed into portable paper surrogates, rough/fair copies and translation.
4. **Making a transportable book.** Copying costs, binding, bundling, packaging, box/sack numbers, receipts, Lhasa–Kathmandu–Calcutta networks.
5. **Making a transportable animal.** Bird/skin/bone collection, drawings and memoranda, packing, boxes, shipment, sorting, description, duplicate redistribution.
6. **Archive after transit.** Hodgson's own issue records; later museum/library sorting and 1921 rebinding by physical dimensions; how institutional custody produces new object boundaries.
7. **Conclusion: portability and proof were co-produced.** What counted as a stable “text”, “specimen”, “copy”, or “species” depended partly on the infrastructures that allowed an object to survive, be matched to records, compared and circulated.

## 8. Working titles

- **Paper, Proof, and Passage: Brian Houghton Hodgson's Himalayan Knowledge Infrastructure**
- **Making Knowledge Travel: Paper, Packaging, and Proof in Hodgson's Himalaya**
- **Paper Empires in Transit: Manuscripts, Specimens, and the Material Infrastructure of Hodgson's Natural History**

The older `Paper Empires` title remains viable; the new evidence gives it a clearer mechanism.

## 9. Immediate empirical programme

Build two linked censuses rather than one loose archive notebook.

### A. Document/manuscript census
Capture support, format, copy-state, seal/colophon, hand, counts, prices, binding/bundle/package/box, labour, sender/recipient, route, receipt, damage and later rebinding.

### B. Specimen/drawing census
Capture taxon/specimen type, collector/preparator/draughtsman, skin/skeleton/bone/living specimen, drawing state, memorandum/label/number, box/case, packing actor/material, sender/recipient, route, arrival, sorting, description/publication, retention/duplicate redistribution, damage/loss.

The analytical comparison should be operation-by-operation, not “Buddhism versus zoology”.
