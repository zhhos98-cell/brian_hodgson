# Article spine — evidence-led history of science version

## Provisional titles

**Paper Empires: Brian Houghton Hodgson and the Material Infrastructures of Himalayan Knowledge**

Alternative if natural history ultimately carries most of the archive:

**Paper Empires: Brian Houghton Hodgson and the Material Infrastructures of Himalayan Natural History**

The choice between these titles should be made after the public/archive census, not in advance.

## Scope rule

Do **not** pre-assign Buddhist manuscripts to a subordinate comparative tier. Hodgson's Buddhist manuscripts, zoological specimens and drawings, geographical/survey papers, administrative records, and later self-archive are distinct classes of knowledge objects. Their relative argumentative weight should be determined by evidence density and by how strongly each corpus exposes the mechanisms under study.

The governing comparison is therefore empirical:

**How were heterogeneous Himalayan knowledge objects made credible, identifiable, countable, portable, auditable after separation, and institutionally usable?**

## One-sentence claim

Hodgson's scholarly authority was produced through material operations — paper selection, copying, drawing, tabulation, annotation, collation, numbering, wrappers and title slips, binding and bundling, packing, shipment, receipt, comparison, sorting, cataloguing, and redistribution — which made heterogeneous Himalayan objects portable and partially auditable while also generating failures of authenticity, copy-state, sequence, priority, provenance, custody, and object identity.

## Provisional abstract

Brian Houghton Hodgson is usually divided retrospectively among colonial administration, zoology, geography, ethnology, and the discovery of Sanskrit Buddhist literature in Nepal. This article instead reconstructs the material infrastructure through which those fields were made practicable. Hodgson's archive preserves unusually dense traces of paper manufacture and procurement, commissioned copying, drawings and specimen tickets, manuscript leaves and palm-leaf originals, wrappers and title slips, lists, boxes, bastas, trunks, crates, institutional receipts, later comparison and cataloguing, and repeated repacking and redistribution between Nepal, Calcutta, London, Oxford, and Paris. Rather than assuming that these operations were secondary to disciplinary knowledge, the article asks how they constituted objects that had to remain identifiable and auditable after physical separation. Buddhist manuscripts expose originals and commissioned copies, scribal error, old and modern hands, paper and palm-leaf supports, damaged and supplied leaves, wrapper titles, box and bundle identities, safe arrival, institutional custody, and philological collation. Zoological materials expose a parallel but non-identical chain of local observations, labels, drawing numbers, skins, bones, packing, shipment, sorting, specimen comparison, type attribution, and redistribution. Later museums and libraries did not merely preserve these objects: label removal, relabelling, delayed unpacking, rebinding, and reclassification altered the evidential relations on which later scholarship depended. Hodgson's work therefore appears as a distributed system for making Himalayan knowledge portable, provable, and reconstructible after movement. The precise balance between Buddhist, zoological, geographical, and administrative cases remains an empirical question.

## Core evidence domains — no fixed hierarchy yet

### A. Buddhist manuscripts, drawings, and textual witnesses

- Commissioned copying for learned societies, including the 64 manuscripts copied for the Société Asiatique.
- Hodgson–Burnouf correspondence on acquisition, receipt, further copies, safe addresses, and intellectual use.
- RAS / BnF / Asiatic Society / Bodleian manuscript material: paper vs palm leaf, dimensions, hands, old/modern, condition, missing/supplied leaves, wrappers, covers, bindings, and copy-state.
- Wrapper titles and Hodgson slips as identity-bearing inscriptions; later re-identifications that reveal earlier catalogue errors.
- Folio numbering, missing leaves, modern pencil foliation, and later attempts to reconstruct sequence.
- Lists, bastas, trunks, boxes and crates; repacking in 1844; institutional receipt and custody.
- Collation and authentication: how `original Sanskrit`, copies, errors, colophons, dates, scripts, wrapper titles, and comparative witnesses produced or destabilised textual authority.
- Cases in which copies become better witnesses than deteriorating originals.
- Buddhist drawings plus explanatory manuscripts as composite text-image packages.

### B. Zoology and natural history

- ZSL / *Zoology of Nipal* manuscripts, drawings, notes, associated publication history.
- Specimen tickets, paired labels, drawing numbers, numbered specimens, boxes and packing instructions.
- `Original and authentic types` as contemporary zoological language rather than a later analytic imposition.
- Correspondence demonstrating movement between Hodgson, collectors/preparators, British Museum, ZSL, East India Company Museum, Cambridge and other institutions.
- Distributed objects: skins, drawings and osteological remains physically separated but intended to be reassembled by number.
- Sorting, comparison, new/old species decisions, duplicate redistribution, and correction of copied names/numbers.
- Type-identification failures caused by removed labels, reused drawing numbers, uncertain accession dates, missing drawings and custody disputes.
- Material inspection of paper supports, watermarks, formats, hands, annotations, copy states and drawing systems.

### C. Paper manufacture and paper regimes

- Hodgson's 1831/1832 work on Nepali paper-making and paper samples.
- 1841 movement of Nepali paper-makers to Darjeeling for enquiries/experiments.
- Procurement accounts for Nepali paper, wax cloth, thread/twine and binding/packing materials.
- Imported/machine-made papers, Nepali handmade paper, mixed dossiers, coloured/blackened paper, office paper, drawing paper, specimen-ticket paper and plant-drying/wrapping paper.
- Paper as a set of affordances — durability, absorbency/drying, wrapping, interleaving, drawing, labelling, folding and bundling — rather than a fixed bearer of epistemic status.
- Test paper-function correlations rather than assuming them.

### D. Geography, survey, administration and documentary authority

- RGS `rgs317723` route material.
- RGS `rgs810460` Waugh-Hodgson / Everest naming material.
- Sealed originals, copies of royal orders, inscription copies, palm-leaf documents recopied onto paper.
- Routes, copying, naming, priority, bureaucratic receipt, and institutional adjudication.

### E. Recursive archive, institutional handling and reputation

- Later annotations and self-corrections.
- Lists of boxes, trunks, drawings, books and scientific/literary stores.
- Hodgson's instruction that literary and scientific stores should be disposed of for the advantage of his reputation.
- British Museum removal/replacement of original specimen labels.
- East India Company Museum preservation of Hodgson's labels under Frederic Moore.
- Long-term non-opening of packed specimens in the India Museum.
- Leiden relabelling and uncertainty about original labels.
- BL/India Office rebinding and reclassification partly by physical dimensions, separating originals from translations/copies.
- Later institutional handling as a variable that can either preserve physical objects while destroying relations, or preserve old relations while delaying access.

## What the article should avoid

- treating `colonialism` as a sufficient explanation without showing the concrete material mechanism;
- imposing modern disciplinary borders on the source corpus before the evidence is mapped;
- reducing Buddhist manuscripts to content or, conversely, allowing Buddhist doctrinal historiography alone to determine the argument;
- using `paper technology` as a decorative metaphor without paper-level evidence;
- assuming that a watermark or paper type automatically maps to an epistemic function;
- treating `original`, `copy`, `old`, `modern`, `authentic`, or `type` as self-evident categories rather than practices of identification;
- assuming that a catalogue number, drawing number, wrapper title, accession label, or later foliation is a transparent identifier;
- making institutional affiliations causal unless correspondence, movement, receipt, comparison, publication, custody or archival handling demonstrates the mechanism;
- narrating Hodgson's late annotations chiefly as ideological conversion.

## Testable claims

1. **Material differentiation claim:** different supports, formats and copy states sometimes correlate with genre, stage of work, destination or evidential status, but the correlations must be demonstrated rather than assumed.
2. **Copy-production claim:** commissioned manuscript copying was a measurable knowledge industry involving paper supply, scribal labour, error, counting and institutional commissioning.
3. **Conversion claim:** drawings, lists, tables, labels, descriptions, translations, extracts and fair copies functioned as conversion devices between local objects and distant scholarly comparison.
4. **Packaging/object-identity claim:** wrappers, bundles, boxes, crates, trunks and their lists stabilised object identity during movement but could also create or preserve misidentifications.
5. **Receipt-and-sorting claim:** safe arrival did not complete circulation; institutional receipt, sorting, comparison, cataloguing and custody were further epistemic stages.
6. **Auditability claim:** numbers, labels, foliation, slips, receipts, copy relations and distribution tables attempted to make physically separated witnesses reconstructible after movement; their failure generated later identification and priority problems.
7. **Institutional-handling claim:** different museum/library practices — label removal, label preservation, delayed unpacking, relabelling, rebinding and reclassification — produced different levels of later auditability.
8. **Recursive archive claim:** later annotations, lists and reorganisations transformed older documents into instruments of revision, priority and reputation management.
9. **Cross-domain infrastructure claim:** Buddhist manuscripts and zoological specimens passed through overlapping but non-identical systems of counting, representation, packing, movement, receipt, comparison and redistribution.
10. **Publication-failure claim:** the failure of *Zoology of Nipal* to stabilise as a single book may expose the infrastructure more clearly than a finished publication would.

## Preferred opening scenes — to choose after census

Possible opening A: a *Zoology of Nipal* drawing/specimen pair whose original label is lost and whose type identity has to be reconstructed from number, accession, measurement and later taxonomy.

Possible opening B: a Buddhist codex whose wrapper title fixed the wrong identity for more than a century before re-examination of the folios overturned it.

Possible opening C: a box/bundle list that mixes Buddhist books, drawings, statistics or scientific stores and is later repacked for international movement.

Possible opening D: two institutions receiving Hodgson specimens but treating the labels differently, producing divergent later capacities to identify type material.

The opening should make the mechanism visible before introducing Hodgson biographically.

## Preferred ending

Return to the archive as a set of objects whose identities were repeatedly remade by copying, packaging, movement, sorting, relabelling, rebinding and cataloguing. Hodgson is analytically useful not because he belongs neatly to one discipline, but because the survival — and partial destruction — of relational traces lets us observe the infrastructures through which nineteenth-century Himalayan knowledge became portable, comparable, credible, auditable and reputationally valuable.
