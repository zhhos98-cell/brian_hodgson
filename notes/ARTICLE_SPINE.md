# Article spine — evidence-led history of science version

## Provisional titles

**Paper Empires: Brian Houghton Hodgson and the Material Infrastructures of Himalayan Knowledge**

Alternative if natural history ultimately carries most of the archive:

**Paper Empires: Brian Houghton Hodgson and the Material Infrastructures of Himalayan Natural History**

The choice between these titles should be made after the public/archive census, not in advance.

## Scope rule

Do **not** pre-assign Buddhist manuscripts to a subordinate comparative tier. Hodgson's Buddhist manuscripts, zoological specimens and drawings, geographical/survey papers, administrative records, and later self-archive are distinct classes of knowledge objects. Their relative argumentative weight should be determined by evidence density and by how strongly each corpus exposes the mechanisms under study.

The governing comparison is therefore empirical:

**How were heterogeneous Himalayan knowledge objects made credible, identifiable, countable, portable, and institutionally usable?**

## One-sentence claim

Hodgson's scholarly authority was produced through material operations — paper selection, copying, drawing, tabulation, annotation, collation, numbering, wrappers and title slips, binding and bundling, packing, shipment, receipt, comparison, sorting, cataloguing, and redistribution — which converted heterogeneous Himalayan objects into portable claims while also generating problems of authenticity, copy-state, error, priority, and object identity.

## Provisional abstract

Brian Houghton Hodgson is usually divided retrospectively among colonial administration, zoology, geography, ethnology, and the discovery of Sanskrit Buddhist literature in Nepal. This article instead reconstructs the material infrastructure through which those fields were made practicable. Hodgson's archive preserves unusually dense traces of paper manufacture and procurement, commissioned copying, drawings and specimen tickets, manuscript leaves and palm-leaf originals, wrappers and title slips, lists, boxes, bastas, trunks, crates, institutional receipts, later comparison and cataloguing, and repeated repacking and redistribution between Nepal, Calcutta, London, Oxford, and Paris. Rather than assuming that these operations were secondary to disciplinary knowledge, the article asks how they helped constitute the objects on which disciplinary claims depended. Buddhist manuscripts are especially important because the archive records originals and commissioned copies, scribal error, old and modern hands, paper and palm-leaf supports, damaged and supplied leaves, box and bundle identities, safe arrival, institutional custody, and philological collation. Zoological materials expose a parallel but non-identical chain of drawings, labels, packing, shipment, sorting, specimen comparison, and redistribution. The article therefore treats Hodgson's work as a distributed system for making Himalayan knowledge both provable and transportable. The precise balance between Buddhist, zoological, geographical, and administrative cases remains an empirical question.

## Core evidence domains — no fixed hierarchy yet

### A. Buddhist manuscripts, drawings, and textual witnesses

- Commissioned copying for learned societies, including the 64 manuscripts copied for the Société Asiatique.
- Hodgson–Burnouf correspondence on acquisition, receipt, further copies, safe addresses, and intellectual use.
- RAS / BnF / Asiatic Society / Bodleian manuscript material: paper vs palm leaf, dimensions, hands, old/modern, condition, missing/supplied leaves, wrappers, covers, bindings, and copy-state.
- Lists, bastas, trunks, boxes and crates; repacking in 1844; institutional receipt and custody.
- Collation and authentication: how `original Sanskrit`, copies, errors, colophons, dates, scripts, wrapper titles, and comparative witnesses produced or destabilised textual authority.
- Buddhist drawings plus explanatory manuscripts as composite text-image packages.

### B. Zoology and natural history

- ZSL / *Zoology of Nipal* manuscripts, drawings, notes, associated publication history.
- Specimen tickets, drawings, numbered specimens, boxes and packing instructions.
- Correspondence demonstrating movement between Hodgson, collectors/preparators, British Museum, ZSL and other institutions.
- Sorting, comparison, new/old species decisions, duplicate redistribution, and correction of copied names/numbers.
- Material inspection of paper supports, watermarks, formats, hands, annotations, copy states and drawing systems.

### C. Paper manufacture and paper regimes

- Hodgson's 1831/1832 work on Nepali paper-making and paper samples.
- 1841 movement of Nepali paper-makers to Darjeeling for enquiries/experiments.
- Procurement accounts for Nepali paper, wax cloth, thread/twine and binding/packing materials.
- Imported/machine-made papers, Nepali handmade paper, mixed dossiers, coloured/blackened paper, office paper, drawing paper, specimen-ticket paper and plant-drying/wrapping paper.
- Test paper-function correlations rather than assuming them.

### D. Geography, survey, administration and documentary authority

- RGS `rgs317723` route material.
- RGS `rgs810460` Waugh-Hodgson / Everest naming material.
- Sealed originals, copies of royal orders, inscription copies, palm-leaf documents recopied onto paper.
- Routes, copying, naming, priority, bureaucratic receipt, and institutional adjudication.

### E. Recursive archive and reputation

- Later annotations and self-corrections.
- Lists of boxes, trunks, drawings, books and scientific/literary stores.
- Hodgson's instruction that literary and scientific stores should be disposed of for the advantage of his reputation.
- Later institutional rebinding/reclassification and the creation of new object boundaries.

## What the article should avoid

- treating `colonialism` as a sufficient explanation without showing the concrete material mechanism;
- imposing modern disciplinary borders on the source corpus before the evidence is mapped;
- reducing Buddhist manuscripts to content or, conversely, allowing Buddhist doctrinal historiography alone to determine the argument;
- using `paper technology` as a decorative metaphor without paper-level evidence;
- assuming that a watermark or paper type automatically maps to an epistemic function;
- treating `original`, `copy`, `old`, `modern`, or `authentic` as self-evident categories rather than practices of identification;
- making institutional affiliations causal unless correspondence, movement, receipt, comparison, publication, custody or archival handling demonstrates the mechanism;
- narrating Hodgson's late annotations chiefly as ideological conversion.

## Testable claims

1. **Material differentiation claim:** different supports, formats and copy states sometimes correlate with genre, stage of work, destination or evidential status, but the correlations must be demonstrated rather than assumed.
2. **Copy-production claim:** commissioned manuscript copying was a measurable knowledge industry involving paper supply, scribal labour, error, counting and institutional commissioning.
3. **Conversion claim:** drawings, lists, tables, labels, descriptions, translations, extracts and fair copies functioned as conversion devices between local objects and distant scholarly comparison.
4. **Packaging/object-identity claim:** wrappers, bundles, boxes, crates, trunks and their lists stabilised object identity during movement but could also create or preserve misidentifications.
5. **Receipt-and-sorting claim:** safe arrival did not complete circulation; institutional receipt, sorting, comparison, cataloguing and custody were further epistemic stages.
6. **Recursive archive claim:** later annotations, lists and reorganisations transformed older documents into instruments of revision, priority and reputation management.
7. **Cross-domain infrastructure claim:** Buddhist manuscripts and zoological specimens passed through overlapping but non-identical systems of counting, representation, packing, movement, receipt, comparison and redistribution.
8. **Publication-failure claim:** the failure of *Zoology of Nipal* to stabilise as a single book may expose the infrastructure more clearly than a finished publication would.

## Preferred opening scenes — to choose after census

Possible opening A: a *Zoology of Nipal* sheet where drawing, numbering, paper, correction and institutional routing coincide.

Possible opening B: a commissioned Buddhist manuscript whose paper, leaf count, crate, receipt, wrapper/title and later collation can all be reconstructed.

Possible opening C: a box/bundle list that mixes Buddhist books, drawings, statistics or scientific stores and is later repacked for international movement.

The opening should make the mechanism visible before introducing Hodgson biographically.

## Preferred ending

Return to the archive as a set of objects whose identities were repeatedly remade by copying, packaging, movement, sorting, rebinding and cataloguing. Hodgson is analytically useful not because he belongs neatly to one discipline, but because the survival of his materials lets us observe the mundane infrastructures through which nineteenth-century Himalayan knowledge became portable, comparable, credible and reputationally valuable.
