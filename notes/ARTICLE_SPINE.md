# Article spine - history of science version

## Provisional title

**Paper Empires: Brian Houghton Hodgson and the Material Infrastructures of Himalayan Natural History**

## One-sentence claim

Hodgson's authority as a colonial naturalist was produced through differentiated paper practices - copying, drawing, tabulation, annotation, watermark-bearing supports, office papers, and institutional recirculation - that converted heterogeneous Himalayan observations into portable scientific claims while preserving conflicts over authenticity, priority, and local knowledge.

## Provisional abstract

Brian Houghton Hodgson's Himalayan natural history is usually encountered through published taxonomies, metropolitan institutions, or the biographies of a colonial administrator who also supplied Buddhist manuscripts to European Orientalists. This article reconstructs a different object: the paper infrastructure through which Hodgson's knowledge was made, moved, revised, and archived. Taking the unpublished *Zoology of Nipal* as its principal case, and extending the analysis to geographical and survey materials, it follows drawings, lists, tables, manuscript copies, correspondence, annotations, and differentiated paper supports across Nepal, Calcutta, and British scientific institutions. Existing research notes suggest a material repertoire ranging from British machine-made wove papers, including Whatman and Towgood, to Nepali lokta, Calcutta papers, and administrative blue papers. Rather than treating these supports as neutral carriers, the article asks whether different papers marked different evidential statuses, institutional destinations, or claims to authenticity. It then turns to Hodgson's later annotations and self-archiving practices, showing how older documents became sites of scientific revision, priority management, and retrospective classification. The result recasts Hodgson's archive as an infrastructure of colonial science: a system in which natural objects became commensurable not simply through taxonomy, but through repeated material operations on paper.

## Evidence hierarchy

### Tier 1 - must carry the article

- ZSL / *Zoology of Nipal* manuscripts, drawings, notes, associated publication history.
- Material inspection of paper supports, watermarks, formats, hands, annotations, and copy states.
- Correspondence demonstrating movement of claims between Nepal/India and metropolitan institutions.

### Tier 2 - strengthens mechanism

- Linnean Society / Kew / RAS / Natural History Museum material where a concrete connection can be documented.
- RGS `rgs317723` route material.
- RGS `rgs810460` Waugh-Hodgson / Everest naming material, especially if it reveals priority, naming, copying, survey paperwork, or institutional adjudication.

### Tier 3 - controlled comparison only

- Buddhist manuscript and classificatory materials.
- Hodgson-Burnouf correspondence.
- Later Buddhist marginalia.

Tier 3 should enter only when it demonstrates that the same material repertoire crossed disciplinary boundaries.

## What the article should avoid

- treating “colonialism” as a sufficient explanation without showing the paper operation that made a claim possible;
- allowing Buddhist Studies historiography to determine the section order;
- using “paper technology” as a decorative metaphor without paper-level evidence;
- assuming that a watermark automatically maps to an epistemic function;
- making institutional affiliations causal unless correspondence, paper movement, publication, or archival handling demonstrates the mechanism;
- narrating Hodgson's late annotations chiefly as ideological conversion.

## Testable claims

1. **Paper differentiation claim:** materially different supports correlate with different genres, stages of work, institutional destinations, or evidential statuses.
2. **Conversion claim:** drawings, lists, tables, labels, descriptions, and fair copies functioned as conversion devices between local observation and metropolitan taxonomy.
3. **Recursive archive claim:** later annotations transformed earlier documents into layered instruments of revision and reputation management.
4. **Publication-failure claim:** the failure of *Zoology of Nipal* to stabilize as a book intensified rather than erased the importance of its manuscript infrastructure.
5. **Cross-domain technique claim:** some paper operations used in natural history also structured Hodgson's manuscript/Orientalist work, suggesting that disciplinary boundaries were less stable at the level of practice than at the level of later historiography.

## Preferred opening scene

Open inside the archive, not with Hodgson's biography. A sheet or sequence from *Zoology of Nipal* should make visible at least three operations at once - for example drawing, classificatory inscription, later correction, paper choice, copy-state difference, or routing to an institution. Only after the material problem is visible should the article introduce Hodgson.

## Preferred ending

Return to the archive's survival as an unfinished scientific object. The analytic payoff is that colonial scientific centres and peripheries were partly assembled through mundane material operations: who copied, on what paper, in what format, with which annotation, for which institutional reader. Hodgson becomes useful not because he fits a disciplinary identity, but because his archive preserves those operations unusually densely.
